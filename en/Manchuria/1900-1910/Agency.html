Manchuria: Agency (1900-1910)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

Catholic mission:
Bishop Pierre-Marie-François Lalouyer M.E.P. 藍祿葉 (1850-1923)

(Continued from previous decade)

Having promoted the division of the Manchuria VA into two sections, Lalouyer continued on as bishop of the northern region, centered in Jilin and including Heilongjiang. He was thus in Jilin (city) at the outbreak of the Boxer violence, which reached both Manchuria VAs in June of 1900. The next month he and two priests (the entirety of the mission) sought refuge at the Russian (not sure what it was, sources call it a “colony”) at Guanchengzi (Changchun) and from there followed the evacuation of civilians towards Russian territory and eventually on a circuitous water route through Harbin, Vladivostok, Japan and finally back to the Manchurian port of Yingkou and eventually by train back to Jilin, where the new church had indeed been destroyed.

In the aftermath and using indemnities paid by the Chinese government, Lalouyer began an aggressive period of construction in Jilin. In 1903, the town consecrated the Sacré-Coeur church in Jilin. Followed soon after by the foundation of Catholic villages Saint Joseph and l’Etoile de la mer near the newly founded town (remember that this is all a frontier region) of Tongken. He also enlarged the small seminary in Qiqihar.

Félix Choulet M.E.P. 1850-1923蘇

Born in Chambéry, France, in 1850. Joined the M.E.P. in 1877 and ordained as a priest in the order in 1880.

Choulet arrived in Niuzhuang via Shanghai in 1880 and worked for a few years at the Chaling seminary, where he also studied Chinese language. He spent three years in charge of a large district in Liaoning, bordering Jilin on the north and spanning Korea to Mongolia east to west. Following this, he was again placed in charge of a seminary and a church district, where he proved able an able administrator especially talented at finding funds for construction projects.

In 1900 Choulet was placed in charge of Niuzhuang and was in the process of rebuilding the church building when the Boxer violence broke out. Choulet learned piecemeal of the deaths of Guillon, nine priests and thousands of Chinese Catholics, as well as the looting of church buildings and residences, to the point that “there was no church, residence or school in the entire mission and most of the Christians have their houses burned or demolished.” (Biography: https://irfa.paris/en/missionnaire/1466-choulet-felix/)

It was in this condition that he was appointed VA of Southern Manchuria in 1901. He painted a bleak picture of the mission “We have a foothold in around ten stations. We still have, at the port of Newchanwang, two churches and an oratory: that is our entire fortune. Everywhere else, we are established on the domain of others. The Seminary occupies a rented house, and it is in houses belonging to pagans that our orphanages are installed.” (Biography: https://irfa.paris/en/missionnaire/1466-choulet-felix/) In 1901 he went to Beijing to discuss compensation (with the French legation) from the Chinese government, returning in 1902 with optimism for a new period of expansion and rebuilding. (The post-Boxer optimism was shared across missions). The Russo-Japanese War of 1904-5 was another period of uncertainty for the missions, but the Japanese victory left Choulet optimistic, as he was convinced that Japan was a better partner than Russia.

Protestant missions

Dugald Christie 1855-1936

Christie was born in Scotland in 1855. He received his qualifications as a doctor and surgeon in 1881. The following year he went to Mukden as a medical missionary and opened the Shengjing Clinic that among much else specialized in cataract surgery (which was mobilized as a metaphor for seeing the light of Christ). For the next 30 years Christie ran the clinic and worked towards opening a full medical school; with funds from Scottish and Irish presbyterian churches, the Chinese government and local people, the clinic became a (teaching) hospital and in March 1912 Christie became the first Principal of the Mukden Medical College. This was the first foreign medical college to be opened in northeastern China.

Christie retired in 1923 and died in Edinburgh in 1936.

Thomas C. Fulton, 1855-1942

Born in Northern Ireland and educated at Queen’s College, Belfast. Fulton arrived to Manchuria in 1884 and stationed at Yingkou. Typical of his generation, he spread his efforts over a wide area, never spending much time in one place, and instead concentrating on opening as many stations and churches as possible. In 1899, he joined the staff of Union Theological College, Mukden, and spent most of the rest of his missionary career in theological education. He retired in 1941 and died the following year.

John Ross

08/04/1842 ~ 08/04/1915

During the first decade of the 20th century, John Ross, approaching the conclusion of his missionary career, contributed to the consolidation of Protestant Christianity in Manchuria through his intellectual, theological, and institutional work. Having previously established churches, completed his Korean New Testament translation, and developed indigenous leadership programs, his activities between 1900 and 1910 concentrated on mission theology and institutional development in Northeast Asia.

Ross documented and interpreted the Manchurian Revival of 1907–1908 in his 1908 publication The Marvelous Story of the Revival in Manchuria. In this work, he characterized the revival as a validation of the indigenized church model he had promoted, rather than as an isolated emotional phenomenon1. The revival originated among Chinese Christians in Presbyterian and Methodist churches and demonstrated the organizational capacity and leadership development of native believers2. Ross continued his role as principal of the theological college he had established in Mukden in 1898. The institution expanded during this decade under his direction, offering coursework in biblical studies, systematic theology, and pastoral care. The college trained Chinese clergy who subsequently assumed evangelistic and leadership responsibilities throughout Manchuria3. His emphasis on theological education reflected his commitment to developing self-governing, self-propagating, and self-supporting local churches.

Ross participated in the inter-missionary Chinese Bible Commentary Committee, contributing Chinese-language commentaries on Job, Isaiah, Matthew, James, and other epistles4. These publications were distributed among local evangelists and preachers, serving as resources for doctrinal instruction and expository preaching. The availability of theological materials in Chinese supported his strategy of developing local Christian leadership.

Ross’s 1903 publication Mission Methods in Manchuria outlined his missiological approach. He advocated for qualified missionaries positioned strategically in urban centers, with emphasis on encouraging indigenous leadership and cultural adaptation of Christian practice5. This work influenced both his immediate mission field and aligned with the broader “three-self” principles being developed across East Asia.

In his later work, Ross addressed the relationship between Christianity and Chinese civilization. His 1909 book The Original Religion of China argued that ancient Chinese religion possessed monotheistic origins, positioning Chinese religious history within biblical theism rather than evolutionary theories of religion. This work represented his effort to establish connections between East Asian philosophical traditions and Christian theology, presenting Christianity as consistent with rather than foreign to China’s religious heritage6.

Ross formally retired in 1910 and returned to Scotland. His approaches to indigenous leadership development, vernacular Scripture, and contextualized theology continued to influence Protestant church growth in Manchuria in subsequent decades.

J. W. Hancher

In the aftermath of the Boxer Rebellion, which left mission stations across Manchuria damaged and demoralized, Dr. J. W. Hancher of the American Presbyterian Mission assumed responsibility for the medical and evangelical restoration of the Yingkou station. From 1900, he oversaw the reconstruction of the Yingkou Mission Hospital, one of the few Western medical facilities in southern Manchuria and a strategic center for humanitarian and religious activities.

The hospital faced significant challenges during the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905). With Yingkou positioned between competing imperial forces, the facility operated as neutral ground where wounded soldiers—Russian, Japanese, and Chinese—received treatment regardless of nationality. Presbyterian mission records indicate that Hancher’s commitment to neutrality secured the trust of Japanese administrators, who subsequently granted him greater freedom of movement than other missionaries. He utilized this access to distribute medical supplies and provide ministry to war-affected communities.

Hancher implemented an integrative approach to evangelism. He required patients to attend Christian chapel services before receiving treatment, linking spiritual outreach with medical care. While this practice of “medical evangelism” received criticism from some colleagues, it proved effective in generating local conversions. By 1910, Presbyterian reports documented over 500 baptisms attributed to the hospital ministry in Yingkou.7

Hancher’s activities extended beyond patient care to include training Chinese assistants in basic clinical procedures and Christian doctrine. He supervised the development of mobile clinics that served surrounding rural areas including Dashiqiao and Gaixian. These outreach clinics facilitated the reestablishment of Christian presence in interior regions disrupted by war and rebellion while providing essential public health interventions that were often the only medical services available.

In 1908, Hancher established a nursing school adjacent to the Yingkou hospital compound. Despite its modest scale, this institution became integrated into the emerging Presbyterian medical training networks in Manchuria and later collaborated with larger facilities such as Mukden Medical College. Following his appointment as senior American missionary in Yingkou after 1905, Hancher assumed diplomatic responsibilities within the mission community. During periods of heightened tension between Japanese colonial authorities and Chinese Christians, Hancher intervened to secure the release of arrested converts. Drawing on the credibility he had established with Japanese military officials during the war, he mediated on behalf of the church while avoiding official retaliation.8

Local residents knew him as “Huan Yisheng” (桓醫生), or “Dr. Hancher.” He provided treatment to poor patients without charge and frequently used personal funds to purchase medicine and food during public health crises.² His reputation extended beyond religious circles, with Chinese elders and foreign officials recognizing him as an exemplar of foreign benevolence. By 1910, Presbyterian records documented that the Yingkou station had become the fastest-growing in Manchuria, with over 3,000 members and 12 new churches established under Hancher’s leadership.

James S. McKee

1865 ~ 1942

Between 1900 and 1910, Rev. J. S. McKee, a missionary with the American Presbyterian Mission, established and expanded Protestant operations in Tiehling (present-day Tieling), southern Manchuria. The 1904 Reports of the Missionary and Benevolent Boards documents McKee’s presence at Tiehling by that year, where he was constructing mission infrastructure and directing evangelistic and educational activities9. Mission records indicate his assignment to Tiehling began by 1902, when the location was designated as a strategic station following the Boxer Rebellion due to its position along the South Manchurian Railway10.

McKee’s work encompassed evangelistic outreach to surrounding villages, conducted with Chinese evangelists. His reports document extended itinerant preaching journeys on foot and muleback to previously uncontacted areas. In 1903, McKee conducted extensive preaching circuits and observed increased receptivity among rural populations in the post-Boxer period. He supervised construction of Tiehling’s first Protestant chapel around 1905 and instituted regular Sunday worship services for a small congregation of Chinese Christian converts. The chapel functioned as both a religious center and temporary shelter during the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), when military operations disrupted missionary activities across Manchuria. McKee continued his pastoral responsibilities throughout this period, serving Chinese and foreign residents while coordinating limited relief operations in the district11.

In educational work, McKee established elementary instruction for boys and girls by 1904, utilizing rented facilities and local teachers trained in basic literacy and biblical studies. These initial educational efforts provided the foundation for more structured schooling under subsequent missionaries. Although Tiehling lacked a resident medical missionary, McKee maintained connections with Presbyterian medical stations in Mukden and Haicheng, facilitating patient referrals and coordinating visiting medical services to Tiehling between 1906 and 1908.

McKee’s most significant achievement was training indigenous church leadership. By 1909, he had prepared and commissioned local Chinese Christians as evangelists and lay leaders, establishing a self-sustaining church structure capable of independent operation.

Archbishop Innokenty Figurovsky Иннокентий Фигуровский

02/05/1863 ~ 06/28/1931

Between 1900 and 1910, Archbishop Innokenty (Figurovsky) directed the expansion and institutionalization of the Russian Orthodox Church in Manchuria, particularly in Harbin, where Russian imperial and religious interests intersected. Serving as head of the Russian Orthodox Mission in China since the late nineteenth century, Innokenty received elevation to archbishop in 1902 and subsequently shifted ecclesiastical authority toward the growing Russian community in Harbin after the Chinese Eastern Railway (CER) construction. His tenure represented a decisive shift in the Church’s role from a diplomatic presence in Beijing to a religious foundation for Russian settler communities in northeast China. Under his administration, Orthodox parishes, schools, and clerical institutions developed along the CER corridor, strengthening both religious practice and ethnic identity among the Russian diaspora in Harbin and surrounding areas. This work extended beyond pastoral care to constitute a civilizational project that integrated the Church with Russia’s imperial infrastructure and cultural expansion during a period of intensified geopolitical competition in Manchuria12.

Following the Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901), which had targeted Christian missions and resulted in the deaths of Orthodox clergy and laypeople in Beijing, Innokenty advocated for the canonization of the “Chinese Martyrs.” This martyrdom narrative became fundamental to the Orthodox Church’s ideological response to Chinese nationalism and anti-Christian violence, offering moral support to a community increasingly positioned in exile. Under Innokenty’s direction, the Church reframed its mission as spiritual resistance against secular forces and revolutionary ideologies perceived as threats to both faith and Russian civilization13. These theological and nationalist positions took institutional form through the liturgical practices and sermons of the Harbin cathedral and its associated seminaries, promoting a conception of Russian Orthodoxy as a defense against global apostasy.

This mission aligned with broader patterns of Russian messianism and revanchism developing within the Manchurian émigré community. The Russian Orthodox Church, particularly under leadership such as Archbishop Innokenty’s, served as a primary vehicle for what scholars have identified as “messianic revanchism”—a religious and political ideology that positioned the Russian diaspora as both carriers of a divine mission and victims of civilizational betrayal14. Scholars have observed that Innokenty’s ecclesiastical work and theological framework in Harbin connect to an established Russian messianic tradition, extending from Dostoevsky through the Slavophiles, which characterized Russia as a chosen nation destined to redeem humanity from Western modernity and atheism.

Korean Christian Leaders

Lee Hwa-chun

He was officially commissioned by the Southern Methodist Episcopal Church (남감리교회) in 1908 to conduct mission work among Korean settlers in Yongjeong (용정), Eastern Jiandao. Following his arrival, he collaborated with Dan Geum (단금), a Chinese lay Christian who supported Korean evangelization efforts, and obtained a site in Waryong-dong where he established the region’s first Methodist chapel. Within one year, his leadership resulted in the founding of nine local worship centers and the incorporation of more than 500 Korean settlers into the Methodist community. However, to avoid conflict with the Canadian Presbyterian Mission, the Southern Methodist Church transferred the Gando (간도) mission field to the Presbyterians through a formal agreement in 1909, leading to Lee’s departure from the region and the complete transfer of properties and congregants to Presbyterian oversight15.

Kim Jong-han

He represented an earlier phase of grassroots Korean Methodist evangelism. Beginning in the late 1890s and continuing into the early 1900s, Kim conducted ministry throughout West Jiandao (서간도), serving Korean migrants and establishing local fellowships without direct institutional support from foreign missionary organizations. While comprehensive records of Kim’s activities during this period remain limited, Methodist historical documentation identifies him as a significant itinerant leader whose work provided groundwork for the eventual resumption of Methodist missions in Manchuria after 1919.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

As previously, the two main organizations in Manchuria were the Catholic and Protestant missions. Of these the Catholic underwent some redistricting during this decade, while the latter welcomed new sources of foreign support and branched out into new social organizations.

Catholic missions:

Two events followed soon after the 1898 division of the Manchuria VA into north and south vicariates (Mudken and Jilin): the decimation of the Boxer violence and the period of growth in the immediate aftermath. The former included the violent death of Bishop Guillon, VA of Mudken, loss of life and property by thousands of Chinese Christians in both districts, and the destruction of nearly all Church property in Mudken and the as yet far smaller VA of Jilin. The latter was a period of rapid expansion, fueled by funds from the Boxer indemnity and by the disgrace of the anti-foreign faction at court, and the relative silencing of anti-foreign voices more generally across society. Both Catholic and Protestant missions expanded during this decade.

Population figures (See 1930-1939 file for entire chart) show a decline of about one quarter of the (baptized) Catholic population between 1899 and 1900.

Protestant missions:

Like the Catholics, the Protestant missions underwent a violent contraction, followed by a period of effusive growth. The latter was likely larger than the rebirth enjoyed by the Catholics, as it was fueled by the “Manchurian Revival” in the late part of the decade.

The Boxer violence was less pronounced for the Protestant missionaries, most of whom sought refuge in the foreign ports or overseas. The number of Christians killed is not given, most likely because the missionaries themselves did not know, especially considering the weak organizational structure of the Protestant Christian community. Nevertheless, there was significant loss of property, including Christie’s small hospital, which was completely destroyed.

Presbyterian Church of Ireland

Between 1900 and 1910, the Presbyterian Church of Ireland (PCI) expanded and consolidated its mission work in Southern Manchuria, establishing significant operations in Mukden (Shenyang), Hsinmintun, and Haicheng. This decade represented a period of institutional development that integrated evangelistic efforts with medical services, educational programs, and revival movements.

By 1901, the PCI maintained 6 ordained missionaries, 5 women missionaries, and 19 Chinese assistants operating from three main stations and 22 outstations, serving the regional Chinese Christian community16. The Mukden hospital, established in the 1880s, underwent expansion in 1903, accompanied by the opening of a new Women’s Hospital under Dr. Edith Buchanan’s direction, which focused on treating local women and training Chinese nurses17.

The revival of 1906–1907 constituted a significant development in this period, generating increased spiritual activity and conversions among Chinese Christians in PCI mission areas. This revival movement shared characteristics with the 1907 Pyongyang Revival in Korea, featuring public confession, repentance, and large prayer gatherings. Within PCI territories, hundreds of individuals joined the church during this two-year period. At Hsinmintun, regular worship attendance doubled, with over 300 baptisms recorded in 1907.

Chinese Bible women and lay evangelists played crucial roles in the revival movement. These women, trained by PCI missionaries, conducted house-to-house visits, led women’s prayer meetings, and provided basic literacy and biblical instruction. PCI statistics from 1908 documented 30 active Chinese Bible women under mission supervision, many operating independently in remote villages.

Educational activities also expanded during this period. By 1909, the PCI operated 14 boys’ day schools and 10 girls’ schools across its field, enrolling over 600 students. The curriculum encompassed Chinese classics, Christian doctrine, geography, and arithmetic. Additionally, a normal school for teacher training was established at Hsinmintun to prepare Chinese Christians for leadership roles in village schools and Sunday schools.

The PCI responded to social crises during this decade. During the 1902 famine, PCI missionaries distributed food and clothing to hundreds of affected families, often collaborating with Chinese Christian converts who served as relief coordinators. This combination of practical assistance and spiritual ministry enhanced community trust in the mission and reinforced perceptions of Christianity as a compassionate ethical system.

The mission operated within complex political circumstances during this period. Following the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), Japanese authority expanded in Manchuria. PCI missionaries maintained cautious but functional relationships with Japanese officials. In Mukden, they successfully negotiated to retain church property and received recognition from local Japanese administrators for their hospital’s impartial treatment of both Chinese civilians and Japanese soldiers.

By 1910, the PCI mission in Manchuria had developed into an established network with increasing indigenous leadership. Churches operated under trained Chinese pastors, and the mission promoted the Three-Self principles of self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating churches. The PCI reported a total baptized membership exceeding 1,500 by 1910, with regular church attendance considerably higher18.

American Presbyterian Mission (APM)

Between 1900 and 1910, the American Presbyterian Mission (APM) operated as a significant Protestant organization in Manchuria, conducting evangelical, educational, medical, and humanitarian activities within a region characterized by imperial competition, population movements, and emerging nationalist sentiment. The mission concentrated its efforts in Shenyang (Mukden), Fushun, Liaoyang, and Tieling, extending services to surrounding rural areas.

Following the Boxer Rebellion of 1900, which disrupted but did not devastate most American missions in Manchuria, the APM quickly resumed operations. By 1902, the organization had reestablished several outstations and restored evangelical and medical services. During the Russo-Japanese War in 1904, APM hospitals functioned as neutral medical facilities. Dr. J. W. Hancher at the Yingkou hospital treated both Japanese and Russian casualties, earning recognition from both parties for his neutral stance and medical service19.

American Presbyterian educational institutions maintained their operations throughout this period. In 1905, the mission administered multiple boys’ and girls’ schools, including boarding facilities in Mukden and Fushun, serving over 500 students. The educational program incorporated Biblical instruction, elementary science, and Chinese classical texts, with growing attention to preparing Chinese evangelists and educators20.

The 1907 revival movement, originating in Korea, influenced APM activities in Manchuria. This movement resulted in widespread conversions and public confession gatherings. American missionaries documented numerous individual confessions and expanded prayer meetings, with Chinese converts assuming leadership roles in local congregations and asserting greater involvement in church administration.

A notable development during this decade was the APM’s expansion of theological education. By 1909, the mission had established a formal training program for Chinese evangelists in Mukden, where students studied biblical interpretation, homiletics, and pastoral responsibilities. This initiative reflected a movement toward “Three-Self” principles—self-supporting, self-governing, and self-propagating churches—which gained further definition in subsequent years21.

The APM navigated relationships with political authorities, particularly with Japanese forces following their victory in the Russo-Japanese War. Missionaries adopted a cautious but generally cooperative approach, maintaining control of their properties while avoiding direct confrontation and emphasizing their spiritual rather than political objectives. In areas such as Tieling, where Presbyterian missionaries including Rev. J. S. McKee worked, they received informal support from local Japanese military officials who recognized their contributions to education and public health services.

By 1910, the American Presbyterian Mission maintained seven primary stations and numerous subsidiary locations, serving over 2,000 regular worship attendees and more than 1,000 baptized Chinese members. Beyond numerical growth, the mission contributed to the introduction of Western medical practices, contemporary educational methods, and a form of voluntary religious participation that generated both interest and opposition during this period of cultural transformation22.

Ecumenical Protestant Women’s Societies

Between 1900 and 1910, Ecumenical Protestant Women’s Societies facilitated Christian expansion in Manchuria through coordinated efforts in women’s evangelism, education, and medical care. These organizations functioned across denominational boundaries, uniting American, Canadian, and Irish Presbyterian women in collaborative training initiatives, revival activities, and spiritual mentorship of local converts.

Central to their approach was the “Bible woman” system, which involved training Chinese and Korean women in scriptural knowledge, prayer practices, and literacy skills to enable household evangelism and care for women and children. Mission documentation from 1909 records over 40 active Bible women working under ecumenical oversight in locations including Mukden, Fushun, and Antung23. These women additionally provided instruction in basic hygiene, midwifery, and reading, integrating practical assistance with religious instruction in culturally appropriate contexts.

The revival movement of 1906–1907 amplified women’s missionary activities. Alexander R. Mackenzie observed that numerous village revival gatherings originated and were maintained by Bible women and female missionaries through all-night prayer vigils, home visits, and group confession gatherings24. This revival pattern, rooted in domestic and community settings, sustained momentum beyond formal missionary-led events.

Medical work constituted another significant component. The Women’s Hospital in Mukden, directed by Dr. Edith Buchanan of the Irish Presbyterian Mission, treated over 1,000 female patients annually by 1910 while operating nurse training programs that prepared Chinese women in both modern medical practices and Christian instruction. Comparable initiatives developed in Liaoyang and Yingkou, frequently through inter-mission partnerships.

Women’s societies established institutional cooperation frameworks. By 1908, joint training programs emerged among American, Irish, and Canadian mission boards to standardize Bible curricula, distribute shared educational materials, and conduct inter-mission quarterly meetings for evangelism coordination. These arrangements created a cross-national Protestant network, particularly among unmarried women missionaries.

Mission records from 1910 documented 57 foreign women missionaries, more than 120 Chinese Bible women, dozens of girls’ schools, and multiple women’s hospitals and clinics operating throughout Manchuria25. This evidence demonstrates that women’s societies had evolved from supplementary roles to central positions in Protestant missionary expansion and the development of indigenous Christian communities.

Medical Missions of the American Presbyterian Mission and the Presbyterian Church in Ireland

Between 1900 and 1910, the medical missions of the American Presbyterian Mission and the Presbyterian Church in Ireland established significant healthcare infrastructure and evangelical networks throughout Manchuria. These missions, operating primarily in Mukden, Yingkou, and Changchun, addressed regional health needs while implementing a systematic approach to Christian outreach through medical practice.

The period following the Boxer Rebellion (1900) and during the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) presented both challenges and strategic opportunities for foreign missionary activity. Dr. J.W. Hancher of the American Presbyterian Mission reconstructed the Yingkou mission hospital after its destruction during anti-foreign violence. During the Russo-Japanese conflict, Hancher maintained the hospital’s neutral status, providing medical care to combatants from both sides as well as civilian casualties. This neutrality facilitated continued operations despite regional instability26.

In Mukden, Dr. Edith Buchanan directed the Irish Presbyterian Mission’s women’s hospital, which treated over 1,000 patients annually by 1910. Her practice concentrated on obstetric and gynecological care, serving women who had limited access to medical treatment under traditional Chinese medical systems or cultural restrictions regarding male physicians. Buchanan’s clinic also functioned as a training center for Chinese women who became known as “Bible nurses”—a role that characterized both Irish and American Presbyterian medical missions27.

These Bible nurses represented a practical integration of healthcare delivery and religious instruction. Selected from local Christian educational institutions, they received training in fundamental nursing skills, hygiene practices, and biblical teachings. Their combined function as medical assistants and religious educators enabled access to gender-segregated domestic spaces typically closed to foreign missionaries. Through providing care during childbirth, treating common illnesses, and instructing women in hygiene practices while incorporating scripture and prayer, they facilitated Christian influence within Manchurian households and communities that remained otherwise inaccessible to direct missionary contact.

While individual Bible nurses remain largely unnamed in historical records, their contributions proved significant during regional health crises. During the 1910 pneumonic plague outbreak, both American and Irish Presbyterian hospitals deployed Bible nurses to communicate quarantine procedures, assist with burial protocols, and conduct symptom surveillance. Their community-level interventions contributed to disease containment efforts and enhanced public confidence in mission-based medical services. Their coordination with Dr. Wu Lien-teh, the Chinese epidemiologist leading plague control efforts, demonstrated early forms of international medical cooperation. The work of Bible nurses also influenced local perceptions of Christianity. During a period when Christianity remained associated with Western imperialism in Chinese public opinion, the dedicated service of these women—often involving personal risk—contributed to more favorable views of Christian practice28. This service presented Christianity not solely as a foreign religious system but as a source of practical medical knowledge and social welfare.

The American and Irish Presbyterian medical missions established lasting institutional and cultural changes through their healthcare initiatives. They constructed hospital facilities, developed local medical training programs, provided medical care regardless of patients’ religious or national background, and positioned both Chinese and foreign women as central figures in religious and humanitarian work. The Bible nurses, despite their anonymity in most historical accounts, functioned as practical intermediaries between Christian doctrine and community service, with their influence extending beyond the duration of the institutions that trained them.

Korean Christian Organizations

Between 1900 and 1910, Korean Christian organizations in Manchuria’s Gando (Jiandao) region developed into significant centers of religious practice, education, and community cohesion among Korean migrants. As Japanese colonial pressure intensified in Korea, tens of thousands of Koreans crossed the Tumen River northward, establishing settlements in towns including Yenchi, Hunchun, Hoeryong, and Holung. Within this context of imperial oversight gaps and political uncertainty, Korean Christian communities experienced notable growth.

Korean migrants frequently established churches in Gando prior to the arrival of foreign missionaries. These locally initiated congregations subsequently aligned with the Canadian Presbyterian Mission, which formalized its regional presence through Robert Grierson’s activities in Longjing (Yongjeong) beginning in 1898. By 1904, the Canadian mission had organized Korean congregations into circuits, with Korean elders, deacons, and preachers assuming primary leadership responsibilities. Religious services occurred in adapted private residences, temporary chapels, or shared school buildings. Despite the absence of dedicated church structures, congregational activities remained active, encompassing Sunday worship, midweek Bible study sessions, and evangelistic outreach to surrounding villages conducted by Korean lay leaders and Bible distributors. By 1909, over thirty Korean Christian congregations operated within the Jiandao district29.

Education and religious activities were closely integrated in Gando. The Korean Christian community, with Canadian missionary support, established separate schools for boys and girls in locations such as Hoeryong, Hunchun, and Longjing. These institutions typically provided Bible instruction, Korean literacy education, and basic mathematics. A primary objective involved training literate Christians capable of reading Korean scripture, supporting church administration, and eventually assuming leadership positions. While the Canadian mission provided funding for many schools, Korean Christians managed operations and instruction. Numerous teachers had received training at Hamheung’s mission schools in Korea and brought both educational experience and nationalist perspectives to their work. Schools operated under challenging material circumstances—frequently utilizing single-room facilities with improvised furniture—yet maintained considerable symbolic significance. They provided Korean children with Korean-language instruction within Chinese territory, outside Japanese oversight.

Churches and schools operated as protective spaces and organizational centers. During periods of heightened Japanese surveillance, particularly following the Russo-Japanese War, churches frequently sheltered nationalist exiles. Congregational leaders such as Yi Dong-hui, who collaborated with the Canadian mission in 1909, utilized church platforms to deliver both religious and anti-Japanese nationalist messages. Yi’s sermons attracted large audiences and contributed to the politicization of Christian venues30. Additionally, Korean Christian women, often trained as Bible women by missionaries, assumed leadership roles in both religious and educational spheres. They provided instruction in schools, conducted women’s prayer meetings, and made household visits offering both spiritual and practical assistance31.

By the decade’s conclusion, Korean Christian leaders in Gando began advocating for increased ecclesiastical autonomy. They sought authority over local finances, pastoral appointments, and theological direction. While these demands would intensify during the 1920s, their origins lay in the established and deeply rooted church communities of 1900–1910. Korean Christians in Gando had developed substantial institutional infrastructure—schools, congregations, and evangelistic networks—that enabled the community not only to maintain itself in exile but to exercise considerable self-determination.

Russian Orthodox Church (ROC)

During the first decade of the twentieth century, the Russian Orthodox Church (ROC) in Manchuria functioned as both a religious institution and an instrument of imperial policy, particularly within the sphere of influence created by the Chinese Eastern Railway (CER). The period of the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) marked a significant expansion of the Church’s activities beyond traditional pastoral duties into areas of military support, humanitarian aid, and symbolic representation of Russian imperial presence.

The outbreak of war in 1904 brought ROC clergy into direct contact with military operations. Priests served as chaplains along the CER corridor and in strategic locations such as Harbin, where the cathedral became a focal point for memorial services honoring deceased soldiers and blessing ceremonies for departing military units. Orthodox liturgical practices—including collective burials and commemorative processions—provided religious legitimacy for the Russian military campaign. Clergy administered sacraments to wounded personnel, conducted battlefield funerals, and organized memorial services in military cemeteries that were frequently located near church grounds32. These religious observances served dual functions: addressing the spiritual needs of Russian Orthodox believers while reinforcing the moral framework of imperial expansion among military personnel and civilian settlers.

The Church’s humanitarian activities extended beyond military chaplaincy. ROC institutions, including monasteries and parish churches in Harbin and other railway settlements, provided material assistance to displaced populations, offering shelter, food, and medical care to Russian settlers, Chinese workers, and Korean refugees affected by the conflict. The visible participation of Orthodox clergy in relief operations enhanced the Church’s public standing and demonstrated its integration within the broader imperial administrative structure. Wartime sermons emphasized themes of suffering, divine trial, and national redemption—theological concepts that would later influence Russian émigré religious thought33.

The expansion of ROC influence during this period occurred under the direction of Archbishop Innokenty (Figurovsky), who assumed leadership of the Orthodox Mission in Beijing in 1903. This appointment extended ecclesiastical authority over Manchuria, with Innokenty identifying Harbin as a key center for Orthodox presence in East Asia. His tenure involved the systematic development of religious infrastructure throughout the CER zone, including church construction and the establishment of seminaries, while addressing the pastoral requirements of Russian railway employees, military personnel, and administrative officials.

Archbishop Innokenty’s responsibilities encompassed diplomatic as well as ecclesiastical functions. He maintained communication with Russian colonial authorities, Chinese officials, and, following 1905, Japanese administrators in contested territories such as Changchun and Manzhouli. His approach combined religious mission with political pragmatism, positioning the Orthodox Church as both a spiritual institution for Russian believers and a cultural influence within the multiethnic population of Manchuria. He supported educational initiatives aimed at promoting Russian cultural assimilation while authorizing the translation of liturgical materials into local languages, though maintaining doctrinal oversight34.

The Archbishop also contributed to the development of a particular theological interpretation of Russian Orthodox presence in Manchuria. In his public statements and sermons, he characterized Russia’s role in East Asia in terms of a civilizing mission, presenting Orthodoxy as a response to what he described as spiritual deficiency in the region. This narrative persisted even after Russia’s military defeat in 1905, with Innokenty emphasizing the long-term spiritual objectives of Orthodox expansion as distinct from immediate political outcomes.

The activities of the Russian Orthodox Church in Manchuria between 1900 and 1910, particularly during the Russo-Japanese War and under Archbishop Innokenty’s leadership, illustrate the intersection of religious institution and imperial policy. The Church’s expanded role during this period established patterns of operation that would influence Russian Orthodox communities in the region for decades to come.

Political allies:

The category of pro-Christian organizations can now include friendly officials within the government. Although missionary sources do not mention specifics, the universal hostility of the pre-Boxer period has shifted such that some local officials (notably in the anti-Christian stronghold of Hulan) are visibly friendly to the missions. Whereas in pre-Boxer sources, local officials are universally hostile, whether due to personal antipathy, fear that the Christians would serve to welcome a coming invasion, or weakness in the face of popular agitation, here they are shown to be more mixed. While there are still anti-foreign officials, including some who incite violence using former Boxers, there are also some like the magistrate of Hulan, who demonstrate support by welcoming the return of the missionaries in a public setting with the pomp of office.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The decade that started with the Boxers initiated a significant period of institutional growth as the Christian missions renewed their work with a renewed sense of purpose, a more friendly (or at least less overtly hostile) political setting, and increased resources coming from home and from the Boxer indemnity.

The immediate aftermath of the Boxers left both missions in a state of shock and disarray. By their own accounts, much of the work of previous decades was disrupted if not destroyed. This was particularly true of the physical infrastructure of schools, churches and dispensaries. The loss of life was significant, but generally regarded as a trial, possibly one sent by God, to test and strengthen the new church, and one in which any suffering would be amply rewarded in Heaven. (Both missions made frequent comparisons to the martyrs of the early Church)

The Catholic mission was physically destroyed. Physical destruction included the burning of the church and residences in Mukden and the smaller, much more recent start on a church in Jilin. It is not mentioned whether there was damage to other physical infrastructure such as the seminary in Chaling, which leads me to suspect that these assets were not harmed. The Protestant mission had a much less permanent physical presence, renting instead of owning property for schools, chapels and dispensaries, and the physical damage was less severe.

Both emerged from the period energized and flush with resources, and both embarked on a period of building. The Catholic missions rebuilt the destroyed church in Shenyang. The Protestant missions completed work on the Union Theological College (Boyd 64), which had been started in 1900, and on the medical school.

Revival Movement

Between 1900 and 1910, a significant Christian revival movement emerged in Manchuria, drawing direct influence from the 1907 Pyongyang Revival in Korea. This revival represented both a religious awakening and a period of social, ethical, and communal change across several Manchurian cities, notably Mukden (Shenyang), Liaoyang, and their surrounding villages.

The Manchurian revival originated from the spiritual developments in Korea. During the 1907 Pyongyang revival, Chinese pastors and theology students including Liu Quan-yue (劉全岳), Hu Man-cheng (湖萬成), and Zhang Si-cheng (張賜禎) traveled to observe the Korean movement. Despite language barriers, these visitors reported being moved by witnessing Korean worship and repentance practices, claiming to have received the Holy Spirit and returning to China with intentions to initiate revival efforts in their congregations.35

Their experience prompted what became known as the 1908 Manchurian Revival. This development marked a reversal of earlier gospel transmission patterns—whereas Christianity had spread from China to Korea in the 1870s, it now flowed back from Korea to Manchuria, indicating a reciprocal spiritual relationship between Korean and Chinese Christian communities.36

In February 1908, Rev. Jonathan Goforth of the Canadian Presbyterian Mission, having witnessed the Pyongyang revival firsthand, conducted special meetings in Liaoyang following an invitation from the Douglas family, missionaries with the Free Church of Scotland. Goforth’s descriptions of the Korean revival prompted strong responses from the Chinese congregation, including public confession of sins, emotional displays such as weeping and trembling, spontaneous prayer, and singing. Participants reported experiencing a tangible sense of divine presence during these gatherings.37

The meetings extended from morning to late evening. Attendees confessed previously undisclosed sins from years past. Women contributed gold jewelry, businessmen acknowledged dishonest practices, and students and preachers demonstrated emotional responses including weeping. Participants described experiencing what they perceived as divine influence. The revival proceeded without formal structure—lacking organized music or emotionally crafted sermons—instead focusing on calls for holiness and repentance.

Following the Liaoyang meetings, Goforth held similar gatherings in Mukden. Initial congregation response was subdued despite encouragement to pray, but subsequently widespread repentance occurred. One elder publicly admitted to stealing church funds years earlier, while another described attempting to poison his wife. Hundreds expressed distress and sought divine mercy. Prayer meetings extended to 2 a.m. as participants displayed intense emotional responses to their perceived spiritual condition.

Church members and missionaries submitted numerous prayer requests concerning estranged family members, colleagues, and those who had abandoned their faith. Simultaneous prayer—with hundreds praying aloud together—became a characteristic feature of the revival. One missionary noted, “Never in the history of the city has there been such a day as that Sabbath day in Mukden.”38

The revival extended to rural areas through the efforts of elders, Bible women, and lay preachers. Individuals offered their homes as prayer locations. Women’s meetings increased in both attendance and spiritual intensity. Youth groups organized evening prayers without external direction. Single confessions in village churches often initiated weeks of revival activities. In one documented case, an entire school began praying, weeping, and confessing when one student acknowledged a lie told years earlier.

Theologically, the revival emphasized the Holy Spirit’s sovereignty. Goforth and other leaders consciously avoided emotional manipulation or theatrical elements. They attributed the Holy Spirit’s work to spontaneous conviction beyond human direction. Missionary Walter Sloan observed that the revival “stirred Korea, wherever He has manifested Himself, as war, and commerce, and education… have not stirred it for centuries.”39

Three-Self Movement Foundations (proto-indigenization)

The decade between 1900 and 1910 witnessed the emergence of what would later be formalized as the Three-Self principles—self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation—within the Christian missions of Manchuria. These developments arose not from systematic planning but from the convergence of missionary policy adjustments, Chinese Christian initiatives, and the regional upheaval following the Boxer Uprising. While the Three-Self Patriotic Movement would not be officially established until the 1950s, this earlier period saw the formation of its fundamental concepts and practices.

The Boxer Rebellion of 1900 proved to be a decisive moment. The expulsion of missionaries, destruction of churches, and persecution of Chinese converts demonstrated the vulnerabilities inherent in foreign dependency. This crisis prompted mission boards and field leaders to reconsider their approaches and accelerated discussions about indigenization. The American Lutheran mission exemplified this shift, as Ose notes: “The policy shifted from a mission-centered approach to a church-centered approach,” with the wartime disruption creating conditions “conducive to the transition to national leadership in mission stations, schools, hospitals, and churches”40.

Presbyterian and Lutheran missions began developing frameworks that prioritized local leadership. The principle of self-governance gained practical expression as Chinese elders and preachers assumed greater responsibilities in congregational life. They conducted services, managed local church affairs, and participated in presbytery meetings. As Park observes, by the 1900s “some Chinese Christians were already serving as elders and had begun to participate in presbyterial leadership,” though missionary oversight remained significant41.

The push for self-support reflected growing recognition that financial dependency hindered long-term church sustainability. The Evangelical Lutheran Church of America (ELC) identified this as a critical issue, and mission policies increasingly encouraged congregations to assume financial responsibility for pastoral support and church operations. However, the economic realities of Manchuria presented substantial challenges. While some congregations achieved financial independence, many continued to require foreign subsidies.

Self-propagation emerged as perhaps the most successful aspect of early indigenization efforts. Chinese evangelists, Bible women, and lay teachers became central to Christian expansion throughout rural Manchuria. Presbyterian missionary reports from this period consistently noted the effectiveness of these local workers, who “often traveled farther and gained more access to the rural population than their foreign counterparts,” according to Park42.

The theological framework for these changes was influenced by broader missionary thinking, particularly discussions surrounding the Edinburgh 1910 World Missionary Conference. Some missionaries began to envision a genuinely contextualized Chinese Christianity, with a minority even supporting the incorporation of Confucian social ideals and local religious elements. However, many continued to view Chinese traditions as incompatible with Christian faith.

Several obstacles limited the full realization of these indigenization goals. Mission boards maintained control over finances, often citing concerns about mismanagement or doctrinal deviation. Church governance structures typically followed Western models, restricting contextual adaptation. Most significantly, missionaries remained reluctant to transfer complete authority to Chinese leadership, particularly in theological education and major decision-making processes.

Nevertheless, the experiences of this decade established important precedents. The recognition that a viable Chinese church required Chinese leadership, local financial support, and culturally appropriate evangelistic methods had gained acceptance, even if implementation remained incomplete. By 1910, Presbyterian and Lutheran missions in Manchuria had made measurable progress toward establishing self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating churches. These developments, emerging from missionary reflection, Chinese Christian agency, and political instability, provided the foundation for subsequent expressions of the Three-Self Movement. As Ose concluded, “All of the hypotheses were substantiated by the research”—the Three-Self principles, while not yet fully realized, had become operational realities by 191043.

Key Theological Issues

The main doctrinal issue encountered by both Catholics and Protestants was how to respond to the Boxer violence on the one hand, and on the other, to the period of opportunity that followed its suppression.

In a nutshell, the Catholic missionaries saw it as their role to stay with the Manchurian church, while the Protestant missionaries were more willing to leave China or to seek refuge in an armed port like Yingkou. While the actual actions of missionaries did differ from this exaggerated difference (for example, Lalouyer, long term VA of Manchuria, newly installed as VA of Jilin fled to Japan, while many Protestant workers were indeed killed at their posts), the difference between the two did reflect a profoundly dissimilar philosophy regarding the role of foreign missionaries in the Chinese church. The Catholics regarded the missionaries as shepherds, and aimed through the provision of sacraments and social services to build complete and essentially self-sufficient Catholic communities. This vision is reflected in the preference for Catholics to reside in close proximity, to intermarry, and to raise children in the faith. The role of the priest as a spiritual center and the church building as a physical center reflected this image of the Catholic communities as islands within a pagan sea.

In contrast, the Protestant missionaries always regarded themselves as a fleeting presence that would ignite the fire of faith and then leave the Chinese church to stand on its own. For this reason, and the fact that many missionaries arrived as couples or complete families they were more willing to flee when the need arose and view the Boxers as a test of faith for the Chinese believers to pass through on their own. Behind this belief is a further assumption about the nature of conversion. While the Catholic mission favored the model of raising children within stable Catholic communities, the Protestants idealized the model of Pauline conversion, a sudden, heartfelt transformation driven less by knowledge or training than by the direct calling of the Holy Spirit.

Without wanting to portray it as a matter of doctrine, the difference is still visible in the aftermath of the Boxers, as the two missionary communities compared notes concerning each other’s respective flock. Both Catholic and Protestant publications emphasized the steady faith of their own converts, while accusing the other of apostasy in the face of difficulty. Catholics were accused of promoting formulaic faith and relying on material benefits (especially help in lawsuits), while Protestants were accused of inflating their numbers by accepting anyone who expressed a fleeting desire to become Christian. For different reasons, each accused the other of seeking quick wins and promoting shallow faith.

Although the hostility between the two groups did die down somewhat, the difference in their mission methods and ideals remained evident in the period of expansion following the Boxer suppression. The Catholics used this time to rebuild institutions, including literally rebuilding destroyed church buildings. The Protestant world, both missionary and Chinese Christian, was swept up in a decade of revival.

Russian Orthodox Nationalist Revanchism

Russian Orthodox nationalist revanchism in Manchuria between 1900 and 1910 should be understood as an ecclesiastical development rather than an anti-Christian or heretical movement. This phenomenon emerged from the expanding and contested role of the Orthodox Church within the Russian diaspora. For Russian settlers and officials facing growing imperial instability and colonial tensions in Manchuria, the Orthodox Church provided both spiritual guidance and a foundation for cultural preservation and imperial identity. While this period did not produce a unified revanchist political ideology, it established the ecclesiastical foundations for later nationalist and fascist movements.

The Orthodox Church functioned as a central institution within Russian colonial administration, particularly in the Chinese Eastern Railway (CER) zone and urban centers such as Harbin. Religious leaders like Archimandrite Innokenty (Figurovsky), who later became bishop of Beijing, extended their missions far beyond traditional sacramental duties. They administered schools, published catechetical materials, maintained Russian liturgical traditions, and provided moral justification for the Russian presence in Manchuria as a sacred civilizing mission. This alignment between Church and state reflected the Synodal Church’s longstanding belief that spiritual mission and imperial expansion were complementary vocations. Consequently, ecclesiastical infrastructure—churches, icons, and religious schools—became instruments through which Russian authority inscribed itself on foreign territory, reinforcing both the physical and symbolic geography of Russian sovereignty in Asia44.

The 1900 Boxer Rebellion fundamentally transformed the Manchurian Church’s self-understanding. Anti-foreign militias killed over 200 Orthodox clergy and believers in Beijing, many of whom the Russian Orthodox Church later canonized as martyrs. Ecclesiastical authorities commemorated these deaths not merely as tragic losses but as theocratic testimony—evidence of Orthodoxy’s redemptive suffering amid political chaos and unbelief45. Martyrdom became central to the Church’s diaspora identity, connecting the Russian presence in Manchuria to a divine narrative of struggle, sanctity, and endurance. This collective memory, carefully maintained by Church authorities, fostered a broader sentiment of righteous grievance and sacred perseverance.

However, tensions within the Church’s institutional position were already emerging. Although clergy officially represented a pastoral and apolitical mission, their institutional presence increasingly intersected with nationalist ambitions and colonial resentment. Intellectuals such as Alexander Pozdneev, director of the Eastern Institute in Vladivostok, promoted cultural nationalism and imperial pedagogy, often appropriating Orthodox rhetoric while marginalizing episcopal priorities46. Simultaneously, Russian imperial officials—including military administrators of the CER—pressured local parishes and clergy to reinforce state loyalty, sometimes in ways that strained canonical norms and ecclesiastical independence.

These competing pressures created conflicting expectations for the clergy, who were required to sanctify both the suffering of the Russian diaspora and the continued assertion of Russian control while preserving liturgical and pastoral integrity. The Church’s role thus became increasingly ambiguous, functioning simultaneously as a site of spiritual continuity and an institution drawn into the ideological work of nationalism. This tension laid the groundwork for later ecclesiastical divisions, particularly after 1917 when politicized Orthodox groups such as the All-Russian Fascist Party in Harbin adopted revanchist rhetoric and selectively invoked Orthodox tradition to justify violent return narratives and anti-Soviet militancy—sometimes in direct conflict with Church leadership47.

The period from 1900 to 1910 should therefore be understood as a moment of ecclesiastical tension rather than theological deviation. The Russian Orthodox Church maintained doctrinal orthodoxy while its institutional functions gradually shifted in response to crisis: from spiritual guidance toward ethnic preservation, from sacramental life toward identity formation. In the Manchurian context, this evolution produced a diaspora Orthodoxy increasingly entangled with the politics of loss, sovereignty, and revenge.

Anti-Christian Leaders

Among the violent anti-Christian movements of this decade, it is difficult to find anything resembling a leadership structure. As a phenomenon, the Boxers themselves were famously leaderless, and the period of violence in Manchuria would seem to bear that image out. No sources give any indication that the movement was directed by any individual or that it had any sort of geographic center. From which instructions or strategy emanated. The only people who are identified are occasional county-level officials (“mandarins” in the sources) who had previously maintained cool relations with the missionaries, and used the outbreak of violence to “let fall the mask” (in the words of one French mission) and encourage the Boxers from behind the scenes. The rare instance of open hostility during the previous decade reached a violent peak during the few months of Boxer activity, notably in the county of Hulan where three Catholic missionaries were killed. That county would later emerge as a Catholic stronghold after that particular official was replaced.

Later and shorter-lived outbursts of localized violence would follow this same pattern, often at the instigation of a local official who was able to direct violence against Christians.

(Note that I use the term “Boxers” as a shorthand. It is clear that the actual violence did include the Boxers in their “classic” form, but also a variety of opportunists such as demobilized soldiers, who used the occasion to loot or extort Christian villages, or simply to settle existing scores. If anything, the “Boxer” violence was a precursor to the subsequent decades of “bandit” lawlessness that would engulf the region.)

There was some resentment from specific communities such as doctors, especially in the aftermath of the plague of 1910. This will be mentioned in the coming decade.

Anti-Christian Organizations

As discussed in question 3, most anti-Christian activity during this decade should be sourced to or channeled through the Boxers, including small bursts of violence that took place years after the fact. As such, the Boxers themselves are the umbrella for the period, but who are they? Mission sources emphasize the frequent two-way migration between southern Manchuria and Shandong/Zhili, meaning that anything happening on the North China Plain would quickly be brought north. Other religious movements were known to travel this route, such as the anti-Christian Zaili rebellion that erupted in Rehe in 1890.

Political support for the Boxers in Manchuria is difficult to pin down, in part because the Sino-Japanese War of 1894-5 had already weakened the chain of military and civil command across the region. Troops are shown acting in conjunction with the Boxers, or at least not opposing them, but I cannot say for certain if that represents orders or a breakdown of discipline. Later events are more clearly attributed to hostile local officials who took the opportunity to “remove the mask” of civility and openly attack the Christians.

The aftermath of the Boxers saw a general decrease in anti-Christian activity, if not sentiment, since small-scale events recurred over the remainder of the decade. Sources describe these as being the work either of former Boxers, abetted by hostile local officials, or by similar groups “secret societies and Boxers in all but name” who were motivated either by old grievances or the desire for plunder.

On the whole, I would characterize the shifting and inchoate anti-Christian sentiment of the period as more mood than movement, and certainly nothing that could be definitively tied to a movement or individual.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

Overwhelmingly the most important event of the period, or of the decades that preceded or followed, was the Boxer Uprising. This event reached Manchuria, in the summer of 1900 and was put down within a matter of months. That specific event was in a sense carried by settlers or sojourners from the Boxer heartland in Shandong and Hebei, the source of Manchuria’s largest number of migrants, but more fundamentally, it was just one especially violent iteration of themes that were already present in the Zaili rebellion ten years earlier, and that would continue to pop up in much more muted forms later.

Evidence from Manchuria confirms that the event was more complex than the story usually told. Missionary reports (as well as official sources) do not dwell on the mystical elements of Boxer lore and instead present it as fairly standard instance of collective violence that was armed and often involved participation of Qing troops. One recorded instance involved the burning of a coal mine by miners who had been sacked. In another, Boxers and Qing troops attacked a village using repeating rifles, while the village itself (mixed Christian and non) had only shotguns. In other words, this was not the mysticism-driven event that it perhaps started out as in Shandong. There was however clearly violence against Christians. Guillon was killed inside the church, which was burned and sacked. The French mission lost seven missionaries and numerous native clergy, and an unspecified number of native Christians (who were widely scattered in villages). The numbers are unclear because there was no chance to gather information between 1898 and 1903. However, in those two years the mission’s own count of Catholics is 25000 (for the undivided VA) and 17,161 (17,000 in south and 10,161 in north), respectively. I suspect that the rise in the number of Christians in these figures represents the beginning of the uptick in conversions in the aftermath, rather than an attempt to undercount casualties, which must have been high.

The aftermath of the Boxers saw the rapid decline of the anti-foreign faction at court, and as a ripple effect, through lower levels of officialdom and into ordinary society. Both Protestant and Catholic missions report a conspicuous warming of local attitudes towards the Christian presence, officials and police coming to call on missionaries and the like. (Notably in Hulan, which had been a stronghold of anti-missionary sentiment and site of the murder of three French missionaries) Yet within this generally warmer atmosphere, there were still unfriendly officials, including ones such as (Southern Manchuria 1904, 1906) who according to reports incited violence against local Christians by recruiting demobilized Boxers. These sorts of officially-encouraged attacks were however significantly less frequent than in the previous decade.

Also worth noting the Catholic-Protestant rivalry seems to have calmed down during this decade. At any rate the language (e.g., referring to each other as heretics) used in missionary publications is less inflammatory, and although there is still a sense that the other was derelict in the face of the Boxers, there is no further mention of lawsuits or coercive diplomatic measures taken against the other group. Both Catholic and Protestant now describe relations (especially personal relationships) as cordial, if still separate, but some backbiting did still occur in internal publications

Confucian Revivalism

Between 1900 and 1910, Confucian Revivalism emerged as a significant anti-Christian movement in China, particularly in regions such as Manchuria where foreign missionary activities had intensified. Rather than constituting a centralized campaign, this revivalism represented a loosely coordinated response across literati networks, gentry circles, and popular culture. The movement sought to reassert Confucian cultural authority while opposing Christianity, which participants viewed as both a spiritual challenge and an extension of foreign imperialism.

The movement drew heavily from earlier anti-Christian literature, most notably the Bixie jishi (A Record of Facts to Ward off Heterodoxy, 1861), which remained in circulation through the early twentieth century. This influential tract, widely attributed to the Hunanese scholar-official Zhou Han (周漢), documented alleged missionary misconduct—including claims of sexual impropriety, infanticide, and cannibalism—designed to provoke moral indignation among Chinese readers. Zhou Han’s contribution to anti-Christian agitation in central China and northeastern regions was acknowledged by both missionaries and Qing officials48.

Confucian Revivalists employed several interconnected strategies to mobilize opposition. They utilized demonological language, labeling missionaries as yang guizi (洋鬼子), or “foreign demons.” This terminology drew upon established Chinese folk religious concepts where gui (鬼) denoted dangerous spirits, particularly those unbound by kinship or ancestral obligations. The label resonated with cultural anxieties about outsiders as chaotic and spiritually contaminating forces. Klein observes that missionaries were perceived not merely as foreign intruders but as cosmologically deviant figures existing outside the moral framework of Confucian familial order49.

Anti-Christian propagandists also exploited Chinese homophonic wordplay in both textual and visual materials. The Catholic reference to God as “the Lord of Heaven” (Tianzhu, 天主) was mockingly reinterpreted as “the Heavenly Pig” (Tianzhu, 天豬), while Christians appeared in woodblock prints as hybrid pig-goat creatures. The character yang (洋, foreign) was punningly visualized as yang (羊, goat), resulting in depictions of missionaries as goats, pigs, or grotesque combinations thereof. This symbolic attack was reinforced through the circulation of the Jinzun Shengyu Bixie Quantu (Complete Picture Gallery Respecting the Sacred Edict to Ward off Heterodoxy), an illustrated companion to Bixie jishi that was widely distributed in the 1890s and continued to influence attitudes through 191050.

Rumor campaigns formed another central component of Confucian Revivalist activities. Pamphlets and oral narratives warned communities that missionaries harvested children’s eyes for medicinal purposes, exhumed corpses for ritual use, and employed supernatural powers to manipulate converts. These allegations generated widespread panic and provided moral justification for violent actions against Christian targets. The apparent origin of many such rumors in oral tradition rather than elite literature suggests the movement’s broad social foundation51.

Although anti-Christian sentiment reached its violent peak during the Boxer Rebellion (1898–1901), Confucian Revivalism persisted after its suppression. Between 1900 and 1910, smaller-scale attacks on churches and Chinese Christians continued, particularly in interior and northeastern provinces experiencing missionary expansion. Local gentry and scholar-officials maintained the circulation of anti-Christian materials, often framing their arguments through references to the Qing dynasty’s Sacred Edict (Shengyu, 聖諭), which emphasized the suppression of heterodox teachings and the preservation of Confucian orthodoxy.

Contemporary missionary accounts confirm both the persistence and sophistication of anti-Christian opposition during this period. In his 1901 pamphlet Shall the Missionary Go?, J.C. Garritt of the American Presbyterian Mission characterized Chinese resentment toward missionaries as extending beyond religious concerns to encompass racial, national, and civilizational dimensions. He observed that Chinese communities associated missionaries with Western imperial expansion and interpreted their widespread presence—including in inland provinces—as evidence of a coordinated effort to undermine Chinese sovereignty. Garritt noted how missionaries were often conflated with merchants and military personnel and accused of participating in broader conspiracies to transform China from within52.

The Confucian Revivalist movement of 1900-1910 thus occupied a significant transitional position in Chinese anti-Christian discourse. While its methods—including demonization, rumor propagation, and symbolic attack—derived from established traditions of folk belief and literati conservatism, its political impact contributed directly to emerging anti-imperialist and nationalist mobilizations. By the 1920s, similar rhetorical strategies would be adopted by secular nationalist and Marxist intellectuals, who reframed Christianity not in spiritual terms but as an instrument of Western cultural imperialism.
