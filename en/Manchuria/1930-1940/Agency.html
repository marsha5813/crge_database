Manchuria: Agency (1930-1940)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

Catholic

Bishop Auguste-Ernest-Désiré-Marie Gaspais, M.E.P.

Having been installed as VA of Jilin in 1923, Gaspais was at the center of the various crises of the 1930s, including the Japanese occupation of Manchuria, the formation of Manchukuo (capital at nearby Changchun, renamed Xinjing), and the subsequent political crises involving the place of Catholicism in the new, highly engineered state.

In 1934 Gaspais founded a new seminary in the Manchukuo capital of Xinjing. In 1940, this seminary was handed over to the Assumptionist order for operation. The seminary opened with an enrolment of about 50 students.

On the whole, Gaspais’ attitude towards Japan was cautious. He was by no means a booster for the new state, but was willing to deal with its authorities as the chief representative of the Church (as in a 1934 meeting of Manchukuo religious leaders in Changchun). He is best remembered for his role in advocating for the acceptance of political and familial rites in the lives of Catholics, convincing the Holy See to reverse its centuries-old decision to ban Chinese Catholics from participating in ancestral or Confucian rites (and the definition of the latter as idolatry). This advocacy was in response to increasing role of public rites in the life of Manchukuo, including a legal instruction to maintain a Shinto shrine in classrooms, rites for the Manchukuo and Japanese emperors, and rites for the war dead. It mirrored crises in Japan, specifically one involving students at Catholic Sophia University in Tokyo

Jean-Marie-Michel Blois, M.E.P.

Blois continued to act in his role as VA of Mukden. He does appear to have been less enthusiastic than Gaspais about cooperating with the new state, but that may simply be a virtue of distance from the new capital. There is no particular evidence either way about his support or condemnation of Japan. His notable achievements during this period consist of 1934 founding a printing house in Mukden to print materials for the Church as it existed under the state of Manchukuo (printers in Hong Kong or China were either unable or unwilling to take on this work).

Protestant

Charles A. Leonard

Charles A. Leonard, a Southern Baptist missionary stationed in Manchuria during the 1930s, played a notable role in defending religious freedom against the increasingly restrictive policies of the Japanese-backed Manchukuo regime. Based in Harbin, Leonard focused his ministry on serving Russian exile communities who had fled the turmoil of the Russian Revolution and civil war. These already marginalized populations faced additional vulnerabilities as Japanese control intensified and new constraints on foreign religious activities were implemented. The Manchukuo government's efforts to consolidate power included bringing all religious activity under state oversight through mandatory registration, surveillance, and in some instances, compulsory Christian participation in State Shinto ceremonies. These measures posed direct challenges to missionary autonomy and fundamental principles of religious liberty. Leonard emerged as one of the most outspoken critics of these policies, understanding their implications for foreign missionaries, local Christian communities, and broader religious freedom.

In 1938, as pressure on religious groups escalated, Leonard organized a gathering of North American missionaries operating in Manchuria. This initiative brought together representatives from various denominations, fostering solidarity within an increasingly isolated community. Under Leonard's coordination, the assembled missionaries drafted a formal protest document articulating their objections to the religious control measures. This document presented a reasoned appeal grounded in international norms and religious liberty principles rather than simple dissent. Leonard extended his advocacy beyond internal protest by compiling a detailed 15-page report on the situation, which he submitted to both the Manchukuo Ministry of Civil Affairs and his North American mission board. This dual approach ensured that the circumstances facing religious communities in Manchuria were documented and communicated to local authorities and the international community. His work contributed to creating a record that could inform public opinion and potentially generate diplomatic pressure on Japanese and Manchukuo authorities1. Leonard's actions had several significant outcomes. He helped defend religious freedom during a period of severe restriction, maintaining resistance among missionaries and local Christians. His efforts to unify the missionary community enabled more effective collective action and mutual support. Additionally, his documentation and advocacy prevented abuses from going unrecorded, creating an important historical record for future accountability and the ongoing struggle for religious liberty in the region.

Although many missionaries were eventually expelled or interned during World War II, Leonard's leadership demonstrated the missionary community's resilience and dedication in Manchuria. While his efforts could not halt the progression of religious repression, they helped preserve principles of religious freedom and established a model of principled resistance against authoritarian control.

John Stewart

They elected to withdraw from general educational activities, selling some school buildings to the government and facilitating the departure of teachers from the country. The Moukden Medical College, however, continued its operations under a carefully negotiated compromise that permitted minimal Christian activity, a concession that reflected the Japanese authorities' recognition of the superior value of medical services over educational work.

Stewart's leadership throughout this turbulent period exemplified a steadfast commitment to Christian witness coupled with an unwillingness to capitulate to pressures that would undermine the mission's fundamental principles. His approach balanced diplomatic engagement with principled resistance, seeking negotiated solutions when possible while maintaining non-negotiable positions when necessary. Through these efforts, he succeeded in preserving the mission's presence and influence in Manchuria for as long as circumstances permitted, even as political and ideological pressures continued to intensify.

The outbreak of war between Japan and Britain in December 1941 brought this chapter to an abrupt conclusion, as Stewart and the remaining Scottish missionaries were interned and subsequently repatriated. Following the war's end, Stewart returned to Manchuria as part of an assessment commission charged with evaluating the feasibility of resuming missionary operations. Initially, he expressed cautious optimism about the possibility of working under the new Communist authorities, but this hope quickly dissipated as he witnessed the regime's growing hostility toward foreign missions. His final years in Manchuria were devoted to managing the inevitable withdrawal process, as the Communist government systematically restricted missionary activities and ultimately expelled all foreign religious workers2.

Stewart's tenure in Manchuria represents a remarkable example of missionary leadership during an era of profound political transformation. His legacy is characterized by resilience in the face of adversity, adaptability to changing circumstances, and an unwavering commitment to Christian faith within a challenging and often hostile environment. Through his principled stance on religious freedom and his strategic navigation of complex political pressures, Stewart demonstrated how foreign missionaries could maintain their spiritual mission while respecting the legitimate concerns of nationalist movements and changing political authorities.

John Sung 宋尚節

09/27/1901 ~ 08/18/1944

In the autumn of 1931, as Manchuria teetered on the edge of crisis with Japanese aggression imminent, John Sung entered the region as part of the Bethel Worldwide Evangelistic Band. The Band, led by Andrew Gih and including Frank Ling, Lincoln Nieh, and Philip Lee, represented a pioneering indigenous evangelistic movement in China. John Sung, already established as a renowned preacher, had been formally invited to join the team following a summer conference, though it was understood that his dynamic presence would likely overshadow the original leader's prominence3.

The Bethel Band's evangelistic approach centered on four fundamental elements: fervent prayer meetings, the formation of evangelistic teams, Bible classes for discipleship, and the establishment of family worship. These methods, refined through Sung's earlier experiences in other Chinese provinces, were intentionally designed to cultivate a robust and self-sustaining indigenous church. Their strategic arrival in Manchuria occurred during a period of acute social and political upheaval, positioning them at a critical juncture in the region's history.

The Band's first major engagement took place at a Lutheran mission conference in Dairen, where Sung's powerful testimony and the group's passionate style initially encountered resistance from some missionaries who objected to the emotional displays and vocal prayers. Rather than moderating their approach, the Band chose to depart, remaining undeterred by the opposition they faced. This decision reflected their commitment to their distinctive evangelistic style, even when it challenged conventional missionary sensibilities.

From Dairen, the Band traveled to Mukden (Shenyang), arriving just before the infamous Mukden Incident—the Japanese military's seizure of the city that marked the beginning of Manchuria's occupation. Within this context of mounting turmoil, Sung and the Bethel Band catalyzed an extraordinary revival. What commenced with modest attendance rapidly expanded as the church filled by early morning, and throughout twenty-seven meetings, more than 1,000 people publicly confessed their sins and professed faith in Christ. The revival's momentum sustained itself across their campaign throughout Manchuria's "Three North-eastern Provinces," ultimately resulting in over 3,000 professions of conversion despite the region being engulfed in warfare and uncertainty4.

Sung's preaching style distinguished itself through its intensity and theatrical elements. He frequently exhausted multiple interpreters during single meetings, demanding they physically enact his vivid illustrations. His sermons consistently emphasized three core themes: confession of sin, the necessity of the Holy Spirit's power, and public witness for Christ. The meetings themselves were characterized by deep prayer, emotional repentance, and an urgent atmosphere heightened by the surrounding political chaos5.

The impact of Sung's ministry in Manchuria manifested across multiple dimensions. The mass conversions and revival represented the most significant spiritual awakening since Jonathan Goforth's earlier ministry in the region, with thousands responding to Sung's calls for repentance and local churches experiencing unprecedented growth. Simultaneously, new converts were systematically organized into evangelistic bands and Bible study groups, creating institutional structures that would maintain the revival's momentum beyond the Band's departure6.

The ministry's emphasis on local discipleship and leadership development proved particularly significant, as it reduced dependence on foreign missionaries while establishing foundations for a resilient, indigenous church. This approach proved prescient given the political upheavals that would follow. Perhaps most importantly, the revival provided spiritual comfort and purpose to Manchurian Christians during a period of fear and instability, spiritually preparing them for the challenges of Japanese occupation and, subsequently, Communist rule.

John Sung's legacy in Manchuria endures as a testament to spiritual renewal flourishing amid adversity. His ministry not only brought thousands to faith but fundamentally transformed both the structure and spirit of Christianity in the region. Despite—or perhaps because of—the context of war and uncertainty, the "sluice gates" of spiritual renewal opened, leaving an indelible mark on the landscape of Chinese Christianity.

Korean Christian Leaders

Han Kyung-hee 한경희 韓敬禧

1881 ~ 1935

During the 1930s, Han Kyung-hee (한경희, 1881–1935) assumed a pivotal role in shaping Korean Christianity and nationalist resistance in Manchuria. His work throughout this decade—encompassing church leadership, education, and community organization—exemplified the convergence of Christian evangelism and anti-colonial activism within the Korean diaspora.

Han's appointment as president of the Southern Manchuria Presbytery (남만노회장) in 1930 positioned him at the apex of the Korean Presbyterian hierarchy in the region. This role allowed him to coordinate church expansion while protecting congregations from mounting Japanese imperial interference. Han articulated a theological stance that rejected accommodation with State Shinto, grounding his ministry in biblical monotheism and Christian ethical opposition to colonial pressures. As Japanese authority in Manchuria intensified, he instructed Korean pastors that "worship belongs to God alone," leading his Presbytery to adopt a measured yet resolute opposition to mandatory Shinto shrine observances.

Education constituted a cornerstone of Han's Christian mission. In 1932, he established Dongmyeong School in Samwonpo, Yuha-hyeon, designed to cultivate Korean Christian leaders possessing both spiritual depth and national consciousness. The school's curriculum integrated biblical studies with Korean history, culture, and civic education. Han viewed instruction in the Korean language and Christian principles as essential to cultural preservation, maintaining that true salvation must speak in our mother tongue.

Recognizing the necessity for broader educational infrastructure, Han participated in founding the Southern Manchurian Korean Education Association in 1933. This organization united Korean church leaders, elders, and community figures to develop a comprehensive educational framework. They planned secondary institutions for students excluded from Japanese and Chinese educational systems, culminating in the establishment of Samsung Middle School in 1936—a project Han initiated that was completed after his death. The school emerged as a center of academic excellence and cultural resistance, offering instruction in Korean literature, ethics, theology, and nationalist thought.

Han additionally served as an advocate for Koreans navigating the complexities of colonial migration. As Manchukuo authorities pressured Korean settlers to adopt Japanese identity or Chinese citizenship, Han worked to secure their legal standing and protect their rights. He mediated land disputes, educational access issues, and citizenship conflicts, utilizing his religious authority to defend the dignity and autonomy of the Korean diaspora.

Although Han died in 1935, his influence extended far beyond his lifetime. The institutions he established and the leaders he mentored sustained the moral and cultural resistance of Korean Christians throughout the remaining years of Japanese occupation. His conception of a church that cultivated both faith and freedom profoundly shaped the theological outlook and political consciousness of the Manchurian Korean Christian community.7

Korean Women Christian Leaders

During the 1930s, as Japanese imperial control tightened across Manchuria, Korean Methodist women carved out spaces for ministry within the displaced Korean communities. Operating under the Korean Methodist Church's Manchurian Mission Conference 조선감리회 만주선교연회, these women navigated both colonial surveillance and the gendered limitations of their own church hierarchy. Their work formed the backbone of grassroots Christianity in Manchuria, where ministry often occurred in informal settings far removed from established church buildings.

Yi Bae-se 이배세 emerged as one of the most influential figures in this movement, serving primarily in Harbin and the surrounding North Manchurian region. She established small schools that functioned as both literacy centers and worship spaces. In these dual-purpose facilities, Yi taught Korean writing alongside biblical instruction, often working with women who had never previously learned to read or write. Her ministry extended beyond formal education to include pastoral care: she visited women in labor camps, offered prayer and comfort to mothers fearing the conscription of their sons, and conducted baptisms in clandestine ceremonies held in barns and other makeshift spaces. These schoolrooms served as sanctuaries where Korean language and Christian scripture were preserved against mounting pressures toward assimilation. Despite constant surveillance by Japanese authorities, she maintained her work and consistently encouraged what she called "courage through the Word."8

Yi Maeryeon 이매련 operated across a broader geographic area, extending her ministry from the Tumen River region into the Jilin countryside. Her work encompassed both teaching and healthcare, as she served as both educator and midwife. Yi delivered children, treated mothers with herbal remedies combined with prayer, and provided instruction on hygiene and health practices—often in homes lacking basic amenities. She organized sewing circles and cooking classes that provided cover for theological discussions, creating spaces where women could articulate both spiritual and cultural yearnings. When confronted by colonial police, she refused to sign loyalty oaths and avoided imprisonment only through the intervention of a sympathetic local official. Among the women she served, she became known as a "mother of exiles"—unmarried, constantly traveling, and always carrying gospel literature and rice for those in need.

In East Manchuria, Nam Kyung-sun 남경순 developed particularly effective educational ministries in the Hunchun and Yanbian regions. She established informal primary schools for Korean children and evening literacy classes for women who worked during daylight hours. Using domestic education as a framework, she taught Korean history and biblical narratives while introducing hymns that reinforced both Christian faith and cultural memory. When Japanese authorities prohibited Korean-language worship in certain areas, Nam organized rotating house churches, moving services weekly between different homes and training lay women to lead prayers, read scripture, and provide mutual care during periods of persecution. Her mission combined evangelism with cultural preservation, creating networks of connection among the displaced through shared worship and song.

Yi Il-shim 이일심 focused her ministry on isolated Korean-Chinese widows and their children in the Longjing and Helong areas, providing spiritual guidance and basic education. She conducted home-based worship services that encouraged women to read aloud, memorize Psalms, and share personal testimonies of faith. Her work emphasized intergenerational transmission, ensuring that second-generation children born in Manchuria could maintain Korean language skills, learn Korean hymns, and preserve connections to their ancestral homeland as part of their Christian identity. She regularly walked considerable distances across difficult terrain to reach scattered households, often arriving with only essential supplies: a blanket, a Korean New Testament, and prepared food to share.

These women lacked formal ordination and did not hold official ecclesiastical positions. However, through their daily work, they preserved, transmitted, and embodied Christian faith within the most vulnerable segments of the Korean diaspora. They conducted baptisms, provided education, offered pastoral care, and shared in community mourning. Their resistance took quiet forms—expressed through scripture, song, and steadfast service. Their witness demonstrates that Christian mission in Manchuria extended beyond male clerical leadership to encompass deeply communal work centered on survival and hope.

The Presbyterian Church in Manchuria during this same period, while notable for male leaders such as Han Kyung-hee and Kim Jin-geun, had fewer formally recognized female evangelists. Nevertheless, available evidence indicates that Presbyterian women fulfilled vital though unofficial roles as teachers, Bible study leaders, and coordinators of women's fellowship groups (geunyeokhoe). Although these contributions rarely appear in official synod records, these women sustained community worship through practical service—preparing meals for traveling preachers, teaching psalms to children, and organizing prayer gatherings in rural villages. While they may not have held missionary titles, they provided essential support for Presbyterian community life in exile.9

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Catholic

In 1932, the parish of St. François-Xavier was entrusted to Father Patrouilleau, a missionary from Tokyo. This church was located to the Japanese concession and in the consular district in Mukden and was used by Japanese and Korean Catholics, and Catholic foreigners.

Under the Manchukuo regime, the Manchurian Catholic church was forced to sever relations with the Chinese church and realign themselves to communicate with Catholic authorities in Tokyo instead of those in Beijing. While this did not likely influence personal communication amongst to leaders, it did complicate routine visits and force measures such as the founding of a new Church printing house in 1934, since the church could no longer source its printed materials from Hong Kong or China.

Protestant

The Protestant churches were forced by Manchukuo authorities to divest any affiliation with the Chinese Church, i.e., the Synod in Shanghai. At the same time, they did remain in communication with and received advice from home boards. The question of how, and to what degree to acquiesce to Japanese demands, was up to some debate, but on the whole, the decision was made to do as little as possible, while hoping to Weather the storm. Reversing the long-standing policy of seeking to distance themselves from extraterritoriality, it was considered the late 1930s whether to incorporate the missions as a “juridical person,” as per the Japanese demand. Doing so would have allowed the schools to continue to operate, but at the cost of acquiescing to demands for Japanese ideological indoctrination, such as Shinto shrine participation. In 1938, the decision was made simply to leave the educational mission all together. The schools were sold to the Manchukuo government for the sum of 641 thousand Manchurian dollars, which was considered to be a fair price. By 1940, the atmosphere had become untenable, and missionaries were advised to repatriate.

As reported to Synod in 1939, the state of the Protestant church was as follows:

Churches: 288 congregations, membership about 30,000, 100 ministers, 250 evangelists.

Schools: before official interference with the school system, there were 30 schools, 200 teachers, and over 5000 pupils.

Hospitals: 14 hospitals, 250 nurses, 50 doctors, including those on the staff of the medical college.

Church of Scotland and United Free Church Missions
In the early 1930s, the Church of Scotland and its predecessor, the United Free Church, maintained a robust missionary presence in Manchuria anchored by institutions such as the Moukden Medical College and various theological schools. These organizations were widely respected for their humanitarian work, providing both medical care and education to the local population. Their neutrality and dedication to service had fostered goodwill among successive regimes, including Chinese warlords and the early Japanese authorities after the occupation of Manchuria in 1931. After the Japanese takeover, the mission schools experienced a surge in enrollment as many public institutions closed, and the Japanese initially allowed the missionaries a relatively free hand to continue their work.

The political climate soon darkened, however. By 1935, Japanese police began arresting Chinese Christians, including professors at the Medical College, often on suspicion of anti-regime activities. Some were imprisoned and tortured, leading to a marked decline in church attendance and interest in Christianity. The Japanese authorities also imposed new requirements on mission schools, most notably the demand that students and teachers participate in acts of obeisance at Confucian shrines as a gesture of loyalty and national unity.

The missionaries, led by figures such as Rev. John Stewart, found themselves in a moral quandary. While they were willing to express loyalty to the state in ways compatible with their faith, they could not in conscience participate in what they viewed as religious ceremonies at the shrines, which they believed compromised their Christian witness. Negotiations ensued, and in 1936 the mission issued a formal statement declaring their willingness to hold commemoration services in their schools at times appointed by the government, but refusing to attend or lead students to shrine ceremonies. Despite further discussions, including a trip by Rev. A. S. Kydd to Moukden in 1937 to confer with Japanese officials, the authorities remained unmoved.

By 1939, it was clear that schools refusing full compliance would be closed. In January 1940, facing no alternative, the mission leadership decided to withdraw from general education, selling some school buildings to the government and allowing teachers to leave the country. The Moukden Medical College, however, was allowed to continue under a compromise arrangement that permitted minimal Christian activity, reflecting the Japanese authorities' recognition of its medical value. The College's secretary, who was Japanese, attended shrine ceremonies on its behalf, and the event was added to the College calendar. These concessions were distressing to some, but most missionaries accepted them as necessary to maintain their essential Christian witness and service.

Throughout the decade, the missions' evangelistic work faced increasing restrictions. Japanese officials grew suspicious of any activity that might set God above the state, and new centers of Christian activity were effectively barred. Public meetings were closely monitored, and detectives often attended services, listening for any word construed as contrary to state doctrine. By 1941, the situation had deteriorated to the point where about half of the missionaries had been withdrawn from Manchuria, and it was clear that the Japanese authorities wished to see them all leave10.

Despite these challenges, the Church of Scotland and United Free Church Missions left a lasting legacy. Their institutions, especially the Moukden Medical College, provided essential services to the local population and earned deep respect for their professionalism and humanitarianism. Their approach was marked by a commitment to political neutrality, respect for local authority, and a willingness to adapt to changing circumstances without compromising core Christian principles. When faced with demands that conflicted with their faith, they withdrew from certain spheres of activity but continued their work where possible, demonstrating resilience and steadfastness in the face of adversity.

Korean Manchurian Methodist Mission Conference

The unification of the Southern and Northern Methodist churches in 1930 led to the formation of the Korean Methodist Church and the establishment of the Manchurian Methodist Mission Conference 조선감리회 만주선교연회. This ecclesiastical structure served as the primary organizational framework for Korean Methodists in Manchuria, coordinating pastoral appointments, expanding Christian education, strengthening rural missions, and fostering communal solidarity during a period of increasing colonial pressure. The 1930s were characterized primarily by institutional development, grassroots evangelism, and adaptive strategies for survival, preceding the later theological and political crisis surrounding Shinto shrine worship.

Formally organized in 1930, the Manchurian Conference consolidated former Southern and Northern Methodist circuits throughout North, East, and Central Manchuria, encompassing regions including Harbin, Tumen, Longjing, and Yanbian. The Conference supervised the appointment of ordained pastors, commissioned women evangelists, and coordinated activities among lay leaders and youth organizations. Annual conferences brought together clergy and lay delegates to review mission work, approve school reports, and administer relief funds. This administrative structure enabled the Korean Methodist presence in Manchuria to maintain remarkable organizational coherence despite geographic dispersion and government surveillance.11

The Conference's rural church and village improvement movement represented one of its most significant initiatives. Recognizing the economic hardships and cultural displacement faced by Korean immigrant communities, the Conference promoted a comprehensive approach to mission work that extended beyond preaching to encompass rural welfare. Churches in agricultural villages undertook projects such as well construction, establishment of communal granaries, operation of childcare centers, and instruction in modern farming techniques. These Conference-guided initiatives sought to address both material and spiritual needs of the Korean population—an approach one missionary characterized as "the gospel with a hoe and a hymnal."12

In education, the Manchurian Conference played a crucial role in establishing church schools and theological seminaries throughout the region. These institutions provided biblical training alongside Korean language education, ethics, and national history. In cities such as Tumen and Harbin, Christian schools offered Korean children opportunities to learn their native language during a period when Japanese or Chinese public schools either prohibited Korean instruction or enforced pro-imperial curricula. The seminaries prepared Korean-speaking pastors, ensuring a continuous supply of indigenous leadership capable of maintaining both theological integrity and cultural identity.

The Manchurian Methodist network supported extensive women's ministries, youth programs, and lay training initiatives. Women evangelists conducted literacy classes, organized spiritual retreats for mothers, and provided mentorship to adolescent girls in moral education and scripture study. The Epworth League, a Methodist youth organization, operated Bible camps and leadership seminars that fostered Christian identity among second-generation migrants. These educational and social ministries were integrated into the daily life of the Korean diaspora, providing spiritual support and moral guidance within a context of cultural fragmentation and political uncertainty.

Although the issue of Shinto shrine worship would intensify in the late 1930s and early 1940s—ultimately leading to theological resistance and government suppression—the Manchurian Conference during most of the 1930s concentrated on building institutional infrastructure. This included the construction of schoolhouses, training centers, churches, and district headquarters that served as centers of stability in a borderland region characterized by migration, dispossession, and surveillance.

By the decade's end, the Manchurian Methodist Mission Conference had developed into a model of resilient, community-based ecclesiology that was structurally organized, locally rooted, and spiritually active. Its significance lay not only in gospel proclamation but in establishing a visible, viable Korean Christian church in exile that remained responsive to its community's needs.

Russian Orthodox Church

During the tumultuous decade of the 1930s and 1940s, the Russian Orthodox Church emerged as a cornerstone institution within Manchuria's displaced Russian community, functioning simultaneously as a source of spiritual guidance and a fortress against encroaching Soviet influence. The Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia (ROCOR), with its administrative center in Harbin, underwent significant institutional expansion during this period under the stewardship of influential leaders such as Bishop Dimitry Voznesensky. The church's institutional network encompassed major establishments including St. Nicholas Cathedral, various monastic communities, and the Harbin Theological Seminary, all of which served as vital cultural centers dedicated to preserving Russian identity in the face of exile and displacement.

The ecclesiastical leadership during this era proved particularly influential in shaping the community's spiritual and cultural trajectory. Bishop Dimitry, who received his appointment as vicar of Hailar in the early 1930s, distinguished himself through his commitment to extensive pastoral outreach, conducting regular visits to isolated parishes scattered throughout northern Manchuria. These pastoral journeys served to consolidate ROCOR's spiritual authority across the region's dispersed Russian settlements. The bishop's influence extended through his family as well, as his son Georgy Voznesensky, who would later become Metropolitan Philaret, contributed significantly to theological education by teaching New Testament studies and pastoral theology at Harbin's St. Vladimir Institute13. Through his instruction, the younger Voznesensky helped cultivate an entire generation of clergy characterized by their doctrinal precision and unwavering opposition to Bolshevik ideology. This leadership structure effectively reinforced the Church's central role in maintaining émigré morale through the provision of liturgical services, educational opportunities, and community assistance programs.

The educational mission of the Russian Orthodox Church in Manchuria proved particularly significant in its broader resistance to Soviet influence. The Harbin Theological Seminary developed into an essential institution for clerical formation, providing traditional Orthodox theological training that had become entirely inaccessible within the Soviet Union due to the Stalinist regime's systematic repression of religious institutions. The seminary's curriculum deliberately emphasized liturgical continuity and the preservation of Russian cultural traditions, positioning itself as a direct intellectual and spiritual counterpoint to Soviet atheistic doctrine. This educational mission extended beyond formal theological training through various publications, most notably The Chinese Evangelist, which served as a vehicle for disseminating anti-communist messaging throughout the émigré community. These publications consistently portrayed Bolshevism as a fundamentally "satanic force" while encouraging émigrés to maintain their Orthodox traditions as concrete acts of spiritual resistance¹. This ideological stance aligned seamlessly with the broader émigré narrative of messianic revanchism—the deeply held belief that Russians were divinely destined to liberate their homeland from what they perceived as godless tyranny14.

The Church's relationship with the Japanese authorities who controlled Manchukuo during this period required careful diplomatic navigation. ROCOR succeeded in maintaining a degree of cautious autonomy under Japanese rule, benefiting from the fact that Japanese authorities permitted Church operations as a useful counterweight to Soviet influence in the region. However, the Orthodox leadership, exemplified by figures like Bishop Dimitry, deliberately avoided any overt political alignment with Japanese imperial ideology. Instead, these leaders concentrated their efforts primarily on pastoral responsibilities and spiritual guidance, skillfully leveraging this studied neutrality to expand their diocesan networks throughout the territory without encountering direct state interference in their religious activities.

The Russian Orthodox Church's presence in Manchuria during this critical decade ultimately represented far more than a simple religious institution serving displaced persons. Rather, it functioned as a comprehensive institution of cultural and spiritual resilience, successfully integrating traditional spiritual ministry with active cultural preservation efforts and sustained anti-communist activism. The Church's expansion under ROCOR's guidance—manifested through the establishment of seminaries, the development of skilled clergy leadership, and the maintenance of consistent ideological opposition to Soviet communism—provided the émigré community with a cohesive identity firmly rooted in Orthodox tradition and sustained by revanchist aspirations for their homeland's eventual liberation15. Despite operating under the constant surveillance of Japanese authorities and navigating the complex political landscape of occupied Manchuria, the Church consistently prioritized émigré solidarity and spiritual cohesion over potentially compromising political entanglements. This approach ultimately established a lasting legacy of spiritual defiance against Soviet repression that would continue to influence Russian Orthodox communities well beyond the boundaries of Manchuria and the temporal limits of this particular historical period.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Not a movement as such, but the incorporation of the Catholic Church into the heavily engineered religious structure of Manchukuo was one that unquestionably would have been perceived as potentially friendly by some.

Methodist Expansion and Lay Mobilization under Korean Leadership

The 1930s saw a parallel transformation within the Methodist denomination. In the early 20th century, Methodist missions in Manchuria had been organized separately by the American Southern Methodist Episcopal Church (MECS) and the Northern Methodist Episcopal Church (MEC), with missionaries such as John W. Mood initiating work in cities like Yanji and Harbin. However, the dual structure created redundancy and confusion on the ground. In 1931, the two branches were unified under the newly established Chosun Methodist Mission Annual Conference in Manchuria, marking a shift toward Korean-led organizational unity16.

This administrative restructuring empowered Korean clergy and laypeople to take full control of church management. Figures such as Kim Dae-rim and Sun Woo-gap served not only as pastors but also as district superintendents and administrators. Under their guidance, the Methodist Church expanded its influence across Eastern Manchuria, planting new congregations, developing medical and educational facilities, and organizing lay associations such as women’s groups, youth fellowships, and Bible study societies. Korean women played an especially active role in this expansion, establishing kindergartens, girls’ schools, clinics, and literacy programs, particularly in areas underserved by state or foreign aid.

Unlike the Presbyterians, who emphasized ordained leadership and theological training, the Methodists placed significant emphasis on lay participation and community service. Their approach fostered a deeply embedded, socially engaged church that addressed not only spiritual needs but also the practical challenges of Korean immigrant communities—poverty, illiteracy, health, and displacement17. This allowed the Methodist Church to become a transformative force in diasporic society, reinforcing Korean identity through both religious and civic leadership.

By the late 1920s and early 1930s, the Methodist Church in Manchuria had grown into a substantial and increasingly indigenized movement. According to the 1929 statistics for the Southern Methodist mission, there were 42 churches in the North Manchuria district and 20 in the Siberia district, with a total of 64 churches. The number of Korean pastors and evangelists in these two districts reached 44, serving a combined membership of 3,496 believers (2,524 in North Manchuria and 972 in Siberia). Sunday schools were active, with 21 in North Manchuria and 7 in Siberia, enrolling a total of 1,609 students (1,371 in North Manchuria and 238 in Siberia). Educational work was also significant, with 16 schools in North Manchuria serving 527 students. The number of foreign missionaries had dwindled, with nearly all church leadership, evangelism, and administration handled by Koreans. However, as the 1930s progressed, the Methodist Church in Manchuria and Siberia experienced a marked decline in numbers and institutional strength. This was due to a combination of factors: the rise of Japanese colonial control in Manchuria after the 1931 Manchurian Incident, increasing anti-religious persecution in Soviet Siberia, economic hardship, and the instability of Korean immigrant communities. By 1931, after the unification under the Chosun Methodist Mission Annual Conference, the church reported 67 congregations (20 in North Manchuria and 47 in East Manchuria) and a total of 1,788 registered members—a significant decrease from the late 1920s. By 1933, the number of churches in the Gan-do region had dropped to 24, with 1,644 members18.

This decline reflected not only the impact of external repression—such as Soviet anti-religious policies and Japanese surveillance—but also the forced migration and dispersal of Korean communities, as well as internal challenges in maintaining church infrastructure and leadership. Many pastors and lay leaders relocated from Siberia to Manchuria, and some congregations were lost entirely. Despite these hardships, the Methodist Church maintained its commitment to education, social service, and lay mobilization, with Korean women continuing to play a vital role in sustaining church and community life

Key Theological Issues

Jilin VA Gaspais was active in campaigning the Holy See to reverse its centuries-old decision to ban Chinese Catholics from participating in ancestral or Confucian rites (and the definition of the latter as idolatry). This advocacy was in response to increasing role of public rites in the life of Manchukuo, including a legal instruction to maintain a Shinto shrine in classrooms, rites for the Manchukuo and Japanese emperors, and rites for the war dead. It mirrored crises in Japan, specifically one involving students at Catholic Sophia University in Tokyo that just narrowly avoided becoming a full-blown crisis (given the backroom maneuvering for and against the Church within the Japanese government).

In 1935, Propaganda Fide issued the instruction Pluries Instanterque formally not only allowing, but encouraging Catholic participation in civic rites that do not directly contradict their conscience as Catholics, as a duty of citizenship. Importantly, this instruction defined these criteria in subjective cultural terms, reviving in essence the much earlier Jesuit argument that because Chinese culture defines ancestor as veneration rather than as idolatry, that the Church should not impose their own definition on the act. This decision was prompted most immediately by the threat of political crisis in Manchukuo and secondarily Japan. With the promulgation of Pluries Instanterque essentially creating precedent, the decision was then applied to similar centuries-old crises of perception and definition, such as the Malabar Rites in India and the disallowance of funerary rites in China, these bans being formally rescinded in 1940 and 1939, respectively. Although many priests on the ground had long taken a de facto forgiving stance to local custom, the formal recognition of the validity of local cultural understandings was a sea-change in Catholic policy that opened the door to Vatican II less than two decades later. This change was long in the making, but it was the threat of crisis in Manchukuo (and lobbying by people like Gaspais and bishops in Japan) that prompted action and set the Church on the road to becoming a truly global entity.

A second but clearly related crisis concerned the pace of indigenization of the Catholic church, particularly the elevation of Chinese bishops and the transformation of administrative status from Vicariate Apostolic to diocese, that is, from a tutelary to a fully self-determining status. Growing an indigenous priesthood and divesting mission from national interest were already overt priorities of the global Church, as expressed in the 1919 Apostolic letter Maximum illud.

Yet unlike the question of cultural recognition, the political crises in Manchuria and across China slowed the pace of indigenization because it was felt that keeping the missionary presence would serve to protect the Church from external threats, particularly those of a foreign nature (where diplomatic cover would be useful and necessary). The greater these threats, the more intense the call to keep the Church under what was essentially diplomatic protection (the same logic as colonialism, it should be noted). The rhetoric surrounding indigenization was less about whether the local Church was ready in terms of sufficient faith (this question was largely settled as early as the Boxers), but rather whether the global climate was simply too dangerous for the Church to exist without European protection. Just like colonialism, the latter argument became difficult to support with the outbreak of war in Europe and the 1940 signing of the Tripartite Act that created the Axis. It would subsequently be revised to say that French (Vichy) sponsorship of the Church provided cover because France was now seen by the Axis as a friendly (or at least neutral) power, but would collapse immediately after the end of WWII, with the long-awaited creation of dioceses (headed by Chinese bishops) taking place across China in 1946.

Anti-Christian Organizations

Although not a movement as such, there were both Japanese and Chinese anti-foreign elements in the government of Manchukuo. Generally, these voices perceived Christianity as a recalcitrant bastion of foreign imperialism. Japanese critics were naturally influenced by what was happening in Japan, such as the shrine controversy at Sophia University (a group of student refused to attend ceremonies at the Yasukuni Shrine) However, these are mentioned in very general terms as “conservatives” but were never significant enough to be tied to any individual, much less an identified faction. The Chinese voices were more muted, as the most strident nationalist cultural defenders (and proponents of ideology such as sanmin zhuyi) had more or less been evicted at the creation of the new state.

Of the two, the Protestants were viewed with greater suspicion, both because many of the missionaries were British and because Protestant Christians had been active in opposing Japan in Korea (1919) and at least some of the Korean migrants to Manchuria were suspected to have been refugees from that suppression. Already under pressure from the previous decade of rising Chinese nationalism, Protestant educational institutions were cast under even greater suspicion. All schools were forced to replace portraits of Sun Yat-sen with those of the Manchukuo and Japanese emperors. The Protestants were the more vociferous (at least in their internal publications) in their opposition to such policies, but also realized that they were powerless to resist. Sources also describe constant surveillance, summary searches, the constant threat of arrest (for foreigners—Chinese Christians were simply arrested), and occasional rumors that (not wishing to display the same weakness that had allowed the Korean revolt to develop) all Christians were to be executed. In all, the Protestants during this decade seemed to take the tactic of laying low and hoping that the storm would pass.

The Catholics were viewed as more willing partners. This comes in part from the long history of the MEP in Japan (including support during Japan’s continental wars), support from well-placed Japanese Catholics, the fact that most MEP were French (and less antagonistic to Japan) and the work of people like Gaspais to actively court a place for Catholics within the new state. Yet even the Catholics were not free of suspicion. In their private correspondence, the small contingent of American Maryknoll priests noted that they were under constant and not always well-intentioned observation.

Yet, many among the Japanese leadership saw common cause with the Christians (again, the Catholics especially) as a bulwark against communism and sanmin zhuyi ideology (the two were often equated in the sources). This is in addition to sympathetic Japanese Catholics acting behind the scenes in Tokyo or Xinjing.

Japanese-sponsored social/religious groups such as the Red Swastika Society and Concordia Society had a similarly double face, supporting the Christians to the degree that they could be made to fit into the carefully-engineered structure of religion as an expression of Manchukuo national unity, but drawing the boundary at foreign mission (especially Anglo-American Protestants). These were semi-independent organizations that were staffed largely by “true believers” in the Manchukuo project and most often high-ranking members of that government. As such, they mirrored directly state initiatives and rhetoric.

Officially, Christians were one of a few approved religious organizations, similar to the later status of “patriotic” churches in China. Propaganda events featuring religion as an expression of national unity would feature a Catholic priest (usually Gaspais), along with an imam and Buddhist monk (others might be included – the structure was not as normative as the “five religions” in China). I have not seen Protestants participating in these events before or after 1939.
