Manchuria: Context (1930-1940)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

Manchukuo never adopted a constitution, and its legal system relied less on codified law than on political edicts issued as needed, and judges who settled cases as they came up, often combining Chinese and Japanese law (which were already similar). The point being that the legal space was still somewhat inchoate. (DuBois 2010) There was direct inspiration for the administration of religion in Manchuria from the Japanese Peace Preservation Law (1937), but this again was not law as such. (Watanabe 1979)

The state of Christianity in Manchukuo closely tracked against events in Japan, although again largely on an episodic level that set political sentiment rather than writing black letter law. As in Japan, Manchukuo Christianity had detractors and supporters, the latter becoming vocal after a 1937 incident in which a group of students at Tokyo’s Sophia University refused, citing religious reasons, to participate in the reverence at the Yasukuni Shrine. This cast the students, and by extension Catholics, as a fifth column within the state, but that argument (and the crisis) was mollified through the intervention of high-ranking Catholics in the government and military. The incident did affect Manchukuo, mostly by deflecting anything similar from taking place there. Catholic missionaries were very keen to avoid a repeat of this incident on their own soil.

The more important legal development came not from the state but from the Catholic Church itself. The 1936 encyclical letter Pluries instanterque, which formally advocated accepting the local explanation and understanding of Shinto ritual in Japan, opened the door to a new Church policy of accepting local religious customs and customs more generally. Of note is the fact that the letter itself was prompted in large part by the potential for the narrowly-averted crisis in Japan to repeat itself in the more complex political setting of Manchukuo. Moreover, as Canon law in this sense respects the idea of precedent, this letter opened the door for far more changes post war. (DuBois 2017; Minamiki 1985)

Manchukuo explicitly declared the equality of the five ethnic groups and freedom of religion through its "Founding Proclamation 建國宣言" and "Regulations for the Protection of Human Rights 人權保障條例." Article 11 of the Regulations for the Protection of Human Rights, issued on March 9, 1932, states: "The people of Manchukuo shall be protected from usury, exorbitant interest rates, and all other forms of unjust economic oppression," and Article 3 specifies: "The people of Manchukuo, regardless of race or religion, shall receive equal protection from the state."

  1. The Dualistic Nature of Manchukuo's Religious Policy Framework

Manchukuo adopted "kingly way politics" as its state-building ideology and promoted Confucianism—referred to as "Yekyo" (禮教)1—as its national doctrine. Consequently, Confucianism became a tool for state education aimed at teaching the principles of the kingly way rather than being practiced as a religion. Alongside kingly way politics, the government also promoted "Harmony of the Five Races," which encouraged unity among the major ethnic groups. This was described in "Proclamation No. 1" in 1931 as follows: "To establish prosperity and world justice through the construction of an ideal paradise, it was declared necessary to eliminate remnants of past harsh politics and disputes, abolish harmful taxes, eradicate bad customs, promote the growth of industry and transportation, and advance religious education" (Go, 2007, p. 270).

While these proclamations superficially reflected modern democratic values—including the elimination of oppressive governance and promotion of inter-ethnic cooperation—they actually functioned as ideological justifications for territorial expansion under the pretense of advancing Asian and global justice. The instrumentalization of religious education served to consolidate ruling authority through ideological indoctrination. This fundamental contradiction between stated principles and actual implementation became increasingly evident in subsequent policy developments.

The Japanese colonial administration's approach to religious freedom exemplified this duality. As articulated by 高木 翔之助 in 滿蒙獨立建國論 (1932, p. 45)4:

"Except for the Soviet Union, which was founded by Lenin and still prohibits new religions, all modern nations guarantee freedom of religion. Since the Manchukuo Republic is, in any case, a modern state, there is no need to prevent individuals from practicing their religions according to their own desires. However, regardless of nationality, those who, under the guise of religious leaders or in the name of deities, mislead the public sentiment or disrupt public order while obstructing the progress of nation-building must be dealt with decisively. We particularly emphasize this point to draw the attention of those leading the great task of nation-building."

This declaration established a conditional framework for religious freedom that subordinated spiritual practices to state-defined parameters of public order and national development. Following the precedent established in Japan and Korea, Christianity and Buddhism received recognition within official religious categories, while Confucianism was repositioned as a secular ideology for social enlightenment. Shinto occupied a privileged position within the Japanese imperial system. However, Manchukuo's treatment of Confucianism diverged significantly, elevating it to the status of state doctrine, which subsequently facilitated its synthesis with Shinto ideology and accelerated the territory's cultural Japanization.

  1. The Ideological Evolution from Confucian Governance to Imperial Synthesis

The theoretical foundation for Manchukuo's governance model drew upon classical Chinese political philosophy while serving contemporary Japanese imperial objectives. During the Warring States period, Han Feizi had argued that effective governance and popular enlightenment could not be achieved through 仁 (benevolence) and 義 (righteousness) alone, advocating instead for legalist approaches emphasizing strong, law-based (法) administration. In deliberate contrast, Confucian political theory promoted 王道 (kingly way) governance, which sought to educate and guide populations through the moral principles of 仁 (benevolence) and 義 (righteousness). The kingly way paradigm prioritized the cultivation of 忠 (loyalty) and 孝 (filial piety) as fundamental civic virtues.

Manchukuo's administration invested considerable resources in inculcating these dual virtues among its diverse population, simultaneously promoting the concept of Japan-Manchukuo unity through policies such as Japanese language promotion and dissemination (回鑾訓民詔書, 19355). The government constructed 88 Confucian temples throughout the territory, where standardized ritual ceremonies—춘정제공 (Spring Sacrificial Ceremony for Confucius) and 추정제공 (Autumn Sacrificial Ceremony for Confucius)—were conducted biannually to reinforce ideological conformity6.

The outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937) marked a critical transformation in this ideological framework. The original kingly way politics evolved into 皇道 (imperial way) ideology, which incorporated Shinto religious concepts asserting Japan's divine status as the "Land of the Gods" and the sacred nature of imperial authority. This ideological shift corresponded with a substantial increase in Shinto shrine construction throughout Manchukuo territory.

  1. Educational Policy Periodization and Religious Integration

The educational policy of Manchukuo can be divided into three periods: the establishment period from 1932 to 1937, marked by the announcement of the "new school system"; the "new deal" period from 1938, around the time of the Second Sino-Japanese War, to the start of the Pacific War in 1941; and the full Japanization period from 1941 to 19458. This educational policy framework provides the essential context for understanding religious policy transformations, as educational institutions served as primary venues for implementing religious control measures. The analysis of religious policy changes must be understood within this educational periodization, focusing particularly on how the Second Sino-Japanese War and Pacific War influenced both educational and religious policy integration. The examination encompasses changes in administrative departments responsible for religious policy oversight, surveillance and control mechanisms targeting religious organizations and clergy, and regulatory frameworks governing religious property and educational assets. While particular attention will be devoted to documenting Protestant and Catholic institutional responses, the discussion encompasses comprehensive religious policies affecting all faith communities operating within educational contexts.

  1. 1932 to 1937: The Establishment Period

Manchukuo's governmental structure comprised hierarchical institutions including the Emperor, Privy Council, Legislative Council, State Council, Court system, and Control Yuan. Within this framework, the Minister of Civil Affairs, operating under State Council authority, maintained responsibility for educational policy, ceremonial protocols, social welfare programs, and religious administration. During the initial establishment period, policy emphasis focused on stabilizing public sentiment through systematic Confucian temple rituals and comprehensive education in social propriety and ceremonial observance.

The Concordia Association (協和会) functioned as a critical intermediary institution for managing religious tensions among diverse ethnic communities. This organization's stated purpose involved mediating inter-ethnic religious conflicts, but its actual function centered on ideological indoctrination and population control through a limited cadre of Japanese administrative personnel, with religious oversight representing a primary operational focus.

Demographic data illustrates the minority status of Japanese administrators within Manchukuo's diverse population. By 1940, Japanese residents numbered between 230,000 (0.8% of total population) and approximately 820,000 (1.8%), eventually reaching 1,662,234 by 1944. The Concordia Association operated as an officially sanctioned organization with Emperor Puyi serving as honorary chairman, the Japanese Kwantung Army commander functioning as honorary advisor, and Manchukuo's Prime Minister holding the presidential position. Established in 1933, this institution ostensibly promoted founding principles and ethnic harmony while actually serving as a mechanism for extending centralized control from administrative centers to provincial territories through systematic self-monitoring networks.

  1. Systematic Development of Religious Oversight Mechanisms

Beginning in 1936, the Religious Issues Research Association was established to develop comprehensive approaches for managing religious and quasi-religious organizations through standardized regulatory frameworks. This institution formulated strategies for religious supervision and drafted implementing regulations through collaborative efforts involving officials and religious leaders from both Japan and Manchukuo territories. Following preliminary surveys conducted in 1936, more extensive investigations were implemented in 1937, accompanied by training programs designed to familiarize religious representatives with Manchukuo's evolving religious policies.

  1. Vatican Relations and Catholic Church Institutional Recognition

Japan's diplomatic efforts to legitimize Manchukuo's governmental authority included securing formal recognition from Vatican authorities. Following the 1929 Lateran Treaty, the Vatican, confronting internal institutional challenges and external political pressures, appointed Bishop Augustin Gaspais (Mgr. Augustin Gaaspiais) in 1934 as the designated representative of Catholic episcopacy within Manchukuo territory. Bishop Gaspais unilaterally severed all diocesan connections with Chinese governmental control and attempted to establish a completely autonomous diocesan administrative system.

Shin Eui Sik (2011) argues that Vatican recognition represented acknowledgment of Manchukuo as a religious rather than political entity, supporting this interpretation through reference to Yoshigoro Taguchi's "Manchukuo and the Catholic Church," which emphasizes the purely religious nature of Roman Curia recognition12. This development represented a significant departure from established Vatican policy, particularly considering Pope Pius XI's recognition of the Chinese Nationalist government following the First United Front's dissolution in 1927, when Communist Party suppression commenced. Pope Pius XI had actively encouraged Catholic participation in the Nationalist regime in 1928. Pope Pius XII, assuming papal authority in 1938, continued supportive policies toward Manchukuo, prompting formal protests from the Chiang Kai-shek government. Regarding Confucian ceremonial practices in Manchukuo, Catholic clergy sought governmental clarification concerning the religious versus secular nature of these rituals, receiving official confirmation that they constituted social rather than religious ceremonies. Based on this governmental response, the Holy See authorized Catholic participation in Confucian rituals on May 28, 1938.

  1. Chronological Development of Religious Policy and Legal Cases

The systematic development of religious control mechanisms can be traced through specific legislative and administrative actions:

December 24, 1932: The "Document on the Prohibition of Political Interference by Lamas" (關于禁止喇嘛干政之件) represented Manchukuo's inaugural religious supervisory decree, announced specifically within the Xing'an General Office, a region with significant Mongol ethnic populations. This document prohibited religious political interference following Manchukuo's establishment, indicating enhanced surveillance of Lamaist activities.13

1933: The "Regulations Concerning Religious Proclamation and Temples" (宗教宣布及ビ寺廟ニ関スル規程) were drafted but remained unimplemented until the "Temporary Regulations for the Control of Temples and Missionaries" (暫行寺廟及布教者取締規則) were announced in 1938. Many provisions within these regulations paralleled the "Proselytization Rules" implemented in Korea in 1915.14

April 28, 1934: The "Document on the Temporary Prohibition of Alms Collection by Monks and Taoist Priests" (關于暫禁僧道化緣之件) prohibited monastic alms collection practices on grounds of social order disruption.

1935: Protestant educational institutions demonstrated resistance to state-mandated Confucian ceremonies, exemplified by Munkwang Middle School's refusal to participate.

July 7-8, 1936: The Ministry of Education convened Western Protestant missionaries and institutional representatives to discuss control methodologies for religious organizations and educational facilities.

August 30, 1936: The "Temple Management Ordinance of Fengtian Province" (奉天省管理寺廟條例) and "Detailed Regulations for Temple Registration Implementation in Fengtian City" (奉天市寺廟登記施行細則) were promulgated, requiring comprehensive investigations of Buddhist, Taoist, and Lamaist temple properties and personnel. These regulations mandated educational office approval for all temple financial transactions and required personal information submission from all religious personnel affiliated with temple institutions.

June 1936: Ministry of Education Directive No. 65, "Regulations on the Disposition and Transfer of Temple Assets" (關於取締廟産變賣之件), established state ownership of all religious organizational assets.

June 30, 1936: Document No. 204 from the Jiandao Provincial Office mandated registration of all temple, church, and missionary facility properties as state-owned assets under the "Protecting the Nation and Promoting the Faith" (護國弘宗) directive, requiring supervisory approval for any property disposition.

  1. Police-Based Surveillance Systems for Religious Monitoring

Following the precedent established in Korea, where military police and civilian law enforcement monitored religious organizations, particularly Christian churches, Manchukuo established a specialized affairs division within the Police Department for religious activity inspection and organizational monitoring. The ideological special police constituted a specialized force tasked with eliminating elements threatening national security and public order under the pretext of ideological reform. The 1935 ideological countermeasure plan specifically designated those "using the name of religion or other organizations to carry out anti-Japanese and anti-Manchukuo activities" as primary surveillance targets.

For Islamic communities, the Qingzhen Council was established to protect members amid power struggles between Japanese and Chinese forces. On May 27, 1937, the Manchukuo government appointed Japanese special affairs commissioner Seiji Honda (本田誠二) as council advisor. In 1933, the Concordia Association surveyed Catholic-majority areas, designating these villages as model "Kingly Way, Joyful Land" (王道樂土) communities with installed police stations for systematic surveillance. Korean churches received particularly intensive monitoring and control.

Article 7 of the "Manchurian Guidance Policy" promulgated by Kwantung Army Command in 1932 stated: "Education shall be based on the educational system within Korea and adapted to the circumstances of Manchukuo, focusing on general education and vocational training. Additionally, the promotion of appropriate religious teachings, guidance of media outlets, and ideological leadership will be pursued. Special efforts will be made to guide the educated class to eliminate anti-Japanese and communist ideologies."

As demonstrated in population data, Koreans were classified as Japanese subjects; however, administrative distinctions separated mainland Japanese from Korean populations. While Koreans nominally held Japanese citizenship and extraterritorial rights within Manchukuo under Kwantung Army jurisdiction, they remained colonized subjects distinct from mainland Japanese. Their religious activities were subject to control mechanisms and served as educational tools for Korean Japanization. Even following extraterritorial rights abolition, Japanese Kwantung Army authority over education, military affairs, and Shinto administration remained intact, with Koreans receiving no exemptions. In 1936, the Korean People's Association was ostensibly dissolved with authority transferred to Manchukuo government administration, but Japanese control mechanisms remained unchanged15.

  1. Political Mobilization of Religious Organizations

During the period when investigations of religious individuals and organizations were conducted and related laws regarding religious organizational assets were established, numerous religious organizations were formed to systematically promote these efforts and facilitate persuasion, appeasement, and legal enforcement. The General Assembly of Islam in Manchukuo was organized on July 3, 1934, with Japanese official Kawamura Kodo (川村狂堂)16 appointed as president. In 1938, Taoist organizations established the "Manchukuo Taoist General Assembly"17 and conducted rituals for Manchukuo's peace and in honor of Japanese and Manchukuo soldiers killed in warfare.

  1. Comparative Analysis: Confucian Rituals in Manchukuo versus Shinto Shrine Rituals in Korea and Protestant Responses

In 1935, schools established by the American Northern Presbyterian Church in Pyongyang, Korea (including Soongsil School) voluntarily closed in protest against mandatory Shinto shrine worship participation. During the same year in Manchukuo, Munkwang Middle School, founded by the Irish Presbyterian Church, refused participation in Confucian ceremonial rites. A publication by the International Missionary Council titled "Religious Freedom under Japanese Rule" (日本支配下における宗教の自由) documented stronger church resistance in Korea, attributing this difference to greater opposition evoked by emperor worship, which lacked the cultural foundation that Confucian worship possessed. The publication noted: "The principle of state-promoted and state-mandated worship forms the foundation of Japanese rule. This has a significant impact on Christian activities," explaining that Christianity was similarly targeted in Manchukuo "because it was the most robust, independent, and intellectual center of spiritual resistance against the 'enslavement plan' of Manchukuo."18

Additional documentation regarding Confucian worship issues and Christianity control requirements appears in the "Religious Investigation Data, Volume 2: Reports on Various Religions in Jilin, Jiandao, and Binjiang,"19 supervised by the Ministry of People's Welfare and conducted by Professor Otani Koho from July 20 to September 3, 1936. In his report to Kwantung Army Headquarters following the investigation, Otani identified Buddhism as a religion expected to cooperate with Japanese governmental public enlightenment efforts, while characterizing shamanism as requiring education and improvement. He emphasized that Christianity, particularly Christian educational institutions, required careful monitoring due to suspected anti-Japanese elements. Regional findings related to Christianity included:

Jilin Province: Confucianism maintained minimal survival but pursued revival through Confucian ritual performance since Manchukuo's establishment. Regarding Christianity, police departments did not yet consider it a public security threat but identified the American Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA) as requiring maximum caution due to perceived underground anti-Japanese activities and state loyalty concept weakening. Three Christian elementary schools operated in Jilin Province, with Munkwang Middle School refusing Confucian ritual participation. Additionally, Munkwang Middle School students deliberately distorted "Long live the Japanese Empire" chants and expressed opposition to Manchukuo during the "Huaihuan Xunmin Edict" (回鑾訓民詔書) commemoration ceremony by singing alternative songs instead of the national anthem. The overall policy toward Christianity throughout Manchuria ideally sought complete eradication; however, if permitted, it should be maintained through simple and unified systems.20 James McWhirter, Munkwang Middle School principal, received warnings that continued refusal to attend Confucian ceremonies could result in school closure and missionary expulsion.

Binjiang Province: Protestant church establishment applications were rapidly increasing, but municipal-level policies remained undeveloped, necessitating prompt measure development. Many textbooks used in denominational schools dated from the Republic of China era, requiring reading supervision. Additionally, since missionaries and Christian affiliates maintained contact with Republic of China churches and reached low-income Manchukuo populations under missionary pretexts, close observation was required to determine potential espionage activities.21

In spring 1936, Liaoyang City, Fengtian Province required Mundeok Middle School and Yukje Girls' School, both established by the Free Church of Scotland, to participate in monthly Confucian rites. However, the schools petitioned to conduct baptisms during these times instead, considering ritual participation religious ceremony, but their request was denied.

In July 1936, representatives of major Protestant denominations operating Christian schools, including the Irish Presbyterian Church, Danish Lutheran Church, and Free Church of Scotland, were invited to visit Manchuria for the Manchurian Christian Presbyterian Conference. During their visit, Ministry of Education representatives invited them to a meeting attended by Ministry of Education officials and Kwantung Army officers. The government demanded that McWhirter, Munkwang Middle School principal, respond with simple "yes" or "no" regarding compliance with Manchukuo laws, threatening school closures and missionary expulsion for non-compliance with national laws. McWhirter pledged to follow Manchukuo law, with other missionaries required to sign as witnesses. At this meeting, a booklet titled "Direction" was distributed detailing the following requirements:

  1. Large-scale religious organizational investigations would be conducted in the near future.

  2. Establishing new religious organizations would require approval, with heretical teachings prohibited. Religious organizational dissolution would occur under Ministry of People's Welfare guidance.

  3. All meetings except regular or customary gatherings such as Sunday services must be reported in advance. Economic or political discussions during meetings were prohibited. Religious organizations must focus solely on religious matters.

  4. Mandatory registration of personal information for church members and evangelists.

  5. Annual reports, denominational property ownership, and doctrinal content must be recorded on designated investigation forms and submitted by January 31.

On September 2, a similar meeting was held in Fengtian, attended by three missionaries from Irish, Scottish, and Danish churches, demonstrating that Christianity control was planned from Manchukuo's establishment.

  1. 1937-1941

Around 1937, with the onset of the Second Sino-Japanese War, Japan's colonization of Manchukuo became full-scale. In November of this year, Manchukuo signed treaties with Japan to abolish extraterritorial rights and transfer administrative control over the South Manchurian Railway subsidiary areas. These treaties aimed to rescind Japan's extraterritorial rights in Manchukuo and to grant Japanese people the same rights as Chinese by allowing various ethnic groups to coexist within Manchukuo. The transfer of South Manchurian Railway subsidiary areas was largely symbolic, considering international public opinion at the time. Article 14 of these treaties allowed the Japanese government and citizens to administer shrines within Manchukuo's territory.

Starting in 1938, Japanese teachers were trained for Shinto education in Manchuria. The Japanese Emperor's visit to Manchukuo in 1940 marked a shift towards "황도주의" (Imperial Way Doctrine) and "황도낙토" (Imperial Paradise). He declared that the development of Manchukuo was due to the divine grace of the Emperor and Japan, vowing to further construct the National Founding Shrine (建国神廟) to fulfill loyalty and filial piety. It was also announced that any person who failed to show proper respect at the National Founding Shrine or Chongryeongmyo (忠靈廟) would be sentenced for the crime of impiety. The administration of Japanese shrines in Manchukuo was expanded independently through the Zaiban Religious Affairs Department, which also increased memorial rites for soldiers who had participated in the war under the banner of ethnic unity.

  1. Key Legislative Developments and Legal Framework

September 24, 1938: Provisional Regulation for the Supervision of Temples and Missionaries 暫行寺廟及布教者取締規則

Previous studies24 compared the previously unimplemented Regulation (1933) and the Promulgation Rules that came into effect in Korea (1915) with the "Provisional Regulation for the Supervision of Temples and Missionaries" (1938, hereafter referred to as the Supervision Regulation) (Table 6 and 7). Although the draft included a clause on fundraising (Article 9), it was not reflected in the final regulation. Instead, the Supervision Regulation detailed the definition of missionaries (Article 1), mandated the reporting of missionary methods (Article 2, Clause 11), and required the registration of real estate information (Article 8).

Additionally, the penalty provisions were specified and made more stringent (Articles 10 to 13). Article 10 states that if the Minister of Civil Affairs judges an organization to be harmful to public interest, its establishment cannot be permitted. Article 11 specifies that if the representative of a religious organization fails to report the required information, they may lose their position. Article 12 indicates that if religious proselytizing violates social order, the minister or mayor can suspend the activities of that organization. Article 13 prescribes fines for failing to report the establishment, dissolution, or merger of an organization. This implies that the government was granted the authority to monitor and regulate everything from the establishment to the operation and dissolution of religious organizations.

    1. Missionary Responses and Resistance Cases

The most documented case of direct resistance comes from Charles A. Leonard (1928–1943), an American Southern Baptist missionary whose journey from Shandong to Harbin placed him at the center of mounting religious tensions. Ministering primarily to Russian exiles in Harbin, Leonard quickly drew suspicion from Japanese authorities who branded him a potential spy. Rather than retreat under this scrutiny, Leonard's response to tightening religious controls was both principled and strategic. He counseled believers to remain unregistered, viewing state registration requirements as fundamental violations of religious liberty. His activism extended beyond pastoral advice—Leonard traveled directly to Changchun to engage both Manchukuo officials and local Christian leaders in dialogue about the policy's implications.

Leonard's most significant act of resistance came in December 1938, when he organized a meeting of North American missionaries that produced a detailed memorandum cataloguing objectionable elements in the new religious regulations. This collective protest culminated in his submission of a comprehensive 15-page formal protest to the Ministry of Civil Affairs in 1939, with copies forwarded to his home mission board. Leonard's opposition centered on two critical concerns: the requirement for government approval to establish or dissolve churches, which he argued undermined religious autonomy, and the looming threat of mandatory Shinto worship practices already imposed in Korea. He warned that extending such requirements to Manchukuo would force Christians to choose between their faith and legal compliance30.

In parallel, Scottish Presbyterian missionaries, particularly those affiliated with the Church of Scotland and Irish Presbyterian missions, engaged in more discreet but persistent resistance. Stationed in cities like Fengtian (Mukden), Harbin, and Hsinking (Changchun), these missionaries rejected overt collaboration with the Concordia Association (協和会)—an organization the Japanese used to politically integrate civil and religious groups. In 1932–1933, when authorities urged all civic bodies to join, the missionaries refused to participate, seeing it as a betrayal of Christian doctrine and an instrument of Japanese imperial ideology. The pressure intensified in 1935–1936, when the Japanese authorities ordered mission schools to adopt a nationalistic curriculum that included ancestor worship, emperor reverence, and militarist indoctrination. In response, several Scottish missionaries in Fengtian and surrounding areas resigned rather than comply, while others continued operating their schools but quietly altered the curriculum, replacing mandated ideology with biblical teaching, Western science, and ethics.31

The pattern of resistance extended beyond individual missionaries to encompass entire institutions. Educational and medical establishments became unexpected sites of organized defiance, with the Moukden Medical College serving as a particularly notable example. When faced with government demands to incorporate imperial rituals and allow state monitors, mission staff chose strategic withdrawal over compliance. Some resigned their positions entirely, while others maintained their roles but systematically minimized ideological content during instruction. These institutions evolved into crucial safe spaces for Korean and Chinese Christians seeking to avoid state surveillance, creating networks of protection that operated beneath the surface of official compliance.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

Speaking specifically about the Manchukuo period, the beginning of the decade saw Japan actively courting the foreign powers in an attempt to raise international support. The unequivocal condemnation of the state by the League of Nations Lytton Commission (which was British-led) came as a genuine surprise to many, and prompted a radical change in attitude, resulting in the state’s increasing isolation and turn to extremism.

This would manifest as a desire to contain the missions, if not expel them. The problem was rarely stated as an ideological clash with Christianity, which the official ideology of the state did not oppose. Indeed, it promoted an ideal of ‘patriotic churches’ under the leadership of the Concordia Association (xiehe hui/kyōwa kai). The guiding myth of the state that its founding was a spontaneous expression by the people of Manchuria was replicated in the ideal that its component pieces would all be drawn to a new patriotic participation. (Chou 1996; Duara 2003) Thus, it was important that religious leaders be shown (in the press and elsewhere) as voluntarily seeking to serve that state as Christian (or other) leaders. It was not a matter of rejecting the religious identity as sublimating it to a corporatist vision. Indeed, the state projected itself as a protector of religion, in contrast to the forced atheism of the Soviet Union or (as they depicted it) Sun Yat-sen’s “communism.” (DuBois 2017; Wiest 1988)

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

What should be remembered is that until Japan’s 1931 invasion, and to some degree even thereafter, Japan was widely viewed by most foreigners and even many Chinese as the progressive force in the region. Only with the 1935 report of the Lytton Commission, which unequivocally stated that the Manchukuo state was a puppet of Japan, was this reputational coffin finally closed. From that point, Anglo-American sentiment would turn decisively against Japan and against the Manchukuo project. This would further foster a mutually hostile atmosphere for Protestant missions in the region.

Patriotism is hard to gauge, because the creation of Manchukuo prompted an exodus of the most patriotic Chinese elements, including the relocation of Northeast University. (Wang 1988) There was little organized underground activity, as these elements preferred to regroup in areas under Chinese control, first behind the Great Wall, and later to the Southeast. Manchukuo did have its nationalist element, especially the ideologues who arrived from elsewhere in the Japanese Empire. (Duara 2003) The growing thought surveillance amongst intellectuals at the (new) Manchukuo Foundation University and the social engineering projects that picked up speed after the Lytton Report did have boosters, but the direction was less anti-Christian than an increasingly paranoiac mix of anti-Western, anti-Communist, and anti-Chinese. Towards the end of the decade, Protestants were particularly caught up in charges of anti-Japanese sentiment (but were not deported). Especially after the French surrender to the Nazis, the French Catholics were much better able to weather this storm.
