Manchuria: Agency (1910-1920)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

Catholic

Pierre Lalouyer, M.E.P. 1850-1923

Lalouyer remained the VA of Jilin throughout the 1910s, remaining in that position until his death in 1923. He seems to have spent the decade facing a series of crises in the new district, including two bouts of epidemic disease, endemic political uncertainty (the greatest complaint in sources from the period is the breakdown of order), and his own declining health.

Marie-Félix Choulet, M.E.P. 1854-1923.

Choulet remained the VA of Mukden throughout the 1910s, retiring from the position in 1920, and remaining in Shenyang until his death in 1923. Like Lalouyer (and for the same reasons), Choulet does not seem to have accomplished much during this decade. He did oversee the creation of a new novitiate (training for religious orders, in this case female under the direction of the Sisters of Providence). He spent much of the decade in ill health and 1912 returned briefly to France for treatment of dysentery, and by the end of the decade was repeatedly petitioning Rome to be allowed to retire. This permission was granted in 1920, but delayed until 1922 in wait of a successor.

Protestant

John Ross

07/06/1842 ~ 08/04/1915

Although John Ross formally retired from missionary service in 1910 and died in 1915, his influence continued to shape major developments in Manchurian Protestantism throughout the decade. His inclusion in this period’s narrative is justified not by direct activity, but by the maturation and expanded influence of the institutions he established, the leaders he trained, and the missiological principles he advocated. Following his return to Edinburgh, Ross maintained intellectual engagement with missionary work. He participated in the 1910 Edinburgh World Missionary Conference, contributed to mission theory, and sustained correspondence with church leaders in Manchuria until his death in 19151. This ongoing involvement provided ideological continuity and guidance, particularly for Canadian and Chinese leaders operating within the organizational framework he had created.

The theological college Ross founded in Mukden in 1898 became central to developing indigenous Christian leadership throughout Manchuria. With an increasingly local faculty, the institution trained pastors and evangelists who expanded church growth, education, and social influence during a period of political instability. Ross’s emphasis on indigenous agency, articulated through the “three-self” model of self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation, enabled many Manchurian churches to function autonomously by the 1910s, maintaining resilience during periods of anti-foreign sentiment and Japanese colonial expansion2.

Ross’s Korean New Testament translation remained widely distributed, particularly among Korean immigrant communities in Manchuria who were fleeing escalating repression in colonial Korea. These Korean Christians, characterized by nationalist and revivalist orientations, established churches and schools using the materials and organizational models Ross had developed. His earlier recruitment and training of Korean and Chinese lay leaders enabled continued church expansion without foreign oversight. Manchurian Protestantism also benefited from Ross’s ecumenical approach. His preparatory work facilitated the formation of the Church of Christ in Manchuria, a unified, multi-denominational organization with independent presbyteries under Chinese clerical leadership. This structure enabled flexible, locally-responsive approaches to social change, revolution, and warlordism throughout the decade3.

Ross’s missiological writings, including Mission Methods in Manchuria, continued to influence mission work across East Asia, offering a framework for culturally adaptive ministry grounded in language acquisition, translation, and local leadership development. His vision persisted beyond his lifetime, guiding missionaries, Chinese Christians, and Korean Christian nationalists through a period of extensive transformation.

John William Hancher

1867 ~ 1939

Dr. J. W. Hancher maintained his leadership of the Yingkou Mission Hospital throughout the 1910s, establishing it as a central institution within the American Presbyterian Mission’s operations in southern Manchuria. By 1913, the hospital treated over 8,000 outpatients annually, providing essential medical services to both urban and rural communities. This medical work represented a practical manifestation of Christian service, particularly significant as traditional evangelistic approaches faced growing resistance in regions increasingly skeptical of Western religious influence4.

Hancher’s medical mission reflected the broader evolution occurring within Protestant missions in China during this period—a shift from exclusively evangelistic focus toward social service-oriented approaches. Operating within a context of mounting anti-foreign sentiment and emerging Chinese nationalism, the Yingkou hospital provided a form of Christian witness that avoided direct confrontation. Hancher maintained that compassionate medical care constituted a demonstration of gospel principles in itself. A notable aspect of Hancher’s methodology during this decade involved his systematic integration of Chinese assistants and staff into hospital operations. He provided training in basic medical procedures to local workers, with some assuming pastoral and evangelical responsibilities within hospital wards5. This approach represented an early implementation of indigenization principles, recognizing local agency as essential for the mission’s sustained effectiveness.

Hancher’s reports and participation in missionary conferences demonstrate his advocacy for a comprehensive mission model. He consistently argued that medical work should not function merely as a conversion tool but should be valued as a legitimate form of Christian witness. He expressed concern that excessive focus on conversion statistics threatened to compromise authentic service to those in need.

The social impact of Hancher’s work extended beyond medical care. The hospital earned the trust of local Chinese authorities, who regarded it as a neutral and dependable institution even during periods of political instability. During times of conflict, including refugee crises and civil disruptions, the hospital provided care to all individuals regardless of ethnicity, class, or religious affiliation, thereby demonstrating an alternative Christian ethic within a deeply fragmented society.

Robert Grierson

1868 ~ 1965

Although Robert Grierson did not reside or directly administer mission stations within Manchuria during the 1910s, his inclusion in the analysis of Christian movements in the region remains justified. From his base in Sŏngjin, Hamgyŏng Province—immediately adjacent to the Korean-Manchurian frontier—Grierson exercised ideological, administrative, and theological influence that extended across the Tumen River into Manchuria. As a senior figure in the Canadian Presbyterian Mission, his decisions affected not only the development of Korean Christian communities in northeastern Korea but also influenced how those communities organized, migrated, and evangelized in the Korean-populated regions of Manchuria.

Grierson’s significance during this decade stems from his institutional leadership and ideological position during a formative period. He advocated for Korean agency and education, expressed sympathy with anti-colonial sentiments, and maintained relationships with Korean Christian leaders who operated across the Hamgyŏng-Manchuria border. His documented friendship with Yi Dong-hui—an independence activist and intellectual founder of Korean communism—culminated in Grierson facilitating Yi’s escape from Japanese authorities in 1913, a decision that reflected his support for Korean nationalist aspirations and his resistance to imperial authority.6

Although the rejection of Grierson’s 1921 mission history manuscript falls outside the decade under examination, the work was based on his accumulated experience and observations from the preceding two decades. In this unpublished history, Grierson criticized the Canadian Presbyterian Foreign Mission Board’s narrow evangelical utilitarianism and attempted to present a more nuanced account of mission development in Korea and Manchuria.7 His frank assessment led to the manuscript’s suppression by mission administrators, who feared such reflections could undermine the ideological foundations of the missionary enterprise. The manuscript’s focus on both Korea and Manchuria demonstrates the integrated nature of these mission fields and Grierson’s understanding of their interconnected development.

While Grierson’s physical presence remained primarily within Korean territory during this decade, his conceptual and administrative influence—particularly in education, theological direction, and the mobilization of Korean Christian workers—positioned him as a significant figure in shaping the mission strategies and conflicts that would characterize the Canadian Presbyterian presence in Manchuria during the 1920s. His influence on Korean Christian autonomy efforts, which became more visible in Manchuria after this period, supports his inclusion in the 1910s Manchurian religious landscape, with appropriate acknowledgment of his geographic positioning.

Dugald Christie

1855 ~ 1936

Although Dugald Christie officially retired in 1913, his influence continued to shape the religious, medical, and educational institutions of Manchuria throughout the 1910s. During this period, Christie played a pivotal role in responding to the pneumonic plague outbreak of 1910–1911, established the region’s first modern medical college, and served as both a respected medical practitioner and an important intermediary between Chinese authorities and international communities.

The pneumonic plague crisis of 1910–1911 marked a significant chapter in Christie’s career. His appointment to the International Plague Prevention Committee represented an unusual recognition for a missionary physician. Christie’s hospital in Mukden served as a central treatment facility, providing care to Chinese, Russian, and Japanese patients without discrimination while maintaining coordination with government officials, including Viceroy Hsi Liang. Christie’s implementation of quarantine protocols and advocacy for comprehensive public health reforms in Manchuria contributed to containing the outbreak and demonstrated the capacity of Christian medical missions to function as reliable public institutions during emergencies. Despite challenging conditions—including widespread panic, overcrowded facilities, and severe winter weather—Christie maintained hospital operations while protecting both patients and staff8.

Christie’s establishment of the Mukden Medical College (MMC) in 1912 represents perhaps his most significant institutional achievement. Following years of negotiation and with support from reform-minded officials such as Viceroy Hsu Shih-chang, the MMC became Manchuria’s first Western-style medical institution. The college trained Chinese physicians and nurses, integrating scientific medical education with Christian ethical principles. The MMC rapidly developed into a leading center for modern medical education in Northeast China, helping to reduce the region’s dependence on foreign medical personnel while producing a new cohort of locally trained professionals. By 1920, the institution had graduated more than 60 physicians, who subsequently served in hospitals, dispensaries, and educational facilities throughout the region9.

Christie’s medical and educational work was closely connected to his diplomatic and cultural engagement. His fluency in Mandarin and demonstrated respect for Chinese customs positioned him as an effective intermediary between Chinese officials and foreign representatives, particularly during public health crises. During the plague outbreak, Christie helped reduce tensions by facilitating cooperation between Chinese authorities and Japanese and Russian medical personnel in the region. His ability to navigate complex political relationships without compromising his mission objectives enhanced the legitimacy of Christian institutions among Chinese bureaucrats and the general population. Christie also maintained a consistent stance against opium addiction, connecting it to broader social and moral deterioration. His medical documentation of addiction and its effects influenced missionary societies to establish treatment facilities and launch anti-opium campaigns throughout Manchuria. These initiatives subsequently became essential components of both church-affiliated healthcare and social reform programs across the region.

Following the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), Christie’s medical network continued to serve refugee populations and war-affected communities well into the 1910s. His dispensary system throughout Manchuria provided sustained relief efforts, and the reputation he established for impartial service during wartime strengthened public confidence in mission hospitals. This humanitarian work further reinforced the moral credibility of Christian missions during a period when foreign involvement in China was frequently viewed with suspicion.

Christie’s memoir, Thirty Years in Moukden (1914), published shortly after his retirement, served both as personal testimony and missiological documentation. The work presented detailed observations of Manchurian society and emphasized the importance of contextualized missionary approaches, arguing that effective Christian service required understanding local conditions rather than imposing Western practices. Christie’s reflections influenced subsequent discussions about indigenization in missionary strategy. By the decade’s end, Christie’s institutional contributions—particularly the Mukden Medical College—represented a concrete embodiment of his comprehensive mission philosophy. The institution reflected his conviction that medical care and education should be culturally integrated, professionally competent, and spiritually grounded. As new missionary leaders assumed his responsibilities, they worked within institutional frameworks shaped by his emphasis on partnership, public trust, and local capacity development.

Korean Protestant Church Leaders in Manchuria

Between 1910 and 1920, three Korean Christian leaders—Kim Jin-geun, Kim Young-je, and Han Kyung-hee—made significant contributions to Korean diaspora communities in Manchuria through evangelism, church establishment, education, and independence activism. Their work profoundly influenced the cultural and spiritual development of Koreans in exile.

Kim Jin-geun 김진근

Kim Jin-geun, a graduate of Pyongbuk Presbytery, was commissioned in 1910 as a missionary pastor to West Jiandao (서간도), where Korean immigration had surged following Japan’s annexation of Korea. Working alongside fellow Korean pastors Choi Bong-seok and Kim Nae-beom, he established local congregations and schools that formed the foundation for sustainable church communities. While detailed documentation of his subsequent activities remains sparse, Kim’s early foundational work created essential congregational and educational structures that later leaders would build upon throughout the 1920s10.

Kim Young-je 김영제

Dispatched in 1910 by Hamgyeong Presbytery, Kim Young-je concentrated his ministry in North Jiandao (북간도), particularly in Longjing and Yanbian. In this region of rapid Korean migration and displacement, he dedicated himself to church planting, community building, and training local lay leaders. His ministry strengthened spiritual foundations while fostering cultural and civic identity through religious instruction and community self-governance. Though not directly engaged in armed resistance, Kim’s emphasis on community development and education helped transform North Jiandao’s Korean churches into centers of cultural identity and national consciousness11.

Han Kyung-hee 한경희 韓敬禧

1881 ~ 1935

Han Kyung-hee, known as the “Paul of Manchuria,” uniquely combined missionary work with anti-colonial resistance. Born in Pyeongan Province in 1881, he converted to Christianity in 1903, studied at Pyongyang Theological Seminary, and was ordained in 1914 before receiving appointment to pastoral ministry in northern Manchuria along the Chinese Eastern Railway.

By 1917, Han was leading the Samwonpo congregation in West Jiandao. In early 1919, before news of the March First Movement reached Manchuria, he organized the region’s first independence demonstration involving approximately 300 participants—known as the March 12 Uprising at Eunyang School. Han was instrumental in establishing the Seoro Military Government (서로군정서) in cooperation with the Shanghai-based Provisional Government. As principal of institutions including Eunyang, Dongmyeong (founded 1924), and Samsung Middle School (opened 1926), he combined Christian education with nationalist principles. He also established the South Manchurian Korean Education Association in 1923 to advance both secular and religious education among Korean youth.

Arrested in 1928 for his political activities and educational leadership, Han was imprisoned in Sinuiju until 1932. Despite personal tragedy—including his son’s assassination—he returned to northern Manchuria in 1933 to minister to over ten communities near Milsan and Horim. In January 1935, during a bandit attack, he died as a martyr on the frozen Osori River. Han received posthumous recognition with the Presidential Merit Medal in Patriotism in 1996, remembered as an exemplar of missionary dedication and national liberation12.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

There is mixed messaging about whether civil officials were pro- or anti-Christian.

Catholic sources especially note instances of cooperation, such as religious processions in Harbin and Tongken (1915) receiving police guard, and repeated mention of courtesy visits from officials who were keen to state their goodwill towards the mission. Goodwill was undoubtedly increased by both Catholic and Protestant medical work in the face of outbreaks of pneumonic plague (1910-11) and influenza (1919). This medical work did create some ill will, mostly among doctors of traditional medicine as well as communities who were upended by epidemic prevention measures, but it is hard to tell how systematic this was.

The construction of schools became a source of official friction due to the new nationalism that followed the 1911 Revolution. Schools faced pressure from the Ministry of Education to institute patriotic education and to secularize. The former was generally less an issue of contention than the latter. Although the Catholics would face some friction in the subsequent decade, the weight of this fell on the Protestants, who placed particular emphasis on education.

Canadian Presbyterian Mission (CPM)

The decade between 1910 and 1920 witnessed significant expansion of the Canadian Presbyterian Mission (CPM) throughout Manchuria, driven primarily by the growth of Korean Christian communities formed through migration, political upheaval, and rising anti-Japanese sentiment. The CPM’s establishment of a mission station in Yongjeong in 1913 represented a strategic response to the pronounced nationalist fervor among Korean Christians in the Kando (Jiandao) region. Missionaries anticipated that their institutional presence might channel the community’s politically charged energy toward spiritual objectives, yet they ultimately proved unable to separate Christianity from its function as an instrument of Korean resistance.

The Canadian approach diverged notably from American missionary practices. Whereas American missions typically avoided employing politically engaged Koreans, Canadian missionaries demonstrated greater willingness to collaborate with figures such as Yi Dong-hui, a prominent nationalist whose appeal drew substantial audiences and who promoted Christianity as a vehicle for national renewal. Grierson and his colleagues recognized that Yi’s influence could accelerate mission growth, and indeed, Yi’s messaging temporarily increased church membership while strengthening the CPM’s position within Korean communities. Yi’s subsequent shift from Christianity to communism, combined with the persistent politicization of church spaces by radical Korean Christians in Manchuria, ultimately revealed the boundaries of CPM influence.

The March 1st Movement of 1919 crystallized these underlying tensions. Korean students, educators, church elders, and ministers affiliated with CPM stations—notably Hamheung (1898), Wonsan (1901), Seongjin (1901), and Yongjeong (1913)—assumed prominent roles in organizing demonstrations that directly contested Japanese colonial rule and subjected Christian institutions to severe repression. Churches faced attacks, believers endured arrest and torture, and at least one CPM church member in Hamheung died while imprisoned.

Canadian missionaries responded by protesting Japanese actions and occasionally providing sanctuary to Koreans within mission compounds, including their refusal to permit Japanese soldiers entry to the Yongjeong mission. However, their reluctance to endorse complete Korean independence generated increasing disenchantment among Korean Christians. While most missionaries condemned Japanese brutality, they refrained from supporting nationalist demands for liberation, favoring instead calls for imperial reform over revolutionary change.⁵This politically neutral position, compounded by Canada’s participation in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, significantly undermined the CPM’s moral standing among Korean believers. Duncan Macrae, among the mission’s most vocal critics of this policy, opposed the treaty and wrote in 1921 that “no man can defend the Anglo-Japanese Alliance from a moral standpoint… it is going to be a tremendous handicap to us Britishers in our mission work if renewed.”

Educational policies further intensified these strains. Before 1919, Canadian missionaries had established and administered schools—particularly for female students—using primarily home funding with minimal Korean participation. Korean Christians had initially accepted this arrangement, expecting that missionary support would advance both their spiritual development and social progress. The post-1919 context, however, marked by heightened cultural nationalism and growing skepticism toward foreign paternalism, prompted Koreans to seek greater authority over church and educational governance. Missionary retention of educational control, especially their hesitation to upgrade facilities or secure Japanese accreditation for their institutions, became a source of considerable tension.

While the most significant confrontations would emerge in the following decade, the period from 1910 to 1920 marked the beginning of a fundamental transformation: Korean Christians increasingly viewed the CPM not merely as a benefactor or protector, but as a foreign institution that inadequately supported Korean aspirations. This shift, grounded in the concrete experience of colonial violence and unfulfilled expectations, would eventually manifest in direct conflict and compel comprehensive reorganization of the mission structure.

Irish and Scottish Presbyterian Missions

Between 1910 and 1920, the Irish and Scottish Presbyterian Missions maintained their established presence in southern Manchuria, concentrating their efforts in cities such as Changchun, Liaoyang, and Mukden (Shenyang). Following the disruptions of the Boxer Uprising, these missions resumed their evangelistic and educational activities with measured persistence. Missionaries including Andrew Weir and James A. Greig adopted an approach that minimized dependence on foreign military protection while emphasizing engagement with local communities.

The Irish Presbyterian Mission (IPM), collaborating with the Scottish United Free Church Mission, prioritized the development of indigenous Christian communities during this period. Their methodology centered on principles of self-support, self-governance, and self-propagation—concepts that would later resonate with Three-Self ideologies. The missions established churches in urban areas while extending their reach into surrounding rural territories, where missionaries provided medical services, religious instruction, and basic education. In rural villages around Liaoyang, they developed Bible women’s ministries and organized preaching circuits that served both Han Chinese migrants and Korean Christian settlers who had crossed the Tumen River from Korea.

The missions invested significantly in training local converts as elders, teachers, and evangelists, encompassing both men and women in these leadership roles. Educational institutions were established for boys and girls, with particular attention given to girls’ education through Bible classes and vocational training programs. In Mukden, where Scottish and Irish missionary activities frequently intersected, the missions also supported medical facilities in cooperation with other Protestant organizations.

A notable characteristic of the Irish and Scottish Presbyterian presence was their relative political neutrality when compared to Catholic missions, which often operated under French or Russian protection. The Irish and Scottish missionaries sought to minimize political entanglements while navigating the increasingly complex political landscape of Manchuria under Japanese imperial expansion following 1905. This stance enabled them to maintain credibility among both Han Chinese and Korean Christians, many of whom regarded Christianity as a source of resistance and empowerment during periods of Japanese occupation and earlier Qing state repression.

Their influence within Korean Christian communities was particularly evident during the March 1st Movement of 1919, when Presbyterian networks—both foreign and indigenous—served as organizational and educational channels for protesters. While the Irish and Scottish missionaries refrained from taking explicit political positions, their institutions functioned as spaces for moral resistance and centers for independent intellectual development.

Despite increasing anti-Christian sentiment throughout Manchuria, the Irish and Scottish Presbyterian Missions sustained their commitment to ecclesial decentralization and educational empowerment, establishing foundations for a locally led Christian movement that would persist through the Republican period and beyond.

American Presbyterian Mission (APM)

Between 1910 and 1920, the American Presbyterian Mission (APM) in Manchuria entered a decisive phase of institutional consolidation, focusing on expanding education and indigenous church leadership amid political instability. A defining feature of the APM’s work during this decade was its development of formal schooling systems, especially in Yingkou (Newchwang) and Mukden (Shenyang). These schools not only functioned as centers of Christian instruction but were also key vehicles for Western-style secular education for Han Chinese and Korean children in the region.

By 1910, the Yingkou Boys’ Middle School had already become a central mission institution, enrolling approximately 150 students annually. The curriculum included Bible studies, Chinese literature, arithmetic, geography, and introductory science, taught in both Chinese and English. APM educators emphasized moral development alongside academic rigor, believing that schools were the most effective way to inculcate Christian ethics in young minds. This educational approach reflected the broader Presbyterian shift after 1900 toward institutional work and “top-down” conversion strategies, focusing on reaching those who could influence others rather than pursuing individual conversions alone. Girls’ education was also promoted, though on a more limited scale. In Mukden, an associated girls’ school supported by the mission offered literacy and homemaking skills and began integrating basic Bible education and medical training by the mid-1910s.

The APM also played a major role in teacher training and curriculum coordination across multiple mission schools. According to Manchuria and Our Mission, by 1914 the APM coordinated a network of over 12 primary schools in Liaoning province, enrolling more than 600 students, many of them from Korean migrant communities. These schools acted as feeder institutions for secondary-level theological education, and several graduates went on to enroll in the Shenyang Theological Seminary, which was overseen jointly by American and Chinese Presbyterian leaders. This educational infrastructure aligned with the broader Presbyterian emphasis on institutional development that characterized missionary strategy after the Boxer Uprising.

The 1911 Chinese Revolution had a profound effect on APM operations. Presbyterian missionaries in Manchuria, like their colleagues throughout China, interpreted the collapse of the Qing dynasty not as a threat, but as a providential opening for national and spiritual transformation. The revolution generated tremendous optimism among American Presbyterian missionaries, who viewed it as a miraculous breakthrough that would sweep aside major obstacles to Christian conversion. Missionaries like Rev. W. H. Lowrie and Rev. J. A. Alexander saw the revolutionary moment as an opportunity to promote self-governing church structures. Their annual reports emphasized the urgency of empowering local elders and preachers and reducing dependency on Western oversight. This push for ecclesiastical autonomy aligned closely with the Three-Self principles that distinguished American Presbyterian missions from Catholic efforts and strengthened the credibility of the church among Chinese Christians, many of whom were themselves sympathetic to republican nationalism.

Additionally, the Revolution initially weakened imperial bureaucratic resistance to Christian education. For a brief period, mission schools found fewer barriers to licensing and expansion, which allowed the APM to formalize teacher training and extend into new rural areas near Tieling and Fushun. Chinese church members increasingly took on administrative and teaching roles in these mission schools, preparing the ground for a more indigenous Protestant infrastructure by the 1920s. However, as political divisions and antagonisms emerged with greater frequency after 1913, and as Yuan Shih-k’ai’s authoritarian tendencies became apparent, the initial optimism began to fade, leading missionaries to adopt more cautious approaches.

The APM’s response to both the Revolution and growing Japanese influence in Manchuria was one of measured engagement and diplomatic neutrality. While avoiding direct political involvement, mission schools and churches offered safe educational spaces and informal support to communities disrupted by warlords and colonial policing. This approach reflected the broader Presbyterian strategy of maintaining stability and order while serving as mediators between competing authorities. In doing so, the American Presbyterian Mission helped position Christianity not merely as a foreign import, but as a culturally adaptable and nationally relevant moral framework, though the ultimate success of this endeavor remained dependent on Chinese agency and leadership rather than foreign missionary direction.

Russian Orthodox Institutions

The decade between 1910 and 1920 marked a critical period of transformation for Russian Orthodox institutions in Manchuria. Three interconnected ecclesiastical structures—the Russian Ecclesiastical Mission in Peking (PEM), the Harbin Orthodox Church network, and the emerging foundations of what would become the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia (ROCOR)—adapted to rapidly changing political circumstances while working to preserve Orthodox religious life in exile. This period established Harbin as a significant center for Russian Orthodox activity outside the boundaries of Soviet Russia.

The Russian Ecclesiastical Mission in Peking served as the primary ecclesiastical authority for Orthodox communities in Manchuria during the early part of this period. Although based in Beijing, the PEM had extended its jurisdiction to Harbin and other Manchurian cities to serve Russian settlers and Albazinians employed along the Chinese Eastern Railway. Throughout the early 1910s, the PEM assigned clergy to Harbin, oversaw church consecrations, and supervised pastoral care and religious education across the region. This established ecclesiastical framework provided institutional continuity as political upheaval began to affect the Russian Empire.

The Russian Revolution of 1917 fundamentally altered the position of the PEM’s Harbin operations. As religious persecution intensified under Bolshevik rule, the PEM’s Manchurian clergy and communities began operating with increasing autonomy from Moscow. Local Orthodox leaders gradually distanced themselves from the Moscow Patriarchate, which they viewed as compromised by communist control. During this period, the PEM-affiliated churches in Harbin became centers of opposition to Soviet religious policies, distributing anti-Bolshevik publications such as The Chinese Evangelist (Китайский Благовестник), which promoted Russian Orthodox identity while criticizing Soviet atheism. The mission’s continued commitment to Orthodox liturgical practices, public religious processions, and education for displaced youth represented efforts to maintain religious traditions in the face of political disruption.

Simultaneously, the Harbin Orthodox Church network experienced significant expansion between 1917 and 1920 as it responded to the arrival of tens of thousands of refugees from the Russian Civil War. Harbin’s primary churches—particularly the Church of St. Nicholas and the Church of the Annunciation—functioned as more than venues for worship; they became focal points for cultural preservation, religious instruction, and community organization. These churches organized major liturgical celebrations, provided catechetical education, and offered advanced religious training for prospective clergy. The institutions also supported Russian monastic communities that had relocated to Manchuria during the refugee crisis. These activities helped establish Harbin as a center of Orthodox religious life for the Russian exile community.

By 1919, the ecclesiastical developments in Harbin had begun to anticipate the formal establishment of ROCOR. Confronted with the disruption of church hierarchy in Soviet Russia, Orthodox leaders in Manchuria convened unofficial church councils, questioned the legitimacy of the Moscow Patriarchate under Bolshevik authority, and created independent diocesan administrative structures. These actions reflected a theological position that the Russian Orthodox Church within the USSR had become institutionally compromised and that an independent “Orthodox Church in exile” was necessary to preserve apostolic succession and traditional faith⁴. The Harbin Orthodox community, along with similar developments in Vladivostok and elsewhere in the Russian Far East, thus became among the first ecclesiastical bodies to operate autonomously in preparation for ROCOR’s formal establishment in Serbia in 1920.

The combined efforts of the PEM’s administrative framework, the Harbin churches’ community-building activities, and the emerging ROCOR movement’s institutional innovations enabled the preservation and adaptation of Russian Orthodox religious life in diaspora. These developments transformed Harbin from a regional missionary center into a significant religious institution, maintaining pre-revolutionary Orthodox traditions while adapting to the circumstances of exile and displacement.

Church of Christ in China (Synod of Manchuria)

Between 1910 and 1920, the Synod of Manchuria—which would later become a constituent body of the Church of Christ in China (CCC) in 1927—developed into an increasingly mature and indigenous Protestant institution. This transformation occurred through the collaborative efforts of Presbyterian missions, local Chinese congregational leaders, and expanding educational and medical networks. The synod’s growth took place against the backdrop of northeastern China’s turbulent political landscape, marked by the collapse of the Qing dynasty and the expanding influence of Japanese imperialism.

Rev. F. W. S. O’Neill of the Irish Presbyterian Mission emerged as a key figure during this formative period. Though his major writings would appear in the 1930s, O’Neill’s leadership in the 1910s significantly influenced the theological direction and administrative structure of Presbyterian work in Manchuria. He promoted adult Bible literacy initiatives, supported Chinese leadership development, and worked to establish consistent congregational governance across regions including Fakumen and Mukden.

Dr. Dugald Christie of the Scottish Presbyterian Mission made substantial contributions to both medical care and education. His establishment of Moukden Medical College in 1912 marked the creation of Manchuria’s first Western-style medical institution. The college represented Protestant commitment to social service by integrating clinical education with Christian principles, drawing students from both Chinese and Korean communities. Christie’s institution addressed critical healthcare needs while simultaneously serving as a center for evangelistic outreach and local leadership training.

These missionary initiatives depended heavily on partnerships with emerging Chinese Christian leaders, particularly elders and lay evangelists who organized preaching groups, established village stations, and supported local church workers financially. Although Western records provide limited documentation of these individuals, their contributions proved essential to the church’s expansion. In areas such as Fakumen, Chinese evangelists led revival meetings and extended church influence to previously unreached communities. By decade’s end, some districts had established as many as twelve new preaching stations, with Bible study, congregational singing, and public testimonies becoming standard features of rural Christian practice.

The Synod maintained an extensive educational network of elementary and secondary schools that combined biblical instruction with civic education. Despite increasing oversight from post-Qing and early Republican governments, mission schools retained considerable independence. Students studied classical Chinese literature, modern academic subjects, and Christian ethics. By the 1910s, several schools were preparing graduates for seminary education or leadership positions in village congregations. The Manchuria Christian College, initially operated by missionaries as a secondary institution, served as a training center for teachers and prospective pastors, many of whom returned to assume leadership roles in their home churches.

Medical outreach expanded beyond Moukden to include mission hospitals in Changchun, Liaoyang, and Kirin, which treated thousands of patients annually and helped reduce local suspicion of foreign missionaries by demonstrating practical concern for community welfare. Women’s clinics, staffed by missionary nurses and increasingly by trained Chinese Christian assistants, provided maternal care and hygiene education—services particularly welcomed by local populations. These medical facilities also functioned as evangelistic centers, incorporating daily prayers, Scripture readings, and Christian discussions into patient care routines.

The Church operated within a fragile and rapidly changing social environment. The 1911 revolution’s aftermath left Manchuria with a significant power vacuum, leading to widespread banditry, opium trafficking, and land disputes. While officially maintaining political neutrality, the Synod frequently provided informal community leadership through arbitration services, educational opportunities, and moral guidance. Missionaries were sometimes called upon to mediate village conflicts, and church buildings often served as community centers during periods of famine or civil unrest. Under these circumstances, the Church increasingly represented stability and moral authority.

Simultaneously, early expressions of Christian nationalism began to emerge. Korean Christian immigrants, many fleeing Japanese colonial rule, played significant roles in Manchurian church life. Their participation strengthened anti-Japanese sentiment within the church and contributed to the politicization of Christian identity. Korean pastors and laypeople connected their Christian faith with the Korean independence movement, particularly during the March 1st Movement of 1919, when Korean Christians in Manchuria participated in organized demonstrations and supported underground communication networks with activists in Korea. Although the Presbyterian synod maintained official caution, it generally tolerated these activities and occasionally provided implicit support.

Chinese Christians also began asserting their desire for greater autonomy in church governance. They increasingly challenged foreign control over financial decisions and policy-making, demanding more equitable representation in synod leadership. While these demands did not produce immediate structural changes during the 1910s, they established the foundation for the synod’s later integration into the broader national framework of the Church of Christ in China.

By 1920, the Synod of Manchuria had evolved into a spiritually active, socially engaged, and increasingly Chinese-directed institution. Its educational and medical programs had achieved regional recognition, its rural evangelistic network had expanded significantly, and its congregational foundation had strengthened considerably. Through both gradual and more pronounced developments, the synod had begun to forge a distinctive Christian identity grounded in faith, education, and national consciousness.

Canadian and Wesleyan Methodist medical missions

Between 1910 and 1920, Canadian and Wesleyan Methodist medical missions in Manchuria developed into distinctive forms of Protestant engagement that extended beyond traditional healing practices. These missions facilitated gender reform, cross-cultural outreach, and provided political refuge during periods of repression. Unlike conventional missionary schools or churches that operated within explicitly religious or nationalist frameworks, medical missions created hybrid institutional spaces that crossed class, gender, and political boundaries.

Dr. Florence J. Murray exemplified the Canadian medical mission effort. Dispatched to Hamheung and later the Kando region in 1918, Murray established women’s clinics and hospitals that provided Korean women with access to modern medical treatment and formal nursing training—opportunities previously unavailable to them. These institutions served dual purposes: delivering medical care while creating new models of Christian womanhood that combined medical professionalism with evangelical commitment. Despite initial cultural resistance and logistical challenges, Murray’s work gradually transformed both missionary medicine and women’s roles within the church.

Rev. W. Arthur Tatchell of the Wesleyan Methodist Mission outlined the strategic importance of medical missions in his 1923 work Medical Missions in China. Tatchell viewed hospitals as evangelical tools, arguing that physical healing reduced resistance to Christianity and created opportunities for spiritual dialogue in regions otherwise unreceptive to direct evangelization. He noted that hospitals and dispensaries treated patients regardless of gender or social background, thereby challenging established Confucian and colonial hierarchies. In Manchurian cities such as Mukden, this egalitarian approach enhanced Christianity’s moral and social standing compared to more doctrinaire or Western-aligned institutions.

Medical missions assumed particular political significance as covert refuges during periods of state repression. Following the March 1st Movement in 1919, hospitals affiliated with Canadian Presbyterian and Wesleyan Methodist networks provided sanctuary for Korean Christians facing Japanese surveillance. While mission doctors officially maintained neutrality, they frequently resisted Japanese demands to surrender political suspects or permit searches of mission premises. These hospitals thus functioned as protected spaces—shielded by international missionary status while remaining sympathetic to local grievances. Contemporary accounts indicate that Japanese authorities often avoided direct raids on these facilities to prevent diplomatic complications. This careful positioning enabled medical missions to offer both practical and symbolic sanctuary in ways that purely religious institutions could not.

Medical missions also fostered cross-ethnic engagement in Manchuria’s diverse population. While many Protestant missions concentrated on Korean migrants or Han Chinese converts, hospitals served patients from all ethnic groups, including Manchu, Mongol, and Russian border residents. Tatchell emphasized how this medical universalism helped Christianity transcend the ethnic divisions that complicated other missionary endeavors. The practice of foreign doctors treating all patients equally—regardless of class, ethnicity, or political affiliation—made a lasting impression on local communities.

By 1920, Canadian and Wesleyan Methodist medical missionaries had significantly altered the character of Christian engagement in Manchuria. They had established healing as a legitimate missionary method, validated female leadership in both Korean society and Christian service, and repositioned Christian institutions as sources of justice and dignity within the context of imperial rule.

Korean Christian Institutions

Between 1910 and 1920, Manchuria experienced significant changes in Korean Christian communities as Presbyterian and Methodist churches expanded amid evolving geopolitical and religious conditions. Following Japan’s annexation of Korea in 1910, substantial numbers of Korean migrants—many of them Christians—relocated to Jiandao and neighboring Manchurian regions. These migrants, seeking refuge from colonial oppression and economic difficulties, were determined to establish churches, maintain Korean identity, and create spiritual and educational institutions beyond Japanese control.

Within Presbyterian circles, the Pyongbuk Presbytery in Korea assumed an important early role by sending ordained Korean pastors such as Kim Jin-geun (김진근) to areas like West Jiandao. There, he and his colleagues founded congregations centered on Korean-language worship and indigenous leadership. While these churches remained nominally connected to the broader Northern Presbyterian mission structure, they functioned with considerable autonomy, particularly given the limited presence of Western missionaries in these remote frontier regions. By the mid-1910s, Korean pastors and elders had established stable church communities with independent schools and lay leadership development programs, effectively expanding Korean Christianity across the border without foreign supervision.

In the Methodist context, churches affiliated with the Methodist Episcopal Church, South (MECS)—an American mission organization—similarly developed in Manchuria during this period. Though initially overseen by foreign missionaries, these churches gradually integrated Korean lay leadership, especially after 1910 when local evangelists and educators from Korea joined their ranks. Leaders like Han Kyung-hee (한경희), despite his Presbyterian ordination, operated within a broader ecumenical context where denominational boundaries were often secondary to the common objective of establishing Korean-led congregations and schools. MECS-affiliated churches in North and East Jiandao frequently depended on Korean Bible women and local elders for evangelistic work and religious instruction.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Korean Christian Movement

The Korean Christian movement in Manchuria between 1910 and 1920 emerged from the intersection of refugee migration and anti-colonial sentiment. Churches functioned beyond their traditional religious role, becoming centers for cultural preservation and political organization. This development was particularly pronounced in regions such as Jiandao (간도) and West Jiandao (서간도), where Korean Christians established institutions that operated with considerable autonomy from both Japanese oversight and Western missionary control.

A distinguishing feature of this movement was its indigenous leadership structure. Unlike earlier missionary enterprises that depended heavily on Western funding and foreign clergy, many Manchurian churches during this period were founded and maintained by Korean pastors, elders, and lay members who had crossed the border with limited resources beyond their religious convictions. The case of Han Kyung-hee (한경희) exemplifies this confluence of religious mission and political resistance. In 1919, Han participated in leading the 3·12 Mansei Movement in Samwonpo, a declaration of Korean independence involving over 300 Christian students and church members that occurred months before news of the domestic March 1st Movement reached the region.

Korean Christian churches also functioned as educational institutions that promoted national consciousness. Operating beyond colonial control, these congregations developed independent curricula that included Korean language and history instruction alongside political education. Schools such as Eunyang School (은양학교), and subsequently Dongmyeong School (동명학교) and Samsung Middle School (삼성중학교), maintained explicit connections to church organizations and participated in efforts to educate a generation of politically aware Christian Koreans in exile.

The movement faced significant obstacles. Japanese authorities monitored cross-border activities closely, and Korean pastors engaged in educational or organizational work frequently faced arrest or were forced underground. Nevertheless, the relative autonomy available on the Manchurian frontier, combined with theological perspectives that connected Christianity with both spiritual and national awakening, provided Korean Christians with a distinctive platform for resistance and the development of a diasporic identity grounded in religious faith and political aspirations.

By 1920, the Christian movement in Manchuria had become an important component of the Korean independence struggle, offering both spiritual refuge and an ideological framework that linked religious salvation with national sovereignty. These scattered congregations and schools across the northern frontier would subsequently provide institutional and moral foundations for the broader Korean Christian nationalist movement of the 1920s and 1930s.

Key Theological Issues

The Protestant mission spent a great deal of energy on internal debate about its own governance structure, finally coming up with a constitution in 1912. The heart of this effort was to clarify the precise relations between the individual missions, the ownership of mission property (including compensation for losses incurred during the Boxer violence), and the decision-making process for questions that would concern the entire union. At the same time, the Manchurian Union was in close and frequent contact with the larger Protestant effort in China, with frequent delegation visits from other missions and participation in the 1908 synod in Shanghai. Topics discussed were often related to the educational and medical missions, rather than theology, as such.

They also began to increasingly push for the independence of the Chinese church, not only intellectually but also financially. At Union meetings in 1914-1915, it was decided to place greater emphasis on individual congregations becoming both independent and self-supporting.

Anti-Christian Leaders

I cannot see any clear anti-Christian movement during this period. More in the category of annoyances and ill omens. None had identifiable strategic or intellectual leaders, or if they did, these individuals are not mentioned in any sources.

Annoyances were related to the collapse of military order in the countryside (aka banditry), a constant drumbeat of natural and public health disasters, and the repatriation of European missionary workers to serve in the Great War. The 1910 plague took the lives of three Catholic and at least two Protestant missionaries/workers in medical missions. The deaths of Chinese Christian workers were not as well recorded but was certainly considerable, given especially that Chinese workers often based in more remote locations.

All of these factors made life difficult for the missions, particularly in their task of communicating with geographically far-flung communities, but none were the result of actions directed specifically against Christians or foreigners. Indeed, outbreaks of epidemic disease provided a chance for medical missions to show their worth and produced significant social good will.

Ill omens were the rise of nationalism, which carried an undercurrent of anti-foreign and anti-Christian sentiment. Akin to the hostility expressed by Qing officials in previous periods, this sentiment was a function of reformist nationalism, including the desire emphasize patriotic education in China’s reformed schools. While not yet at the level of open conflict, both missions describe an atmosphere of young Chinese reformists seeking such measures as demanding the removal of religious imagery from classrooms. Particularly given proximity to Russia, missionary writings occasionally compared these trends to the rise of Bolshevism. Particularly among the Catholics, Bolshevism would become their greatest fear and drive many of their subsequent alliances and actions, particularly their embrace of Japan.

But again, it is worth repeating that none of these movements were identified with a leading individual. Sources might occasionally mention “a student” giving a speech, but otherwise these reports were more samplings of sentiment among a certain segment of the educated, particularly those coming from the south as political appointees. (Anti-Christian sentiment from southern political appointees was also seen in the Qing period)

Anti-Christian Organizations

Apart from a concern that the post-revolution (but not yet Guomindang) government might potentially develop in an anti-foreign direction, there was nothing in organized form that would be regarded as anti-Christian. In the minds of missionaries (Catholic especially), Bolshevism was equally a threat from the newly-founded Soviet Union and from within certain political elements in China. Missionaries were on vigilant watch for either sort of infiltration, seeing foreign or politically nationalist agitation as at least part of the reason behind a growing desire for clerical independence and the full equality of Chinese priests within the foreign-dominated Catholic mission. However, the fear of Bolshevism was just that, a fear for the movement becoming a threat in the future. None of the missionary sources report any actual contact with any actual communist movement, foreign or domestic.
