Korea: Agency (1910-1920)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. 이승훈 李昇薰 Yi, Sung-hun

03/25/1864 ~ 05/09/1930

In 1910, Lee's formal profession of Christian faith at Sanjeonghyeon Church in Pyongyang, following his attendance at Rev. Han Seok-jin's sermon during the spiritual intensity of the Great Pyongyang Revival, marked a complete reorientation of his life's purpose. He restructured Osan School as a Christian educational institution founded upon the principles of reverence (敬), love (愛), and sincerity (誠), integrating daily worship, biblical study, and spiritual character development throughout all aspects of the curriculum.

This newfound commitment faced immediate and severe testing when Lee was arrested in 1911 during the Anak Incident and the 105-People Incident , a comprehensive suppression campaign conducted by Japanese colonial authorities against Korean nationalist activities. His detention, lasting nearly a decade in facilities including Daegu Prison and Mapo Prison (마포형무소), rather than diminishing his resolve, became what he termed his "school of faith." During this prolonged incarceration, Lee engaged in intensive biblical study, reading the New Testament more than one hundred times and the Old Testament more than twenty times, utilizing scripture both as spiritual sustenance and as a foundation for theological reflection on the interconnected nature of Christian sacrifice and national liberation.

Following his release in the mid-1910s, Lee resumed his educational mission with enhanced clarity and determination. He cultivated ongoing relationships with Pyongyang Theological Seminary, recruiting graduates and missionaries to join Osan School's faculty, thereby strengthening the institution's capacity to prepare Christian youth for both spiritual and political leadership roles in Korea's future. The school's established patterns of daily prayer, hymnal singing, and scriptural study embodied Lee's understanding that "education is the root of independence" transcended mere intellectual rhetoric to constitute a comprehensive program of spiritual formation and civic preparation1.

Lee's theological vision achieved its most significant public expression in 1919 when he joined the 33 national representatives who drafted and signed the Declaration of Independence during the March First Movement. Representing the Protestant Christian community, Lee worked to establish an unprecedented interfaith alliance incorporating Cheondogyo and Buddhist leadership, articulating his conviction that "a people without a nation cannot enter the Kingdom of Heaven." This declaration reflected his theological understanding that individual salvation and national liberation constituted inseparable imperatives. His prominent role in the demonstrations resulted in another period of imprisonment, while Japanese colonial authorities forcibly closed his cherished Osan School in an attempt to dismantle the institutional foundation of his educational and spiritual influence. However, this suppression served only to solidify his standing as one of modern Korea's most esteemed Christian leaders2.

Throughout the decade from 1910 to 1920, Lee's identity as a Christian steward and public theologian became definitively established. He demonstrated the capacity to endure suffering through scriptural faith, to integrate economic sacrifice with national purpose, and to prepare a generation of students who understood education not merely as a vehicle for personal advancement but as a sacred responsibility for Korea's liberation.

  1. 안창호 安昌浩 Ahn, Chang Ho

Following his forced exile in 1910, Ahn Chang-ho (안창호) fully shifted his energies to building an organized, global independence movement throughout the 1910s. No longer able to operate openly in Korea after the Shinminhoe's suppression during the 105-People Incident, Ahn redirected his vision toward uniting the scattered Korean diaspora and equipping them with the character, education, and organizational discipline he had long championed.

Upon resettling in the United States in 1911, Ahn founded the United Korean Association (공립협회, Gongnip Hyuphoe), which represented the first truly unified Korean organization in America. Recognizing the fractured state of early immigrant communities, he believed that only through systematic association and shared moral values could Koreans abroad meaningfully support the cause at home. The Association subsequently evolved into the Korean National Association (대한인국민회, Daehanin Gukminhoe, KNA), which became the largest and most influential Korean diaspora organization during this period. Through this institution, Ahn promoted Christian-based civic virtues, regular meetings, mutual aid, and publication of the Gongnip Sinbo (공립신보, Independence Newspaper), America's first Korean newspaper, which circulated news of the Korean situation globally3.

Equally transformative was Ahn's founding of the Heungsadan (흥사단, Young Korean Academy) in San Francisco in 1913. Deeply inspired by Christian fellowship and YMCA organizational structures, Heungsadan institutionalized his educational ideals into a formal network that trained young Koreans in honesty, cooperation, self-discipline, and national service. Modeled on catechism-style question-and-answer sessions and moral pledges, the organization framed its mission as both a spiritual and civic calling: to nurture modern, ethical leaders who would one day rebuild a sovereign Korean republic.

Ahn's vision during the 1910s demonstrated remarkable systematic, long-term planning. He proposed a five-stage roadmap that would first cultivate individual character, then establish vocational and academic networks, build an independent economic base, develop diplomatic and military readiness, and finally wage a coordinated independence struggle when conditions were ripe. Rather than focusing solely on direct confrontation, Ahn emphasized that sustainable liberation depended on morally prepared citizens and a resilient community abroad4.

This strategic approach became especially vital after World War I. With Wilsonian self-determination igniting hope among colonized peoples worldwide, Ahn mobilized the Korean National Association and Heungsadan to issue petitions, send envoys, and publicize Korea's plight in the United States and Europe. He stressed that Christian churches in immigrant communities should serve not only as places of worship but also as centers of education and fundraising for the national cause.

When the March 1st Independence Movement erupted in 1919, Ahn's decade-long groundwork bore fruit. The communication and funding network he had built helped channel vital material and moral support to the provisional government in Shanghai. Ahn himself played an important role as an advisor and acting Prime Minister in the early Korean Provisional Government, advocating for transparent governance and youth training despite deep factional divisions within the exile leadership.

By the close of the 1910s, Ahn Chang-ho's achievements had redefined what it meant to be a Korean patriot abroad. He had institutionalized a unique blend of Christian ethics, civic education, and democratic republicanism, demonstrating that the church and faith communities could be powerful forces for national awakening. His work laid the practical and ideological foundation for Korea's enduring independence movement well into the 1920s and beyond, establishing it not as a spontaneous uprising but as a deeply cultivated project rooted in moral regeneration, collective unity, and spiritual conviction.

  1. 조만식 曺晩植 Cho, Man-sik

02/01/1883 ~ 10/18/1950

Following his formative years at the Jeongchik English School and subsequently at Meiji Law College in Tokyo, Cho Man-sik remained in Japan throughout the 1910s, where he developed into both a Christian leader and nationalist intellectual. During his legal studies, he became deeply engaged with the Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA) in Tokyo and the Korean Student Association. His leadership proved instrumental in bridging regional and denominational divisions among Korean students, as he regularly organized joint worship services and facilitated student discussion circles focused on independence and social reform.

The formal annexation of Korea by Japan in 1910 transformed the Tokyo Christian Youth Association and the affiliated church into crucial venues where Cho and his contemporaries articulated their resistance to colonial rule. Many participants in these religious and intellectual gatherings later contributed to the 2.8 Declaration of Independence , issued by Korean students in Tokyo on February 8, 1919, which subsequently catalyzed the March 1st Movement in Korea. Although Cho did not directly sign the 2.8 Declaration, his peers widely recognized his role in cultivating the spirit of national self-reliance and unity that informed these activities.

After completing his legal studies at Meiji University in 1913, Cho returned to Korea and immediately assumed responsibilities at Osan School in Jeongju, Pyeonganbuk-do, an institution founded by Yi Seung-hoon. Yi Seung-hoon, a prominent independence activist, had been detained during the 105-Person Incident of 1911–12, when Japanese authorities conducted extensive arrests of nationalists suspected of plotting against the Governor-General. Cho stepped forward to maintain Osan School's operations despite intensive colonial surveillance and severe financial constraints. His educational approach encompassed not only conventional academic subjects but also Christian ethics and civic responsibility, establishing his reputation as an embodiment of his guiding principle of "self-sufficiency and non-violence".5

Cho's dedication to national independence reached a critical juncture in 1919 when he participated in the March 1st Independence Movement. He temporarily left his position as principal of Osan School to travel to Pyongyang, where he coordinated local demonstrations and student mobilization efforts. Japanese police arrested him while he was in hiding in Gangdong near Pyongyang, resulting in nearly a year of imprisonment at Pyongyang Prison. Upon his release, Cho resumed his educational responsibilities at Osan School while expanding his activities to include organizing local self-help cooperatives and Christian youth movements.

By 1921, Cho's influence had broadened significantly when he accepted the position of general secretary of the YMCA Youth Council and became an elder at Sanjeonghyeon Church, a congregation that had separated from his original spiritual home at Jangdaehyun Church.6 This period consolidated Cho Man-sik's standing as both a prominent figure in Korean nationalism and a devoted Christian educator who demonstrated that spiritual renewal must serve as the foundation for political and social liberation.

  1. 이승만 李承晚 Rhee, Syngman

Syngman Rhee's path to Christianity began during his studies at Pai Chai Hakdang, a school operated by American Methodist missionaries. Initially motivated by the prospect of stable employment, Rhee's education there exposed him to English language instruction, Western ideas, and Christian teachings. This educational experience also introduced him to democratic concepts that would influence his subsequent political activities.

While at Pai Chai, Rhee became involved with the Independence Club, where he advocated for reform and independence from corrupt Confucian rule and foreign domination¹. Following the dissolution of the Independence Association in 1899 and his implication in the "Park Yeong-hyo Coup Plot," Rhee was imprisoned at Hansung Prison and faced a death sentence.

During his imprisonment, Rhee returned to the Christian sermons he had heard at Pai Chai and turned to biblical texts for comfort and meaning, resulting in his conversion to Christianity. This period proved significant as Rhee evangelized among fellow prisoners, converting more than forty individuals, including several yangban elites. These prison experiences established the foundation for his later Christian nationalist perspective.

Rhee's theological and political ideas developed further in his writings for Sinhak Wolbo7, where he presented his vision for Korean church and national development. He argued that Christianity was essential for national salvation and civic renewal. In these articles, Rhee advocated for Korean churches to embrace "self-growth, self-sustainability, self-governance," extending the Nevius Method's three-self principles by adding unification under a shared spiritual identity. He believed that Christian enlightenment would enable Koreans to move beyond traditional constraints of law, rigid ceremony, and sin, achieving the freedom and equality he observed in the United States and Britain.

Rhee's prison writings demonstrated his connection between church and nation-building concepts. He viewed the church as more than a religious institution, describing it as the new stream of the Korean people—a vehicle for promoting civic responsibility, equality before God, and self-reliance. He envisioned Korean churches that would operate independently of foreign missionaries, sustained by Korean leadership and grounded in democratic values8.

His advocacy for self-sufficient, unified churches connected to his belief that Korea could become Asia's first Christian nation, capable of sending missionaries abroad and emulating the role of the United States and Britain as model Christian nations. Rhee's development illustrates how his Christian faith, formed during imprisonment, became intertwined with his political ideals—combining religious conviction, democratic principles, and nationalism that would characterize his later role as Korea's first president

  1. E. T. Schofield

(1867–1970

Schofield, a Canadian Methodist missionary, arrived in Korea in 1916 and served as a professor of anatomy at Severance Medical School while dedicating himself to medical mission work. His deep empathy with the Korean people's aspirations for independence compelled him to take a stand against Japanese oppression. In April 1919, immediately following the March 1st Movement, Scofield personally visited the site of the Jeamri Massacre, where Korean Christians had been locked inside their church, shot, and burned alive. Working alongside missionary Underwood, he inspected the victims' remains, documented the destruction within the church, and captured photographic evidence of the atrocity.

Drawing upon these findings, Scofield reported the brutal massacre to the international press, thereby exposing the Japanese military's actions to global scrutiny. His intervention was guided by his conviction that "there is no neutrality for brutality," a principle that came to define his moral position. Through these efforts, Scofield transcended his role as mere witness to become what would be remembered as a "missionary of empathy" who genuinely shared in the suffering of the Korean people. His documentation and testimony helped establish international awareness of the legitimacy of Korea's independence struggle while strengthening the moral resolve within the missionary community to oppose colonial violence9.

  1. William A. McCune

1870–1930

McCune, an American Presbyterian missionary who arrived in Korea in 1905 and concentrated his efforts primarily in Pyongyang and the northwestern provinces. McCune's work proved instrumental in building the church's foundation for the emerging nationalist movement. Throughout the 1910s, as Japanese authorities intensified their control through military police governance and orchestrated the fabricated "105-Man Incident" to suppress Christian leadership, McCune maintained his steadfast opposition to Japanese rule. From both the pulpit and the seminary lectern, he consistently proclaimed that "Koreans must believe in Jesus Christ as citizens of an independent nation," emphasizing the inseparable connection between national consciousness and Christian faith under colonial domination10.

Despite being labeled a prominent "anti-Japanese missionary" by Japanese authorities and subjected to constant surveillance, McCune refused to compromise his position. He continued to encourage Korean Christians to view their faith as a wellspring of strength for resisting oppression and preserving national dignity. His unwavering message provided the northwestern churches and seminaries with the spiritual rationale necessary to serve as strongholds during the March 1st Movement. McCune's contributions are thus remembered for establishing the spiritual foundation that enabled Korea's churches to function as active centers of the independence movement.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

  1. Songjuk Suicide Squad (松竹決死隊)

Following the National Debt Repayment Movement of 1907, organized anti-Japanese activities by Korean women encountered severe constraints. Nevertheless, a circle of Christian female leaders and students in Pyongyang established the foundation for underground resistance, with Sungui Girls' School (숭의여학교) emerging as a pivotal operational base. Around 1913, a group of teachers at this institution, most notably Hwang Aeduk (also known as Esther Hwang), Kim Kyung-hee, and Park Jung-sook, founded the Songjuk Suicide Squad (松竹決死隊), which represented one of Korea's earliest women-led clandestine independence organizations. The designation "Songjuk" ("Pine and Bamboo") embodied the virtues of unwavering resolve and moral integrity, while "Suicide Squad" reflected their commitment to sacrifice their lives for national liberation.

The school dormitory functioned as a clandestine center for cultivating national consciousness. Within this restricted environment, students and teachers conducted covert meetings, crafted national flags, shared intelligence about independence activists abroad, and coordinated local demonstrations. This Christian women's network merged spiritual discipline with militant patriotism during an era when Korean women were systematically excluded from meaningful public or political participation.

Membership in the Songjuk organization operated under stringent secrecy protocols, requiring both sponsorship from an existing member and unanimous consent for admission. To maintain operational effectiveness and broaden their influence, the society adopted a hierarchical structure dividing members into specialized subgroups. The more experienced members constituted the "Song Brothers," while younger students formed the "Juk Brothers," with each group assuming responsibility for outreach and establishing cells across different provinces. Certain members attempted to establish connections with Korean diaspora communities, though documentation of sustained overseas branches remains inconclusive.

These women demonstrated remarkable self-reliance in their fundraising efforts. Students manufactured and sold handicrafts, performed various tasks such as selling rice cakes at local markets, and allocated portions of their stipends to support the independence cause. These accumulated funds were transmitted to sustain provisional government activities in Shanghai and other exile communities, ensuring a continuous flow of resources when open contributions were impossible under Japanese surveillance11.

Their activities extended beyond financial support. Leaders such as Kim Maria, a devoted Presbyterian and prominent activist associated with the Songjuk circle, clandestinely transported the independence declaration from Tokyo to Korea, concealing it within her kimono sash. She and Hwang Aeduk traveled throughout cities including Busan, Daegu, and Gwangju to disseminate information about the forthcoming independence demonstrations, mobilize local Christian leadership, and encourage participation in the 1919 March First Movement. In Pyongyang particularly, the organized women's demonstrations directly resulted from the Songjuk network's preparatory work.

Despite the arrest of many core leaders and deaths from harsh conditions and illness, the principles of the Songjuk organization endured. Its ideals persisted through successor organizations such as the Gyeolbaekhoe (결백회, "Purity Society"), which maintained the same commitment to moral discipline, economic independence, and national self-reliance. These women advocated domestic reforms including temperance campaigns and boycotts of Japanese goods while discretely accumulating new resources for independence activitie12s.

The Songjuk Suicide Squad distinguished itself not merely through acts of resistance but as an early manifestation of a self-organized, exclusively female underground network rooted in Christian faith and modern education. Its legacy demonstrates that despite operating within the deeply patriarchal Confucian society of early twentieth-century Korea, these women established themselves as active agents of national liberation, connecting local resistance to global independence movements through education, faith, and clandestine solidarity.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

March 1st Movement

From the early years of the 20th century, Korean Christianity had become deeply intertwined with the nation's struggle for independence. When the March 1st Independence Movement erupted in 1919, both Methodist and Presbyterian churches had established themselves not merely as religious communities but as critical hubs of education, social awareness, and national consciousness.

The composition of the movement's leadership reveals Christianity's central role. Among the 33 signatories of the Declaration of Independence, 16 were Christians, including 9 Methodists and 7 Presbyterians, making Christianity the single largest group within the leadership circle. When examining the broader list of 48 national representatives, an additional 4 Presbyterians were previously overlooked in public memory, indicating that Protestant involvement was substantially higher than commonly recognized13.

The churches' influence extended well beyond symbolic representation through their educational networks. By 1910, Presbyterians had established over 500 schools nationwide, while Methodists had founded more than 150 schools. These mission schools served as incubators for Korea's modern educated class, with many students and teachers organizing protests while using classrooms and chapels as spaces for reading and reproducing the Declaration. This institutional framework enabled the independence movement's rapid spread from Seoul to the provinces14.

Women's participation represents another significant dimension of Christianity's contribution to the movement. Christian girls' schools including Ewha Girls' High School, Baejae, Sookmyung, and Jinmyeong became centers of student activism. At Ewha alone, more than 200 students participated in organizing protests, while female students in provincial schools risked expulsion and imprisonment to join demonstrations. Presbyterian churches maintained detailed records of punishments inflicted on their female students and church women, demonstrating that women's participation constituted a fundamental component of the resistance rather than incidental involvement.

Churches also functioned as spiritual centers of national awakening. The Methodist Sangdong Church and its youth societies had participated in earlier protests against the Eulsa Treaty, including attempts at collective appeals to the throne, leading to the emergence of the "Sangdong faction," an influential circle of Christian nationalists. These networks subsequently contributed to the larger Samil Movement, providing leadership, funding, and communication systems that surpassed other civil organizations of the period.

The statistical evidence underscores Christianity's substantial impact. Of the total schools established by foreign missions before 1910, approximately 70% were Presbyterian, and more than 90% of organized Christian student groups that mobilized in 1919 belonged to either Presbyterian or Methodist denominations. During the March 1st Movement, churches served as venues for secret planning meetings and prayer vigils, with Japanese colonial police frequently targeting pastors and Bible study leaders for arrest and torture as suspected organizers of local uprisings15.

The March 1st Independence Movement thus represented not merely an anti-colonial demonstration but a manifestation of spiritual conviction rooted in Christian faith. Churches, schools, pastors, missionaries, and thousands of lay believers formed an extensive network connecting urban centers with rural villages across demographic lines, transforming aspirations for national self-determination into concrete expressions of faith-motivated action. Christian principles emphasizing human dignity, divine governance, and the prophetic obligation to resist injustice positioned the Korean church as a decisive force in shaping Korea's modern historical trajectory.
