Korea: Agency (1940-1950)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Son, Yang Won 손양원 孫良源

    1. Birth/Death: 1902/07/07 (Gyeongsangnamdo Haman-gun) ~ 1950/09/28 (Geollanamdo Yeosu-si)

    2. Education: He was born between an elder father and a deacon mother. During his third year at Chilwon Elementary School, he was expelled for refusing to bow towards the palace of the Japanese Emperor in the 'Dongbangyobae 東方遙拜' (Eastward Distant Bow) ritual. With the help of Australian missionary Frederick J. L. Macrae, he was readmitted to school and graduated at the age of 15 in 1917. He was baptized by Macrae at the age of 17. In 1919, while attending Jungdong Middle School in Seoul, he was expelled again because his father, Son Jong-il, participated in the March 1st Movement. He then transferred to Changshin Middle School in Changwon, followed by studying abroad at Sogamo Middle School in Tokyo, Japan. During this time, he attended Tokyo Central Church, a Holiness church, and was inspired by Pastor Nakada Juji's street preaching and missionary work for leprosy patients. In 1923, he returned to Korea and met pastors Choi Sang-rim and Joo Ki-chul at the Kyungnam Bible Institute operated by the Australian Presbyterian Mission. After graduating, he worked as an evangelist at a leprosy facility in Busan with missionary Mackenzie. In 1938, he graduated from Pyongyang Theological Seminary.

    3. Contribution: Son Yang Won exemplified unwavering Christian conviction through his roles as a pastor, independence activist, and advocate for marginalized communities. His life was marked by principled resistance to religious compromise and extraordinary acts of forgiveness that earned him recognition as the "Atomic Bomb of Love."

During the Japanese occupation, Son Yang Won took a firm theological stance against mandatory Shinto shrine participation, viewing it as a violation of biblical commandments. This position put him at odds with colonial authorities and even some within his own denomination. When the Presbyterian Church faced mounting pressure in 1938, with most synods capitulating to government demands, Son Yang Won initiated grassroots opposition efforts. His resistance activities included educating his congregation about the theological conflicts between shrine participation and Christian doctrine. The colonial government responded by restricting his ministry and eventually transferring him to Aeyangwon Church in 1939.

His defiance culminated in arrest during a coordinated crackdown on Christian resisters in September 1940, when he was detained following a Wednesday evening service. Under Japanese rule, authorities changed his name to Dae Chon Yang Won and subjected him to severe interrogation. Court records indicate he was convicted for preaching messages deemed subversive to state authority, including teachings about divine sovereignty and prophetic interpretations that challenged Japanese imperial ideology. He endured imprisonment and torture until Korea's liberation in August 1945.

The post-liberation period brought new challenges as ideological conflicts erupted across Korea. During the Yeosu Incident of October 1948, communist insurgents targeted civilians, including Son Yang Won's two sons, who were teacher training students and Christian youth leaders. When confronted by revolutionary forces and ordered to renounce their faith, both young men refused and were executed. In a remarkable demonstration of Christian grace, Son Yang Won responded to this personal tragedy by expressing gratitude during his sons' funeral service and subsequently adopting the child of one of their executioners, raising the boy as his own family member.

Son Yang Won's compassionate service extended to Korea's most stigmatized population—those suffering from Hansen's disease (leprosy). At Aeyangwon, a medical facility established in 1909 by American missionary Robert Wilson, he worked to transform both treatment approaches and social attitudes toward the disease. Rather than maintaining professional distance, Son Yang Won chose to live in community with patients, sharing meals and daily life to demonstrate their inherent dignity. His ministry continued even when Japanese authorities seized the facility's assets in 1942 and imposed shrine worship requirements that drove away many patients who refused to compromise their beliefs.

When the Korean War erupted in 1950, Son Yang Won remained at Aeyangwon while others evacuated, determined to continue caring for vulnerable patients. North Korean forces arrested him for his pastoral role and Christian leadership. After enduring interrogation and torture, he was executed by firing squad on September 28, 1950, becoming a martyr for his faith and service to others.

  1. Chu, Ki-chol 주기철 朱基徹

  1. Birth/Death: 1897/11/25 (Gyeongsangnam-do Woongcheon-gun) ~ 1944/04/21 (Pyongyang)

  2. Education: As his name suggests, he was born into a devout Christian family, and he followed Christianity faithfully. He attended Osan School, a Christian school located in Pyeonganbuk-do during middle school, where he was baptized. In 1915, he enrolled in the Department of Economics at Chosun Christian University with the belief that he needed to create industries to help the destitute and hungry people. However, he had to discontinue his studies due to a vision problem. In 1922, he entered Chosun Presbyterian Theological Seminary and graduated while working as a missionary. Following this, he served at Busan Choryang Church and later took on a pivotal role in Korean Christianity at Pyongyang Sanjeonghyeon Church.

  3. Contribution: He was not only a Presbyterian pastor but also a leading figure in Korea’s anti-Japanese independence movement, remembered for his unwavering resistance to shrine worship and for giving his life as a martyr. Today, Chu Ki-chol stands among the most revered figures in Korean Christianity, alongside leaders like Son Yang-won.

Pastor Chu’s defiance of the compulsory shrine worship reveals how the majority of Korean church bodies under Japanese colonial rule complied with these demands, effectively aligning themselves with Japan’s efforts to stifle Korean Christianity. Pyongyang, often called the “Jerusalem of the East” for its role as the heart of Korean Christian revival, was home to Sanjeonghyeon Church — the congregation Chu served from 1936 onward. His outspoken opposition to shrine worship led to his arrest in July 1938. While he was in detention, the church council reversed course and formally permitted shrine worship on September 7, 1938. Despite constant police surveillance, Chu and the majority of his congregation at Sanjeonghyeon remained firmly against the practice.

Colonial authorities, working through the church council, repeatedly silenced Chu’s preaching and arrested him multiple times. In his absence, Charles F. Bernheisel, the missionary who had founded Sanjeonghyeon Church, stepped in to preach. Officials tried to pressure Bernheisel through compliant elders to deliver sermons that supported shrine worship, but he and most members steadfastly refused. By 1939, the council pressured Chu to resign, and by 1940, Sanjeonghyeon Church was forcibly shut down.

One letter from Bernheisel vividly describes the church’s fate:

“[Easter] On Sunday afternoon at 4 o’clock, an elder accompanied by a police officer came to Sanjeonghyeon Church, took the keys, locked all the doors, and posted a notice saying services were suspended due to ‘unfavorable circumstances.’ This was under the authority of the church council directed by the Chosun Governor-General. The police denied any responsibility for their actions. In reality, the police and the seven council members acting as their agents destroyed the congregation’s unity and left it in chaos. Yet the members refused to yield, declaring their commitment to truth and faith even if it meant closing their beautiful new church instead of compromising their loyalty to God.” (Park, 2016, p. 157)

The persecution extended to Chu’s family. While he and his wife were jailed, colonial police and the council forcibly evicted his mother and children from their home, leaving them to find shelter in barns.

By late 1940, broader policy changes signaled Japan’s tightening control. In October that year, the Methodist Church ruled that foreigners could no longer hold leadership roles; the Presbyterian Church followed suit in November (Park, p. 161). Missionaries in the Pyongyang Council likewise chose to withdraw. In 1941, the Bernheisel family concluded their 41-year mission in Korea and returned home. Meanwhile, Pastor Chu endured brutal torture in prison until his death on April 21, 1944, in Pyongyang. His final letter to his wife expressed his enduring love, his fears for the Korean church’s future, and his steadfast faith:

“My dear, it seems my body can hold out no longer. I regret that I could not care for you because of the burden the Lord entrusted to me. Please look after my mother for me. I will go to be with the Lord and pray for the Korean church. Tell the church this for me. Do not bury me in Wungcheon; lay me to rest on Dolbaksan in Pyongyang. When my mother passes, bury her beside me… What will happen to the Korean church in the days ahead? The hardships it faces will not end with shrine worship but will continue in new forms. The church must stand on the side of truth — who will shepherd the flock well? … I miss the Sanjeonghyeon congregation. May it become a church of truth… Use the life insurance I took out for Youngjin’s wedding, and when my mother dies, arrange her funeral.” (Park, 2016, p. 164)

Chu’s concern for the spiritual integrity of the Korean church then still resonates today. While Japanese authorities officially recorded his death as illness, testimonies from fellow prisoners suggest he was killed through forced drug injections, making his martyrdom all the more poignant.

  1. Ham, Tae-young 함태영 咸台永

  1. Birth and Death: 1896/03/05 (Hamgyeongbuk-do Musan-gun) ~ 1964/10/24 (Seoul)

  2. Education: It was described in the 1910-1920s.

  3. Contribution: Ham Tae-sik was a multifaceted figure: a judicial officer, politician, Presbyterian pastor, and committed participant in Korea’s independence movement. Though historians continue to debate his relationship with Syngman Rhee’s later authoritarian government, Ham is widely recognized for his efforts to reunite Korean Christians and rebuild churches that had been shattered by Japan’s forced assimilation policies during the early 1940s.

The years between 1945 and 1950 brought immense political upheaval to the Korean peninsula. Notably, the key figures vying to lead the new nation — Gu Kim, Kim Gyu-sik, and Syngman Rhee — were all Christians who envisioned founding the emerging republic on Christian ideals. Ham was an influential voice for this conservative Christian camp, dedicating himself to reviving Korea’s church life and fostering a renewed Christian culture.

In 1941, Ham stepped down from his pastoral duties at Yeondong Church in protest against the intensifying Japanization campaign. He withdrew to a rural village, where he remained in seclusion until Korea’s liberation in 1945. Once independence was achieved, he turned his attention to rebuilding the fragmented Protestant community and worked to promote church unity. However, the South Convention of the Joseon Council of Churches, where Ham was active, struggled to distinguish itself from the United Church of Japan and the Korean Presbyterian Church, which had been forcibly consolidated during the final years of Japanese rule. Ultimately, many denominations prioritized reconstructing their individual churches over creating a single unified body. Nevertheless, when the Presbyterian Church of Korea Southern Assembly was formed in June 1946, Ham was appointed its vice-president and played a key role in restoring the denomination’s presence across the South.

Christian reconstruction was not only a church matter but a political one as well. Among the three main post-liberation leaders, Syngman Rhee — who lacked a strong domestic power base — invested most heavily in supporting the revival of Christianity. Ham lent his backing to Rhee, convinced that the fledgling republic should rest on Christian moral foundations. Having led the Christian community in Gyeonggi Province, Ham was a crucial ally for Rhee, who himself had been a leader during the March 1st Movement and needed grassroots church networks to solidify his influence.

In December 1945, Ham joined forces with leaders from various faiths — including Catholics, Buddhists, Confucians, Cheondoists, and the Daesoonjinrihoe — to establish the Independent Central Council of Religion. This coalition called for immediate national independence and opposed foreign trusteeship. With the United Nations recognizing the legitimacy of the South Korean government in December 1948, the peninsula’s division became a settled reality, and a movement for tripartite Christian cooperation gained momentum. Shared faith among Rhee, Kim Gyu-sik, and Gu Kim made it possible for Christians to spearhead this effort, though it ultimately fell short of its goals. At the center of these negotiations stood Ham Tae-sik, who tried to bridge Catholic and Protestant divides to advance a common front.

Beyond his political activities, Ham also focused on the countryside, establishing a Rural Gospel School to help revive and spread the Christian faith in rural communities left impoverished by years of Japanese militarization and exploitation.

  1. Yoo, Jae-gi 유재기 劉載奇

  1. Birth and Death: 1905/05/19 (Gyeongsangbuk-do Yeongju-si) ~ 1949/07/14 (Seoul)

  2. Education: It was described in the 1930-1940s.

  3. Contribution: Following Korea's liberation in 1945, the nation faced severe agricultural challenges as approximately 80 percent of the population consisted of small-scale farmers struggling under the legacy of colonial land ownership structures and Japanese economic dominance in rural regions. The pressing need for agricultural reform centered on dismantling colonial property systems, removing Japanese administrative remnants, and restructuring land distribution to enable farmer self-sufficiency. Despite these reform efforts, post-liberation economic inflation and opposition from former colonial landowners perpetuated rural poverty.

The Christian community responded to liberation by launching various nation-building initiatives. In North Korea during early September 1945, pastors Yoon Ha-young and Han Kyung-chik established the Christian Social Democratic Party, which prioritized democratic governance and social transformation rooted in Christian values rather than conventional socialist ideology. Their activities faced significant obstacles when Soviet military authorities implemented martial law and detained party members.

South Korea witnessed Pastor Kim Jae-joon's establishment of the Sunlin Brotherhood, primarily comprising Christian students from Eunjin Middle School in Yongjeong, Gando, where Kim had previously taught. This organization focused on missionary work, healthcare, education, leadership development, rural assistance, and democratic advocacy grounded in Christian principles. Kim articulated his vision for Christian-based national development in his presentation "Christian Ideals for Nation Building: The Ultimate Ideal and Reality of State Formation," promoting governmental structures that protected religious freedom, worship rights, press liberty, assembly privileges, publication rights, and individual conscience. His national construction philosophy advocated for Christian social democracy that transcended both capitalist and communist extremes.

Concurrently, Bae Min-soo championed an anti-communist approach to nation-building, viewing the post-World War II period as a critical phase in the confrontation between democratic and communist ideologies. He positioned Christianity as the primary defensive force protecting democratic principles against communist influence. Right-wing political factions simplified the ideological debate surrounding national reconstruction into a binary struggle between democracy and communism, or freedom versus authoritarianism. Christian organizations strongly endorsed right-wing national construction philosophy by drawing parallels between the democracy-communism conflict and the Christianity-communism divide.

Yoo Jae-gi's nation-building efforts aligned closely with Pastor Kim Jae-joon's movement, drawing political inspiration from Christian social democratic theories while implementing economic initiatives through the Jesus Village movement. In 1946, he collaborated with Ham Tae-young to establish the Heungguk Brotherhood, designating rural areas as Jesus Villages for cultural development programs. They deployed evangelists and founded rural gospel schools to cultivate local leadership. Over fourteen sessions, the Rural Gospel School graduated 448 students and trained thousands of additional participants as Jesus Village Crusaders, ultimately contributing to the formation of approximately 300 cooperatives centered around village leaders who had completed the Rural Gospel School program.

Regarding the individual mentioned at the beginning, his post-liberation rural development work represented a continuation of earlier efforts despite previous controversies. The Governor-General of Korea had previously identified his Rural Research Society as a clandestine independence organization, leading to his arrest in July 1938. Following his 1941 release, he assumed leadership of the Gyeongbuk Presbytery within the Korean Presbyterian Church for National Mobilization, actively promoting Pacific War participation and Shinto shrine worship in support of Japanese policies. While he received national recognition in 1995 for rural enlightenment contributions, this honor was subsequently withdrawn after investigations revealed his pro-Japanese activities between 1940 and 1945. His renewed commitment to rural development following 1945 liberation demonstrates his continued dedication to agricultural improvement despite his controversial wartime record.

  1. Kim, Jae-joon 김재준 金在俊

  1. Birth and Death: 1901/09/26 (Hamgyeongbuk-do, Gyeongheung) ~ 1987/01/27 (Seoul)

  2. Education: He grew up learning the Four Books and Three Classics of Confucianism and Eastern classics from his Confucian scholar father and gained a reputation as a writer. In 1920, leaving behind a newly married family and a stable income from a financial cooperative secretary position, he moved to Seoul. In Seoul, he attended liberal arts, English, and current affairs courses at the YMCA. He also frequented various libraries in Seoul, where he delved into modern books and was introduced to Christianity.

In 1921, he was deeply moved and decided to convert to Christianity after attending a revival meeting held by Pastor Kim Ik-du at Seungdong Church in Seoul. Three years later, in 1924, he was baptized by Pastor Kim Young-goo and studied theology at Aoyama Theological School in Tokyo from 1926 to 1928, and then at Princeton and Westminster Theological Seminary in the United States from 1928 to 1932. After being ordained by the East Manchuria Presbytery in 1937, he served as a pastor. In 1940, due to the demands for Shinto shrine worship by the Governor-General of Korea, which led missionaries to voluntarily close the Pyeongyang Theological Seminary (now, Presbyterian University and Theological Seminary, Chongshin University), he founded the Chosun Theological School (now, Hanshin University) in Seoul. He also contributed to the formation of the Presbyterian Church in the Republic of Korea, a progressive denomination in South Korea, and founded the Kyungdong Church, one of the representative churches of the Presbyterian Church of Korea. However, conservative pastors famous in the church community for their doctrinal and political conservatism, such as Kim Jang-Hwan and Han Kyung-Chik, attacked Kim Jae-joon as a heretic.

  1. Contribution: In the aftermath of Korea's liberation, Kim Jae-joon developed a distinctive approach to national reconstruction centered on Christian principles that diverged significantly from the mainstream Protestant political engagement of the era. While the broader Korean church community aligned itself with the "Christian nation-building" agenda promoted by prominent figures such as Syngman Rhee, Kim Kyu-sik, and Kim Gu, thereby providing substantial backing for conservative politics and liberal democratic establishment, Kim Jae-joon pursued a non-partisan ideological path that treated contemporary political doctrines and frameworks as temporal constructs while advocating for national development rooted in spiritual and ethical foundations.

Kim Jae-joon's religiously grounded approach to national construction stood in marked opposition to Han Kyung-chik's methodology, which emphasized that democratic systems could only thrive within Christian cultural contexts and concentrated primarily on opposing communist ideology while promoting American-style liberal democratic principles.

During a lecture delivered on August 19, 1945, at the Chosun Theological School gathering, Kim Jae-joon presented his vision titled "Christian Ideals for Nation Building." He articulated that the supreme objective of national development should be manifesting God's realm within earthly existence. This divine kingdom, according to his interpretation, represented not a posthumous celestial destination but rather materialized when divine purpose permeated every dimension of human experience and spiritual influence governed all aspects of existence. Furthermore, this sacred domain extended beyond individual boundaries to encompass all humanity and the cosmos, creating an interconnected unity among global populations.

  1. Kim Jae-joon outlined a systematic progression for establishing the new state structure. Initially, a political committee would be established, followed by constitutional drafting for democratic governance, then convening a comprehensive national assembly to deliberate and ratify these foundational documents, thus creating the legislative framework. Subsequently, popular elections would determine the chief executive, who would then assemble a cabinet to complete governmental formation. Nevertheless, he acknowledged that this representative democratic nation-building process represented an unprecedented undertaking in Korean historical experience, requiring cautious step-by-step implementation given the population's limited political awareness.

Drawing from Reformed Church theological principles, Kim Jae-joon conceptualized the nation's territory as divine property under God's stewardship. He argued that divinely appointed authorities should function as caretakers responsible for God's people, with political activities directed toward establishing communal prosperity rather than policies favoring elite groups or minorities, ensuring benefits for all citizens.

Concerning the treatment of Japanese collaborators, Kim Jae-joon advocated for comprehensive pardons, contending that while these individuals' hearts supported independence efforts, external circumstances compelled their cooperation with Japanese authorities. He maintained that national reconstruction should involve universal participation, including forgiven former collaborators, with fundamental decisions determined through popular consensus. Constitutional frameworks, governmental structures, and leadership selection processes should all reflect collective will, requiring guaranteed civil liberties including press freedom, assembly rights, intellectual expression, and publication privileges, alongside equal opportunities for all citizens and majority rule following open discourse and deliberation. This approach would foster unity and collaboration between the nation collectively and individual citizens, ultimately creating governance embodying Lincoln's principle of government by, of, and for the people. Kim Jae-joon envisioned establishing a society honoring individual dignity and protecting life's sanctity.

The distinction between Kim Jae-joon and Han Kyung-chik centered on Kim's interpretation of divine kingdom realization "on earth as in heaven" as the national development ideal. He anticipated God appointing stewards to fulfill divine intentions, with these divine representatives serving as shepherds promoting collective wellbeing. These shepherds would exercise public authority according to divinely granted power, with justice realization as the fundamental law governing their authority.

  1. Han, Kyung-chik 한경직 韓景職

  1. Birth and Death: 1902/12/29 (Pyongannam-do, Pyongwon-gun) ~ 2000/04/19 (Seoul)

  2. Education: He enrolled in Jin Kwang Elementary School, established by missionary Samuel Moffett (Korean name: Mapo Samyeol), and graduated in 1916 when he married his spouse Kim Chan-bin. After graduating from Osan School, he majored in natural sciences at Soongsil University, where he also received teachings from Lee Seung-hoon and Cho Man-sik. This connection later led him to serve as the president of the Cho Man-sik Memorial Business Association.

Upon graduating from Soongsil University, he went to the United States and studied humanities at Emporia College and theology at Princeton Theological Seminary. However, he was diagnosed with tuberculosis upon graduating from Princeton Theological Seminary and underwent treatment for two and a half years. Later, he was invited by Cho Man-sik to teach English and Bible studies at Pyongyang Sungin Commercial School and concurrently lectured at Soongsil University. In 1933, he was called to serve as the minister of Sinuiju Second Church and received his ordination from the Uisun Presbytery in 1934. However, in 1942, the Japanese authorities forcibly removed him from his position as the chair of the church council.

  1. Contribution: Following Korea’s liberation, Han Kyung-Chik firmly believed that Christianity, once suppressed under Japanese rule, could now flourish openly. He held that while the state should not control religious life, faith could — and should — shape the nation’s politics in profound ways.

After stepping down from his pastoral post at Sinuiju Second Church, Han took up the directorship of Borinwon, an orphanage, and also helped organize a self-governing body to keep order in the transitional period before the Soviet military entered North Korea. This committee, which Han served as vice-chair alongside Yoon Ha-young, was chaired by Lee Yoo-phil, a seasoned independence activist who had previously worked in Shanghai. With the Soviet army poised to occupy the northern half of the peninsula while the Americans were to take the south, tensions were high in Sinuiju, the administrative hub of North Pyongan and a stronghold of Christianity where pastors often emerged as local leaders. In response to the Soviet-backed formation of the Communist Party, Han and other church leaders set up the Social Democratic Party. However, Soviet interference soon made this work unsustainable, forcing Han and Yoon Ha-young to relocate to the South later in 1945. In Seoul, Han joined Kim Jae-joon in teaching at the Chosun Sikha School and also served as an interpreter for the U.S. military.

Once settled in South Korea, Han devoted himself to two primary missions: supporting the resettlement of refugees who fled the North and advocating for a new nation built on ideological foundations that stood in stark contrast to the communist North. He acquired property in Seoul’s Jung-gu district that had previously housed Tenrikyo, a Japanese religious sect, and turned it into Bethany Church — an initial sanctuary for around 20 North Korean refugees. This congregation would grow into Youngnak Church, which by late 1946 counted over 1,400 members, including nearly 1,000 adults. Han’s ministry also expanded to include an orphanage and the founding or stewardship of numerous schools serving displaced Northerners, such as Youngnak High School, Daekwang High School, Soongsil Middle and High Schools, Sungui Girls’ Middle and High School, Boseong Girls’ Middle and High School, and Soongsil University. In his preaching, he urged these new southerners to see themselves as ‘sojourners,’ anchoring their dislocated identities in Christian faith.

Deeply concerned with South Korea’s future, Han saw the political and social turmoil of the immediate post-liberation years as a critical proving ground for the Korean people’s capacity to live as free citizens after decades of colonial rule. He frequently spoke of the chaos that came with newfound freedom: a society that disregarded laws, ignored civic responsibilities, and evaded taxes. In his sermons from this period, he drew parallels between Korea’s liberation and the Israelites’ exodus, describing the fledgling nation as wandering in a wilderness in need of spiritual direction.

In his notable sermon, “Christianity and Politics,” Han insisted that the Korean church must take an active role in shaping the new state. He traced historical church–state relationships from the Roman Empire under Constantine — where the church became subordinate to the state — to the modern American model of strict separation, the model on which Korean Protestantism was founded. Yet Han argued that Christians, as citizens of both their nation and the kingdom of heaven, had a duty to participate in political life. For him, the priority for South Korea in 1946 was to build a “fully independent state,” because without political freedom, he maintained, all other freedoms — including religious freedom — were vulnerable, as painfully demonstrated by the Korean church’s forced compliance with Shinto shrine worship under Japanese rule.

Han was deeply critical of materialist ideologies, especially communism, which he saw as fundamentally incompatible with Christian principles. He preached that while some leftist ideas, such as equality and class liberation, might appear to align with Christian ethics, the methods of orthodox communism clashed directly with the faith. In multiple sermons delivered between 1946 and 1947, he warned against the “materialist dictatorship state” and the ideological confusion he saw gripping South Korean society — where students, workers, and farmers were torn between competing visions of democracy, communism, theism, and atheism. Han argued that the church must lead a “thorough ideological movement” to root believers in a clear Christian worldview.

To counter communist influence, Han championed a form of Christian socialism or social democracy rooted in biblical justice, while also rejecting any kind of totalitarianism. He declared that the new Korean state must be democratic, with Christianity as its moral backbone. This message featured prominently in his second-anniversary sermon at Youngnak Church in December 1947.

Unlike Kim Jae-joon, Han Kyung-Chik did not directly link this vision of a democratic state to the coming of the kingdom of God. Instead, he emphasized that the church should provide the spiritual foundation for the nation’s civil institutions. Both leaders, however, shared the conviction that true justice should reflect God’s sovereignty and that civil authorities — as stewards appointed by God — were responsible for upholding freedom, punishing wrongdoing, and protecting human dignity. This view closely parallels the social ethics of the Swiss Reformer Ulrich Zwingli, as Im Hui-gook (2017) points out, though Zwingli relied heavily on cooperation with secular councils while Kim and Han primarily exercised their influence through preaching and teaching, consistent with American principles of church–state separation.

Drawing inspiration from the Israelites’ nation-building at Mount Sinai, Han urged his congregation to help lay the spiritual groundwork for South Korea’s future, which still lacked full independence despite liberation. Ultimately, the democratic republic that emerged in 1948 — complete with a constitution, National Assembly, president, and cabinet — reflected the vision for nation-building articulated by both Kim Jae-joon and Han Kyung-Chik.
