Korea: Context (2010-2020)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

South Korea

Between 2010 and 2020, South Korea experienced significant government interventions in religious affairs, reflecting broader constitutional tensions around church-state separation, religious freedom, tax equity, human rights, and national security. This decade saw both collaboration and conflict between religious communities and the state as various legal and administrative measures generated controversy and reshaped the relationship between religion and public policy.

The Four Major Rivers Project and Religious Environmental Opposition (2010-2012)

The Four Major Rivers Restoration Project, launched under President Lee Myung-bak's administration, became one of the first major policies to provoke organized religious opposition during this period. The government presented this large-scale infrastructure initiative as necessary for flood prevention and water security through river dredging, dam construction, and waterway restructuring. Religious leaders, particularly from Catholic and Protestant progressive organizations, mounted strong resistance to the project. The Catholic Bishops' Committee for Justice and Peace and the National Council of Churches in Korea issued public statements condemning the project as environmentally destructive. These religious voices grounded their opposition in Christian theological frameworks, specifically eco-theology and biblical creation stewardship, arguing that the government prioritized economic development over environmental protection and divine creation principles. The religious opposition represented an early example of how theological concerns could mobilize organized resistance to government policy, establishing a precedent for faith-based activism on environmental issues1.

Religious Leader Income Taxation Implementation (2013-2018)

The introduction of income taxation for religious leaders represented the most significant administrative reform affecting religious communities during this decade. Historically, South Korean pastors, monks, and priests had been exempt from income tax, a practice that faced increasing criticism as inequitable and constitutionally problematic. In 2015, following extended legislative debate, the National Assembly passed a revised Income Tax Act that classified religious leaders' income as taxable under either "other income" or "earned income" categories, with implementation beginning January 1, 2018. The government justified this reform under Article 38 of the Constitution, which establishes universal tax obligations for all citizens. Many Protestant leaders opposed the taxation policy, arguing it violated religious autonomy and breached the constitutional principle of church-state separation outlined in Article 20. To address these concerns, the government provided implementation flexibility, allowing religious figures to choose between two income reporting methods and exempting religious organizations from mandatory income withholding requirements. Despite these accommodations, opposition persisted, with some religious groups filing constitutional complaints challenging the policy2.

Comprehensive Anti-Discrimination Legislation Debates (2007-2020)

The most sustained and contentious policy debate of the decade centered on comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation, particularly provisions addressing sexual orientation and gender identity discrimination. The National Human Rights Commission of Korea began advocating for such legislation in 2006, but implementation faced significant religious opposition. In 2007, when the Ministry of Justice attempted to conduct public hearings on draft legislation including sexual orientation as a protected category, major conservative Christian organizations launched intensive opposition campaigns. This mobilization resulted in the removal of sexual orientation protections from the proposed legislation. The issue resurfaced in 2020 when the progressive Justice Party introduced new comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation in the National Assembly. The bill aimed to prohibit discrimination across education, employment, and public services based on various characteristics including race, religion, gender, and sexual orientation3. While the National Human Rights Commission supported the legislation, major Protestant denominations argued it would criminalize traditional Christian teaching on sexuality and restrict religious institutions' doctrinal autonomy. Legal scholars from Christian institutions characterized the legislation as potential "legislative overreach" that could compromise constitutional religious freedom guarantees4.

Conscientious Objection Recognition and Alternative Service Establishment (2018)

A significant development in church-state relations occurred with the 2018 Constitutional Court decision recognizing conscientious objection rights for military service based on religious convictions. South Korea's mandatory conscription policy, maintained due to ongoing tensions with North Korea, had historically resulted in prison sentences for religious minorities, particularly Jehovah's Witnesses, who refused military service based on pacifist theological beliefs. The Constitutional Court ruled that imprisoning conscientious objectors violated constitutional rights to freedom of conscience and religion. The Court ordered the government to establish alternative civilian service options, which were subsequently implemented. This decision marked a significant shift in state treatment of religious minorities, demonstrating increased institutional respect for individual religious convictions even when they conflicted with national security policies5.

The 2010-2020 period revealed the complex dynamics of implementing constitutional principles in a religiously diverse democracy. These policy developments demonstrated how religious freedom, church-state separation, and equal legal treatment, while clearly articulated in constitutional text, proved challenging to implement in practice. The decade's controversies forced both religious communities and government institutions to clarify their positions and justify their claims through legal and political processes. While these debates were often contentious, they represented genuine democratic engagement with competing visions of religious freedom and state authority. The period's legacy lies not in the complete resolution of church-state tensions, but in the development of more sophisticated mechanisms for managing such tensions in a pluralistic society. The outcomes varied: some issues were resolved through compromise, others through decisive policy implementation, and still others through creative solutions that balanced multiple legitimate interests. These experiences provide important precedents for South Korea's continuing evolution as a democracy, demonstrating that while religious freedom and secular governance can create tensions, they are not inherently incompatible when managed through strong institutions and principled leadership committed to both democratic values and human dignity.

North Korea

From 2010 to 2020, North Korea's policies and incidents related to Christianity represent a historical record of an era where the inextinguishable vitality of faith coexisted with thorough surveillance, oppression, systematic persecution, and human rights violations. During this period, North Korea was ranked as the world's worst persecutor of Christians by the international Christian missionary organization Open Doors for 20 consecutive years, treating Christian faith itself as an act of regime subversion6. According to North Korean authorities, Christianity was a tool of American imperialism's infiltration and a foreign element threatening the juche ideology and the Kim family's idolization system.

While North Korea's constitution nominally guarantees religious freedom, this remains merely a formal provision. In practice, only a minimal number of state-sponsored churches like Bongsu Church and Chilgol Church have been operated for propaganda purposes targeting foreigners. Religious activities among ordinary citizens are strictly prohibited, with severe punishments including forced labor, torture, and execution imposed upon detection. The Christian population within North Korea is estimated at approximately 400,000, with reports indicating that at least 50,000 to 70,000 of them are imprisoned in political prison camps7.

In the latter half of the 2010s, North Korea further intensified its ideological and cultural control. The "Anti-Reactionary Thought and Culture Rejection Law" enacted in 2020 particularly defined the influx of external ideologies and religious activities as "anti-state acts"8. This law treats Bible possession, hymn singing, contact with South Korean missionary organizations, and underground church gatherings as serious crimes, extending surveillance targets to include youth, women, and children. Consequently, in 2017, twelve residents in North Hamgyong Province were arrested on charges of contact with South Korean Christian missionary organizations and transferred to political prison camps. Reports also exist of a 2019 incident where an underground church on the outskirts of Pyongyang was discovered, resulting in the public execution of five individuals. Additionally, in cases of North Korean defectors, their Christian faith status was intensively investigated when captured and repatriated from China. For instance, international human rights reports document a 2022 case where a resident in South Hwanghae Province was sentenced to 15 years after a Bible was discovered. Testimonies also exist of forcibly repatriated defectors who confessed their Christian faith during interrogation being placed in solitary confinement or even summarily executed.

North Korea's persecution of Christianity functioned not merely as suppression of religious freedom, but as part of a comprehensive ideological control and surveillance governance strategy for regime maintenance. Christians were classified as "hostile class" in the social stratification system, facing extreme discrimination in school, workplace, and residential selection, with believing families becoming surveillance targets across generations9. Nevertheless, underground churches and faith communities did not completely disappear but continued to exist covertly, with some North Korean defector Christians bearing witness to the existence of faith communities within North Korea in the international community, keeping alive the spark of hope¹⁰.

While the international community and human rights organizations have continuously called for North Korea to guarantee religious freedom, North Korea has ignored or rejected these appeals as "hostile forces' interference in internal affairs." The U.S. Commission on International Religious Freedom (USCIRF) and the UN Commission of Inquiry on Human Rights in North Korea (COI) have designated North Korea's religious persecution as "crimes against humanity," which remains an important human rights issue under international law10.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

COVID-19 Pandemic and Shincheonji Church Stigmatization

The COVID-19 cluster infection outbreak at Daegu's Shincheonji Church in February 2020 sent shockwaves through Korean society. A 61-year-old female congregant continued attending church events even after developing symptoms, leading to a dramatic surge in confirmed cases from 31 to 6,767 within just one week. Of these cases, 63.5% were linked to Shincheonji members. This incident turned Shincheonji congregants into targets for social ostracism. Some restaurants and cafes posted signs reading "Shincheonji Members Prohibited," while online communities leaked personal information of church members and subjected them to violent threats. One office worker was fired solely for being a Shincheonji member, and cases of congregants being physically assaulted by their own family members were reported. The fallout extended beyond Shincheonji, with anti-Protestant sentiment spreading throughout society, resulting in approximately 400,000 Protestant believers leaving their churches during 202011.

Large-Scale Rally Against LGBTQ Rights

On October 27, 2024, 1.1 million Christians (according to on-site estimates) gathered at Seoul's Gwanghwamun Square and Yeouido to hold rallies opposing same-sex marriage legalization and anti-discrimination law enactment. The rally proceeded under the banners of "family protection" and "defending Christian faith," with its political overtones strengthened by supportive video messages from Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon12. Participants chanted slogans like "homosexuality is sin" and opposed the Seoul Queer Culture Festival's use of public spaces, forcing the festival to relocate. Rally organizers emphasized opposition to lowering the age requirement for gender reassignment surgery, claiming that "Western LGBTQ policies destroy families." Critics pointed out that this movement would likely lead to the spread of hatred and undermine the principle of separation between church and state.

Persecution of Christians in North Korea

The North Korean regime classifies Christians as members of the "hostile class," subjecting them to three-generation collective punishment. According to a 2024 report, cases have been confirmed of Christian families, including two-year-old children, receiving life sentences, with approximately 70,000 Christians imprisoned in labor camps. North Korean defector Kim testified that "if Christian faith is discovered in North Korea, immediate execution or the entire family being dragged to concentration camps follows." South Korean churches operate specialized educational facilities for defectors, such as the "School of Freedom," providing Bible education alongside social adaptation programs13. As of 2018, South Korean Christian organizations continue supporting underground churches by smuggling Bibles into North Korea and producing radio broadcasts for secret believer networks.

Internal Church Conflicts: The Case of Pastor Jun Kwang-hoon

In January 2025, following the issuance of an arrest warrant for President Yoon Seok-yul, conservative Christian supporters stormed the Seoul Western District Court, injuring five police officers and damaging windows. Pastor Jun Kwang-hoon, identified as being behind this incident, claimed it was a "righteous struggle against the court's injustice." However, major denominations including the Korea Christian Council declared their break with him, stating that "violence cannot be justified in the name of faith."14 This incident starkly revealed the ideological rift between conservative and progressive Christian forces.

Unification Efforts: 2024 Inter-denominational United Worship

On October 27, 2024, 2.1 million Christians (1.1 million on-site, 1 million online) participated in an inter-denominational united worship service in downtown Seoul under the theme "Social Purification through Repentance and Revival". While this event explicitly opposed expanding LGBTQ rights, it simultaneously included a declaration that "churches should become mediators of reconciliation rather than causes of social division." Speakers from the UK and Germany warned Korean churches that "Western LGBTQ policies have led to family breakdown." Worship participants adopted a statement promising expanded support for the socially vulnerable and maintaining political neutrality15.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

With Asian-Pacific Countries

In the shifting dynamics of the Korean Peninsula, North Korea remained the world's most brutal persecutor of Christians. Throughout this period, Open Doors, an international Christian monitoring organization, consistently ranked North Korea as the worst country for religious persecution. According to testimonies from North Korean defectors and reports, imprisonment in political prison camps for faith-related reasons was routine, and entire families faced punishment simply for possessing a Bible16. South Korean churches and missionary organizations continued their humanitarian aid to North Korean residents and covert missionary work—this wasn't mere relief efforts, but a response to the conscience and calling of believers.

Our diplomatic relationship with China was economically tight-knit, yet showed clear friction over religious freedom issues. When China revised its Religious Affairs Regulations in 2018, the state dramatically tightened control over religious activities. As a result, hundreds of Korean missionaries living in China were expelled, and local Christian communities faced severe pressure. Nevertheless, Korean churches maintained clandestine support and exchanges through networks with China's underground churches17. This was the practice of the belief that "the Gospel transcends borders"—a solidarity of faith communities that rose above inter-state conflicts.

Our diplomatic relations with Japan throughout the 2010s were a continuous series of conflicts over comfort women, forced labor, and Dokdo territorial disputes. These weren't merely political issues—they affected national sentiment and the practice of believers. Korean churches prayed for peace and reconciliation amid Korea-Japan tensions while adding their voice to demands for historical accountability. However, religious cooperation with Japanese churches notably contracted under government tensions, and civilian-level interfaith reconciliation efforts hit their limits18.

With Chistian-majority Countries

With the United States, we maintained our traditional alliance, which extended beyond military and economic ties into Christian networks. Korean-American evangelical churches collaborated in missions, education, and relief work, with theological exchanges flourishing especially among megachurches. Yet conflicts arose over social issues like abortion, homosexuality, and immigration policy. The solidarity between American evangelical circles and Korean conservative Christianity influenced each other not only theologically but politically as well19.

Our relationship with Australia was quiet but steady. The missionary alliance forged after the Korean War continued into the 2010s, with Australian churches actively participating in Korean missionary training, local welfare cooperation, and Asian missionary partnerships. Korea and Australia shared welfare models and missionary strategies, jointly grappling with the church's role in the post-Christian era20.

Patriotism and Nationalism

Within this diplomatic context, Korean society displayed new dimensions of patriotism and nationalism. Major events like the 2014 Sewol ferry tragedy, the 2016-2017 Park Geun-hye impeachment crisis, and the 2018 inter-Korean summit simultaneously revealed public anger and hope, frustration and solidarity. Conservative Christian rallies with Korean flags combined patriotism with anti-communism and anti-LGBTQ movements under the banner of "prayer for the nation," showing a completely different form of faith practice from the candlelight vigils in the squares21. Meanwhile, progressive Christian circles focused on human rights, justice, peaceful unification, and anti-discrimination issues, actively participating in candlelight rallies and social movements. They emphasized public theology as prophetic faith practice and led movements questioning the church's responsibility in society. The nationalism of this period wasn't a single current, but a field where multiple Christian ethics and patriotic discourses clashed within a fractured political landscape.
