Korea: Agency (2010-2020)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. 옥한흠 목사 玉漢欽 Han-heum Oak

12/05/1938 ~ 09/02/2010

Oak Hanheum (1938-2010) is evaluated as a unique figure who holds the position of father of the discipleship training movement in Korean Protestant church history. His life and ministry demonstrate the character of a theological practitioner who transcended the dimension of a simple pastor and transformed the entire paradigm of the Korean church. This examination seeks to evaluate his legacy by presenting objective facts and evangelical interpretations in a balanced manner.

Oak Hanheum 's discipleship training movement was built upon a solid theological foundation, not merely pastoral methodology. He was selected as a government scholarship recipient in 1975 and completed his master's program in practical theology at Calvin Theological Seminary in Michigan, then pursued his doctoral program in pastoral ministry at Westminster Theological Seminary1. Particularly, through Hans Küng's "The Church" discovered at the Westminster Theological Seminary bookstore, he obtained theological answers to discipleship training, recalling this as "a moment of rebirth as a seminary student"2. His theological background was most influenced by Reformed Protestant leaders such as John Calvin and Martyn Lloyd-Jones, and he also formed his pastoral philosophy through Catholic Hans Küng as well as Anglican John Stott, Daecheon-deok, and Baptist Billy Graham. This broad theological background gave his discipleship training the character of an ecclesiological reform movement that transcended simple growth-ism or methodology.

Oak Hanheum pioneered Sarang Church in 1978 and served as senior pastor until 2003, introducing the "Awakening the Laity" discipleship training and spreading the discipleship training movement to churches both domestically and internationally3. His discipleship training was inspired by the Navigator mission's training methods, but with help from Bang Seongi, who received discipleship training at the Navigator mission, he began discipleship training at Seongdo Church and expanded the college ministry from 12 to 350 people over two years4. Particularly noteworthy is that his discipleship training clearly recognized the relationship between ecclesiology, especially the Holy Spirit and apostolicity, arguing that biblical churches must be apostolic, and to be apostolic, the entire church including lay people must believe they are successors of the apostles. This was a fundamental challenge to the clergy-centered structure of the Korean church at that time.

The influence of Oak Hanheum 's discipleship training movement on the Korean church was extensive and lasting. In surveys asking seminary students about the most respected pastors, he ranked first, surpassing Pastor Han Kyung-jik and Pastor Cho Yonggi. This demonstrates the influence his character and pastoral philosophy had on the next generation of pastors. "Awakening the Laity" published in 1984 gained tremendous popularity due to people's interest in discipleship training and the remarkable growth rate demonstrated by Sarang Church. This book became a steady seller exceeding 100 editions and was translated and published in 11 languages including English, Chinese, and Japanese5. The spread of discipleship training is also confirmed in specific churches. As mentioned in the text, various churches such as Busan Hosanna Church, Suyeong-ro Church, and Bundang Woori Church were influenced by him and pursued healthy church growth and renewa6. Currently, his ministry and theology continue to be reexamined through the International Discipleship Training Center, CAL-NET (Called to Awaken the Laity Network), and others.

The theological evaluation of Oak Hanheum's discipleship training is complex. From a positive perspective, he was a practical Reformed theologian and a thorough ecclesiological reformer of this era, a faithful disciple of Christ and a true pastor who lived uprightly throughout his life, awakening the Korean church while aiming for a missional church centered on discipleship training ministry. However, critical perspectives also exist. Although Oak Hanheum's discipleship training clearly proclaimed the church's apostolic mission and the importance of lay people, it has been evaluated as being biased toward knowledge transmission and internal church activities, resulting in individualistic spirituality and biased community nature, missing discipleship appropriate to God's kingdom and not being completely integrated with holistic mission concepts7. Particularly, Korean church discipleship training has been criticized for being "biased toward knowledge transmission" as it directly reflects the modernist idea that the accumulation of knowledge by individuals with rational reason is essential for change, and overcoming the limitations of modernism becomes a very important task when considering the future of discipleship training.

Oak Hanheum's life as a pastor demonstrated a rare example in the Korean church. He received great evaluation for emphasizing frugality and simplicity despite being a large church, having no ethical and moral problems, and trying to raise each believer as a true disciple of Jesus. He made efforts to live exemplarily both in the church community and socially, including early retirement on April 18, 2003, five years before his term ended. However, he also showed human limitations. Like not a few Protestant pastors, he could not properly care for his family, and he himself confessed regretting being negligent toward his family while concentrating on ministry. This was the price of his dedication to discipleship training to the extent that he was called a "madman."

Oak Hanheum's discipleship training legacy continues to develop even today. At the 14th anniversary memorial service in 2024, the message was delivered that "we must make every effort in the ministry of building each person as a disciple of Jesus Christ even in the rapidly changing pastoral environment"8. This shows that his discipleship training spirit remains valid even in the age of artificial intelligence. However, there are several challenges for contemporary application. There is criticism that one of the causes of the decline in Korean church membership over the past 20-30 years is discipleship training, with criticism that it "drives people to despair"9. This demands reflection on the methodological rigidity and uniformity of discipleship training. From an evangelical perspective, Oak Hanheum's discipleship training was a legitimate movement based on biblical ecclesiology, and its limitations can be understood as arising from historical circumstances and methodological immaturity. What is important is that his core message that "the church is the body of Jesus, and each believer should become a member who builds God's kingdom" remains valid10.

  1. 이명박 李明博 Myung-bak Lee

12/19/1941 ~ Present

The years 2010–2020 under President Lee Myung-bak represented a continuous series of events and controversies that unfolded at the intersection of faith and politics. From overcoming childhood poverty to rising from president of Hyundai Engineering and Construction to the presidency, his life was filled with a success story centered on faith. His mother's dawn prayers and church life greatly influenced the formation of his worldview, and his long service as an elder at Seoul's Somang Church demonstrated his commitment to aligning faith with life. This background followed Lee Myung-bak throughout his presidential term and beyond.11

Lee Myung-bak often did not hide his faith in official settings. Particularly in 2004, during his tenure as Seoul mayor, he became the center of controversy when he declared at a youth and student prayer meeting at Jangchung Gymnasium that he would "dedicate Seoul to God."12 His belief that "Seoul is a city ruled by God, and its citizens are God's people" received support from Protestant circles, but voices of concern arose from other religious communities and civil society regarding the religious neutrality of public officials.13 He also revealed his religious inclinations by mentioning "God's will" in major policies and events such as the Cheonggyecheon restoration, and during his presidency, he even publicly invited pastors from Somang Church, which he attended, to the Blue House for worship services.14 While these actions drew active support from Protestant circles, they simultaneously resulted in heightened controversies over religious bias.15

The Lee Myung-bak administration appointed a large number of Protestants to key positions in the cabinet and Blue House. Ten out of sixteen ministers and sixteen out of forty-one Blue House secretaries were Protestant, creating the neologism "Protestant code appointments" and sparking social controversy.16 Particularly, the 2008 incident where police searched the vehicle of the chief administrator of Jogyesa Temple provoked strong backlash from the Buddhist community, triggering massive protests where over 10,000 monks and approximately 200,000 Buddhist followers gathered in front of Seoul City Hall.17 Buddhist dharma assemblies condemning the Lee Myung-bak government were held at temples nationwide, and the conflict deepened further when it was revealed that the Blue House had received reports on the movements of dharma assemblies at major temples.18

These incidents were not mere happenings. Lee Myung-bak's faith, intertwined with his political actions, created complex repercussions, and his religious character became a catalyst for discussions about the healthy boundaries between religion and politics in Korean society. These discussions continued even toward the end of his term and after his retirement, and Lee Myung-bak emphasized messages of faith-based self-reflection and social harmony.19 However, the evaluation of the legacy left by his faith and political actions remains an ongoing challenge in Korean society.20

Meanwhile, in diplomatic aspects, Lee Myung-bak's faith and policy decisions also had distinct influences. During his tenure, the Republic of Korea made efforts to elevate its status in the international community through achievements such as being selected to chair the G20 summit and expanding Official Development Assistance (ODA). These accomplishments strengthened the new identity of "from developing to developed nation" domestically and fostered national pride.21 However, expectations for elevated international status brought unexpected tensions in diplomatic friction with Japan. Particularly, Lee Myung-bak's 2012 visit to Takeshima (Dokdo) intensified conflicts with Japan surrounding the comfort women issue, subsequently marking the beginning of diplomatic communication breakdown between the two nations' leaders and the prolonged conflict over historical issues.

President Lee Myung-bak's story demonstrates how an individual's faith and political decisions can become catalysts for national and social conflicts. His actions represent a complex narrative that interweaves the social influence and political implications of faith, along with the resulting conflicts and possibilities for reconciliation—a series of symbolic events that distinctly reveal the influence of religion on society and politics in modern Korean history.

  1. 이태석 李泰錫John Lee Tae-seok

10/17/1962 ~ 01/14/2010

Father John Lee Tae-seok (1962–2010) was born in Busan, South Korea, and dedicated his life as both a doctor and Catholic priest. His life became widely known through his missionary work in the village of Tonj, South Sudan. From childhood, he was deeply moved by the story of Father Damien, who cared for leprosy patients in Belgium, and nurtured a dream to someday dedicate himself to the poor and marginalized. After graduating from medical school and building his career as a doctor, he joined the Salesians of Don Bosco, studied theology, and was ordained as a priest in 2001. In November of the same year, he was sent to Tonj, South Sudan, as a Salesian missionary, choosing this region—then suffering from civil war, poverty, and disease—as his mission field.

Upon arriving in Tonj, Father Lee began his life of dedication through medical missionary work. He personally laid bricks and applied cement to build a 12-room hospital, and traveled to Tonj and about 80 neighboring villages, treating an average of 200-300 patients daily. Through vaccination programs and mobile clinics, he fought against diseases prevalent in harsh conditions—leprosy, tuberculosis, malaria, measles, and cholera. For leprosy patients, he made shoes by hand and treated them without wearing gloves, while providing them with work opportunities by having them cultivate fields and catch fish from the river.

Father Lee's mission extended far beyond simple medical care. Believing that "if Jesus were here, he would have built a school before a church," he established schools and personally taught mathematics and music, dedicating himself to the holistic development of children. He transformed the church space with electric lights and the hospital waiting room into study areas so children could dream of a new world through education, where they could read and study even at night. He founded a brass band, putting musical instruments instead of guns into the hands of boys who were on the verge of being recruited as child soldiers, helping them heal from the wounds of war through music.

His cultural mission was driven by a desire to show children a new world. Father Lee showed the children of Tonj various movies and videotapes, helping them nurture dreams of becoming doctors, teachers, rural development leaders, and engineers. Through music and arts, he healed their inner wounds and guided them away from becoming accustomed to violent culture. As a result of this cultural mission, brass bands became symbols of peace and hope throughout South Sudan.

The village roads of Tonj were paved through his efforts, and solar-powered satellite TV and internet were introduced, greatly improving the living conditions of the village. His students studied medicine in Korea and returned to Tonj to continue medical service, and Father Lee's missionary activities were sustained through scholarship and support foundations. Despite being diagnosed with terminal colon cancer in 2008, he continued to worry about the children and patients of Tonj until the end, leaving behind the words "Don Bosco, Everything is Good" before passing away peacefully. His life and mission remain as a model of incarnational mission that went beyond simple medical and educational activities to embody love, dedication, and the practice of God's love22.

  1. 김진욱 선교사 Jin-wook Kim
    1972 ~ 2019

Missionary Kim Jin-wook (1972–2019) was sent by City Mission Church and conducted missionary work in the Diyarbakır region of southeastern Turkey. From 2016, he dedicated himself to sharing the Gospel with Syrian refugees and Kurdish people in Turkey, striving to become their friend. His ministry went beyond simple Gospel proclamation to building deep fellowship with local people, sharing hope and love in life with young people. Missionary Kim strictly kept his faith commitment. Under the conviction that "I will not go to sleep without sharing the Gospel with at least one person each day," he daily shared the Gospel and the hope of God's kingdom with local people.

On November 19, 2019, while Missionary Kim was out for evangelism, he was stabbed by a local 16-year-old boy and was transported to the hospital but ultimately martyred. This incident was strongly viewed not as a simple robbery but as a targeted attack against Christians in the area, and his death was recorded as the first martyrdom case in Korean Christianity during the 130-year history of Ganghwa Christianity. His ministry and dedication sent deep shockwaves through the Korean church community, leading to ongoing memorial services and missionary commitments honoring his life.

Missionary Kim took John 15:13-14 as the opening verse of his mission letters: "Greater love has no one than this: to lay down one's life for one's friends," and he lived out the love of the Gospel through his life23. After his martyrdom, Korean churches and local communities held a memorial talk concert at the Ganghwa Christian History Memorial Hall to honor his life and faith, and the Kim Jin-wook Scholarship program, scholarship student selection, martyrdom memorial projects, and prayer movements are actively ongoing. Particularly, the newly established 'Kim Jin-wook Scholarship' program at Ganghwa Deoksin High School aims to nurture Christian leaders while supporting future generations24.

Missionary Kim Jin-wook's martyrdom was not merely a sacrifice but a crystallization of love, Gospel, and dedication that deeply resonated with Korean churches and the global missionary community. The spirit of his dedicated life and faith continues today through prayer movements and literature campaigns, serving as an example of martyrdom faith for many people.

  1. 전광훈 全光焄 Kwang-hoon Jeon

05/08/1956 ~ Present

From the 2010s through the early 2020s, Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon emerged as one of the most controversial figures in Korean Protestant circles and the political arena. Based at Sarang Jeil Church, he had been carrying out pastoral ministry, but from the mid-2010s onward, he expanded his public activities beyond the boundaries of religious leadership to become a far-right political activist. His trajectory encompassed theological statements, rally speeches, media exposure, and even political party formation, garnering support from conservative constituencies while simultaneously triggering social conflict and denominational divisions25.

His most prominent political activities intensified after he assumed the position of representative chairman of the Korea Christian Council (Han-gi-chong) in 2019. He led massive anti-government rallies at Gwanghwamun, shouting slogans like "Moon Jae-in must step down," "Eradicate the Juche faction," and "Protect the Republic of Korea." Through political speeches and prayer meetings connected with the Liberty Korea Party, he emerged as a symbolic figure of the far-right Protestant camp26. During this process, Pastor Jeon attempted to lift the heretical designations of individuals already classified as heretics under the name of Han-gi-chong, or appointed them as co-chairmen, facing criticism as a "heresy defender" and drawing fierce opposition from the heresy countermeasure committees of major denominations27.

During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, Jeon defied government quarantine guidelines and proceeded with in-person worship services and rallies. This led to a massive cluster infection at Sarang Jeil Church, and his attitude of evading social responsibility while criticizing the government concentrated public criticism against him28. Media outlets including MBC, YTN, and CBS reported on Jeon's inflammatory remarks, torture-like congregational training, and sexually harassing expressions, raising questions about his moral character29. Since 2023, he has continued to consolidate his supporter base through 'Liberty Daily' and YouTube channels, but there's a clear phenomenon of alienation from mainstream society and the ecclesiastical establishment30.

  1. Korean Christian Federation (KCF)

KCF in the 2010s gradually lost its status as the representative united organization of Korean Protestantism, entering a path of rapid decline due to politicization, heretical issues, and internal corruption. During this period, the KCF became both a symbol of conservative religious circles and a harbinger of their downfall, profoundly impacting public trust in Korean churches and the direction of the unity movement.

In the early 2010s, the KCF still functioned as the largest united organization representing approximately 12 million believers. Its founding background was a conservative reaction against the progressive-leaning National Council of Churches in Korea (NCCK). When the NCCK emphasized North-South reconciliation and unification while criticizing anti-communist ideology, conservative religious leaders who felt threatened by this united around major denominations under the banner of defending national security and conservative church values, resulting in the establishment of the KCF57. However, contrary to its founding ideals, the KCF gradually became politicized by actively supporting specific regimes and ideologies, while internally developing an entrenched structure of vested interests.

The 2011 allegations of vote-buying involving Pastor Gil Ja-yeon exploded the KCF's internal crisis. This process revealed a complex web of fraudulent activities surrounding the representative chairmanship, decisions to lift heretical designations, and power monopolization by specific individuals. Major denominations including Presbyterian Church in Korea (Tonghap), Hapdong, Kosin, and Baekseok successively withdrew, and in 2012, the establishment of a separate united body, the Korea Church Coalition (KCC), effectively collapsed the KCF's representativeness. From this point, the KCF was reduced to an organization centered on minor denominations, with analyses suggesting that only about 3% of all Protestants remained58.

Amid this decline, the KCF walked an increasingly politicized path. When Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon of Sarang Jeil Church was elected as representative chairman in 2019, the KCF moved toward becoming a center of far-right political activities. He pushed through the lifting of heretical designations for Pastor Byeon Seung-woo, who had been defined as heretical, and appointed him as co-chairman of the KCF, thereby dismantling existing principles for dealing with heresy. Simultaneously, he declared solidarity with the Liberty Korea Party, expressed support for general elections, and issued a statement calling for President Moon Jae-in's resignation. Throughout this process, the KCF maintained an extreme anti-government stance, claiming that "Juche ideology believers had occupied the Blue House"59.

Under Jeon Kwang-hoon's leadership, the KCF directly violated government quarantine guidelines during the COVID-19 pandemic by forcing through Gwanghwamun rallies, resulting in cluster infections. This caused fatal damage to the credibility of not only the KCF but Korean churches as a whole. Pastor Jeon himself was infected, and some conservative YouTubers and KCF figures spread COVID-19 conspiracy theories, further undermining public trust.

Backlash arose from Korean churches broadly. Organizations such as the Christian Ethics Movement, the Church Coalition, and the Healthy Small Church Alliance officially declared disconnection by defining the KCF as "an organization that does not represent Korean churches." Major denominations had already withdrawn from the KCF or suspended activities, with even the Campus Crusade for Christ and Kihaseong departing60. The KCF had essentially fallen from being a truly representative united organization to becoming an activity space for some far-right political forces and heretical groups.

By the late 2010s, the KCF could no longer realize its founding ideals—the vision of Protestant unity and contribution to social public good. Blind support for conservative ideology, collusion with megachurches, acceptance of heretical figures, and power-centered operations undermined religious conscience and morality, and by blurring the boundaries between religion and politics, seriously damaged the church's public standing. Christian networks demanding church reform had been calling for the KCF's dissolution since 2011, defining it as "an organization that has completed its historical lifespan."61 The subsequent movement toward new united organizations like the Korea Church Total Federation was a response and purification flow made against this background.

In conclusion, the KCF from 2010-2020 became a symbolic case comprehensively demonstrating the politicization, corruption, and heretical problems of Korean conservative Protestantism, leaving massive repercussions of declining social trust in Korean churches as a whole and the reorganization of the unity movement.

  1. Korean Church Alliance (KCA)

During the 2010s, Korean Protestantism encountered a decisive turning point marked by the intersection of fragmentation and reorganization within its ecumenical movements. The Korea Christian Council (KCC), previously the largest conservative coalition, lost credibility within ecclesiastical circles due to controversies over heresy rehabilitations, money-driven elections, and political bias, resulting in the withdrawal of major denominations. Amid this crisis, the Korea Church Federation (KCF) was established in 2011, positioning itself as an alternative to the KCC. However, it remained a limited coalition centered on medium-sized denominations, leaving Korean Protestantism with a representational vacuum.

During this period of division, efforts emerged to form a new coalition centered on denominational leaders, culminating in the official launch of the Korea Church Alliance (KCA) in 2017. The participation of major denominations—including the Presbyterian Church in Korea (Hapdong and Tonghap), the Korean Methodist Church, and the Assemblies of God in Korea—in a single official coalition for the first time represented an unprecedented event in Korean church history62. The KCA sought to overcome the problems of previous ecumenical movements by emphasizing denomination-centered decision-making structures, transparent administrative operations, and political neutrality.

One of the KCA's most distinctive characteristics was its capacity for coordinated responses to social issues. Particularly in 2020, amid the COVID-19 pandemic, the KCA collaborated with individual denominations to establish quarantine guidelines and encouraged the transition to non-contact worship services, cooperating with government policies. However, when collective infections occurred in some churches and distrust toward churches escalated, the organization also issued statements criticizing government measures as political actions that unfairly scapegoated churches.

Another significant development during this period involved the infection spread originating from Shincheonji's Daegu church and Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon's political rallies. These incidents became catalysts for sweeping societal criticism of "Protestantism as a whole," prompting the KCA to reaffirm its position on churches' political neutrality and social responsibility. Prior to the 2020 general elections, the organization emphasized that "church-level support for specific candidates should be avoided" and issued official statements urging pastors to refrain from political remarks. Notably, in 2025, the KCA clearly delineated its boundaries with extremist political forces by declaring its principle of "supporting neither the far-right nor the far-left"63.

The KCA also devoted efforts to traditional church service activities, including social service, North Korea assistance, and disaster relief. During the 2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics and the 2024 Gangwon Winter Youth Olympics, the organization deployed volunteers and organized welcoming activities while conveying messages of Korean Peninsula peace and unification. Additionally, the KCA clarified Christian positions on major social issues such as anti-discrimination laws, abortion law revisions, and private school law amendments through policy proposals and critical statements based on biblical values64. Simultaneously, however, Korean churches were plagued by recurring scandals including the Myungsung Church succession controversy, clergy sexual crimes, and church financial corruption. Consequently, according to a public opinion survey released in 2020, only 31.8% of respondents indicated they "trust Korean churches." In this context, while the KCA focused on restoring church credibility and preventing next-generation departure, it failed to overcome structural limitations of overall membership decline and weakened social influence65.

In conclusion, during 2010–2020, the KCA endeavored to restore representational legitimacy through new denomination-centered ecumenical movements, emphasizing political neutrality and social responsibility while positioning itself as a unifying force for divided church coalitions. However, facing enormous challenges including rapidly changing social environments, recurring internal ethical problems, and plummeting trust in an increasingly secularized society, these efforts represented a turning point but failed to provide a complete solution.

  1. Catholic Committee for Justice and Peace (CCJP) of Korea

In 2010, the CCJP raised serious concerns about ecological destruction regarding the Four Major Rivers Project, which was being forcibly implemented under government leadership at the time and hosted the seminar "Christian Response to the Four Major Rivers Project"66. This was an attempt to concretize the Church's ecological theological position that went beyond simple policy opposition, arguing that humanity must transcend anthropocentric development paradigms. The following year in 2011, the CCJP officially issued a statement opposing the construction of the naval base in Gangjeong Village, Jeju, urging society to prioritize the values of peace and life67. The CCJP visited the Gangjeong Village site with residents and celebrated Mass, encompassing even forms of physical solidarity, delivering a theological message that national security logic should not overwhelm community survival rights. In 2013, they actively voiced concerns about the conflict surrounding the construction of transmission towers in Miryang. The conflict that arose during the process of erecting massive transmission towers on land that elderly residents considered their livelihood base and life space was not merely a regional dispute but an event that revealed the essential dilemma between development and survival, economy and dignity that Korean society as a whole faced. The CCJP expressed its position on this issue from a perspective centered on life and solidarity, accepting residents' suffering as the Church's pain through field Masses and messages of comfort.

Beyond these environmental and development-related issues, the CCJP devoted itself to activities for the rights of the socially vulnerable and those who suffer. Even after the Yongsan tragedy (2009), it continued to comfort bereaved families and served as a channel to convey their demands to society, leading efforts to protect workers' rights through solidarity Masses for laid-off Ssangyong Motors workers and visits to protest sites. Particularly during the Sewol ferry disaster in 2014, it participated in declarations calling for truth clarification and was remembered as a representative religious institution that stood in solidarity with victims' families. The CCJP continuously celebrated memorial services and commemorative Masses for the victims, demonstrating that the Church is a "community that suffers together and remembers."

The CCJP also raised its voice in the area of women's rights. In 2011, it showed solidarity with the 1000th Wednesday demonstration calling for resolution of the Japanese military comfort women issue, and after the announcement of the Korea-Japan government agreement on comfort women in 2016, it issued a strongly critical statement arguing that victim-centered resolution principles had been ignored. Simultaneously, the CCJP systematically conducted social doctrine education for consciousness growth within the Church community. It annually commemorated Social Doctrine Week by hosting various seminars, forums, and lectures, and through the "Social Doctrine School," it opened educational venues where laypeople, youth, and religious could reflect together on faith and social reality68. During election periods, it sent questionnaires to major political parties asking about their positions on policies related to life, peace, labor, environment, and social welfare that the Church considered important, and by publicizing the results, it guided believers to participate as voters with social responsibility.

At the regional level, each diocesan CCJP responded to local issues and developed independent activities. For example, the Daejeon Diocese established a Social Doctrine School, presided over Life and Peace Masses, participated in anti-nuclear movements and the establishment of forced labor statues, and strengthened youth CCJP activities by operating programs such as peace pilgrimages and youth social doctrine training.

Thus, from 2010 to 2020, the Catholic Committee for Justice and Peace consistently acted for solidarity with those who suffer, realization of social justice, and protection of human dignity, and these activities were not merely social movements but expressions of faith and a journey to realize the Church's prophetic mission. The CCJP's voice was the Church's conscience responding to the pain of the times, and its existence became a reference point that constantly made Korean society reflect on religion's public nature and social responsibility.

  1. Christian Ethics Movement of Korea (CEMK)

During this period, the Korean church faced serious social distrust due to internal issues such as church succession practices, financial opacity, and ethical lapses by some leaders, as well as external challenges including politicization controversies and the pandemic situation. Within this crisis context, CEMK played a central role in promoting church self-reflection and ethical reform.

One of the most representative activities was the "Public Trust Survey of Korean Churches." This survey, conducted regularly since 2008, continued in 2010, 2013, 2017, and 2020, quantitatively demonstrating the sustained decline in society's trust toward Protestant churches. According to the 2013 survey, Protestant churches had a trust rating of 19.4%, significantly lower than Buddhism (32.8%) or Catholicism (30.3%).69 This result evidenced that Christianity's image in Korean society was being perceived more in terms of hypocrisy, authoritarianism, and political bias rather than trustworthiness. Based on this sense of crisis, CEMK organized various practical campaigns. Under the banner of the "Good Society Movement," they launched initiatives including youth debt relief campaigns, credit counselor training, fair election movements, and ethical investment campaigns. Particularly, concrete practices such as debt counseling for young people, financial education, and loan conversion support demonstrated that CEMK was providing actual assistance beyond mere declarative movements. Following the Constitutional Court's ruling on abortion laws, they actively participated in subsequent legislation and social discussions, and through the "Education with Rest" campaign, they advocated for improved rest and educational environments for both teachers and students.

Communication with young people was also a major focus. Programs such as the "Self-Break Project," "Church Youth Project," and "Youth Prayer" were designed to bridge the gap between young people's sense of alienation within churches, their faith concerns, and their social responsibility consciousness. Creating a church culture where youth could take initiative and publishing regular prayer texts that combined social practice with faith represented experiments in capturing the voices of the younger generation that Korean churches had ignored throughout the 2010s.

CEMK also publicized social issues related to Christian ethics through "Right Values Seminars" and encouraged daily practices responding to environmental problems like fine dust and climate crisis through the "Voluntary Inconvenience Movement." Workshops on "Making Air Purifiers," campaigns for "Not Spreading Fake News," "Using Respectful Language," and "Caring for Emotional Laborers" were attempts to connect faith with ethical attitudes in daily life. These practices emerged from concerns about Korean churches retreating into realms of faith disconnected from ethics, presenting an alternative of ethical faith.

Critical efforts toward structural church problems and improvement initiatives were also strongly developed. Regarding anti-church succession movements, CEMK actively intervened through commentaries, forums, cultural festivals, press conferences, and petition drives. In the "Church Financial Health Movement," they continued education on comprehensive income tax for pastors and strengthening financial transparency. This series of activities indicated that their focus extended beyond merely criticizing phenomena to actually changing internal church systems.

When the COVID-19 pandemic struck Korean society in 2020, CEMK strongly criticized cluster infections at some churches that ignored quarantine rules and the politicized statements and actions of Christian leaders, issuing statements demanding the restoration of churches' public character and ethics. During this period, with cluster infections originating from Shincheonji and political rallies led by Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon, Korean churches found themselves at the center of social condemnation, and CEMK's 2020 opinion survey revealed the shocking result that four out of five citizens did not trust churches.70 In this crisis phase, CEMK presented a direction that Korean churches should communicate with society, engage in self-purification, and restore trust through ethical practice. Simultaneously, through activities discovering and recognizing churches working with local communities, and through solidarity with various civil society organizations, they contributed to realizing universal values such as human rights, environment, peace, and minority rights. These activities, linked with CEMK's online content such as "Good Tree," also contributed to forming public discourse connecting faith and life.

In summary, from 2010 to 2020, CEMK diagnosed the ethical condition of crisis-stricken Korean churches, presented alternatives through concrete practices, and performed an important mediating role for restoring trust between churches and society. Nevertheless, structural problems within churches and social distrust still remain deeply rooted, and the task of restoring ethics and public character that CEMK presented continues to be an ongoing challenge.

  1. Association of Gospel Attorneys & Lawyers (AGAL)

This was established in July 2020 to legally defend evangelical values, freedom of faith, and religious liberty. The founding conference was held at the Sheraton Palace Hotel in Seoul, with prominent Christian legal professionals including attorney Jeon Yong-tae, former National Intelligence Service Director Kim Seung-gyu, and former Constitutional Court Justices Ahn Chang-ho and Cho Dae-hyeon serving as co-representatives, publicly declaring the formation of a conservative Christian legal network71.

AGAL's core activities are concentrated on blocking the enactment of comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation (anti-discrimination law). The organization strongly opposes such legislation, arguing that it would directly conflict with Christian values by imposing state protection obligations regarding issues like homosexuality, gender transition, and abortion, while legally constraining the expression of faith and conscience72. In particular, they have focused on actively informing the general public and church communities about the content of such bills through various channels including "academic conferences to reveal the reality of anti-discrimination law," "production of legal reference materials," "distribution of church summary guides," "petition drives and statement releases."

As a legal organization, AGAL has also provided legal consultation and litigation support to churches and civic organizations. They offer legal services representing Christian positions in cases related to anti-discrimination law or gender issues, while building practical response capabilities. Representative examples include providing legal consultation and connecting attorneys when churches or pastors criticizing anti-discrimination law face legal action, and operating support systems backing constitutional appeals or administrative lawsuits73.

In the education and research sector, AGAL has operated continuous education programs for Christian legal professionals such as the "Understanding Anti-Discrimination Law Academy," while simultaneously attempting to systematize evangelical interpretations of constitutional, human rights, and freedom concepts. An important characteristic here is their presentation of an evangelical human rights perspective distinct from existing secular human rights discourse through concepts like "natural rights," "faith-based legal theory," and "Christian ethics and public order"74.

AGAL has also actively participated in legislative and political monitoring activities. They have worked to reflect the Christian community's position in the legislative and amendment process through analyzing provisions of anti-discrimination bills or basic human rights bills introduced in the National Assembly, delivering opinion papers to legislators' offices, and issuing statements. In 2022 and 2023, AGAL issued strong opposition statements regarding multiple anti-discrimination law-related bills, defining them as "anti-biblical attempts that shake the foundation of the rule of law"75.

In 2024, they criticized the Lausanne Seoul Congress declaration for remaining silent on anti-discrimination law, characterizing this as a betrayal of the evangelical position. AGAL expressed concern that this declaration could be exploited by pro-anti-discrimination law forces and declared their intention to continue the movement to block such legislation alongside evangelicals worldwide76. In October of the same year, AGAL publicly labeled the Christian Ethics Movement of Korea (CEMK) as "apostate forces" and demanded a retraction statement after CEMK took a favorable stance toward anti-discrimination law at a "Korean Church United Worship Service," triggering conflicts within the Korean church community77.

Through these activities, AGAL serves as a focal point in conservative church legislative response strategies based on a network of over 200 Christian legal professionals, law professors, and pastors, and is evaluated as an organization that has had practical influence on the repeated introduction and withdrawal process of anti-discrimination legislation. Furthermore, the controversies they have led have served as a catalyst for promoting extensive pro and con discourse about anti-discrimination law not only within churches but across the religious community, civil society, and political circles at large.

Organizations like the Methodist Women's Leadership Development Institute formed church sexual violence task force teams to create response manuals and expanded the "With You" movement for victims.

In 2019, groups like the Women's Committee of Bible Korea and the Women Ministers' Association of the Presbyterian Church in the Republic of Korea held theological seminars on "Scripture and Women's Liberation" and experimented with alternative churches and worship practices. This movement also expanded into new media, where young women theologians shared biblical interpretation, sermons, and church reform discussions with the public through YouTube and blogs, creating a new generational current82.

Throughout 2010-2020, women's Christian organizations maintained multilayered and practical movements including rallies, campaigns, institutional reforms, theological discussions, and victim support aimed at eradicating sexual violence within churches and achieving gender equality. Their activities posed the themes of "justice and equality" to Korean churches and society, driving waves of change.

  1. Multicultural and Immigrant Mission Movement

Entering the 2010s, Korean society gradually normalized the presence of multiculturalism and immigrants. As of 2019, the immigrant population had already exceeded 2.22 million, accounting for 4.3% of the total population, with immigrants of diverse backgrounds—including migrant workers, marriage migrant women, foreign students, Koryo-saram (ethnic Koreans from former Soviet states), and North Korean defectors—beginning to settle nationwide¹. Amid these changes, Korean churches and private missionary organizations began to recognize new missionary callings and launched various ministries for immigrants.

In the early 2010s, the 'Naseum Community' in Seoul's Gwangjin District and 'Global Village Love Sharing' in Guro District established themselves as representative private institutions for immigrant mission. Beyond simple gospel evangelization, they operated diverse welfare and educational programs including foreign worship services, free medical clinics, shelters, Korean language education, multicultural schools, Mongolian schools, and daycare centers. Global Village Love Sharing, in particular, which originated from migrant worker human rights movements in the 1990s, had expanded to such a scale by the 2010s that it transferred 52 facilities to nearby churches free of charge, broadening its ministry to include baby boxes, group homes, multicultural alternative schools, foreigner-exclusive worker clinics, and immigrant broadcasting stations83.

In provincial areas, the Daejeon Comprehensive Welfare Center for Foreign Immigrants attempted a social enterprise model by operating the multicultural restaurant 'I'm Asia' to create employment opportunities for immigrant women. Cheonan Sky Central Church was commissioned by the government to operate a Foreign Worker Support Center and Multicultural Family Support Center, working to improve immigrant settlement and welfare through counseling, education, free medical care, and cultural events. Ansan Onnuri Church established an 'M Center' in a foreign-concentrated area, providing worship services for 14 national communities, shelters, one-on-one discipleship, free medical care, and education. They implemented a missionary strategy of raising local ministry workers so immigrants could lead their own faith communities and be 'reverse-sent' to their home countries.

Mission to North Korean defectors (saeteomin) was also noteworthy. Seoul's Great Commission United Mission established social enterprises (box factories, curtain factories, cafes, etc.) to support defectors' self-reliance and settlement, operating talent development programs so they could grow as future North Korean church leaders. Conversely, Hari Church in Wanju, North Jeolla Province attempted immigrant women's mission in rural areas but discontinued due to lack of professional staff, financial difficulties, and overlap with local community welfare facilities.

In the late 2010s, existing missionary organizations that had depended on donations experienced COVID-19 and financial crises, acutely recognizing the need for self-reliance. Consequently, 'Business as Mission' emerged as an alternative. Representative examples include the following organizations:

  • Turtle Books: Developed art education content supplied to domestic and international kindergartens, international schools, and nursing homes, while operating art classes for multicultural family children. They pursued self-reliance by crossing boundaries between profit and non-profit sectors.

  • Café Binuvel: Thai returnee immigrants operated cafes and second-hand clothing outlets locally, simultaneously pursuing economic self-reliance and faith communities. This model demonstrates the combination of returnee immigrant 'reverse mission' and 'business mission.

  • Koryo-saram Village Cooperative: The Koryo-saram village in Gwangju Metropolitan City worked toward job creation, economic self-reliance, and restoration of Koryo-saram history and culture through a cooperative comprising about 30 stores with annual sales of 5 billion won.

  • Multicultural International School: An alternative school in Pocheon provided academic certification and Korean social adaptation education for multicultural children who entered Korea mid-education, playing a key role in multicultural social integration.

These organizations applied corporate management principles—differentiation strategies, cost advantage strategies, and focus strategies—to missionary fields, seeking sustainable mission through business model innovation84.

During this period, multicultural and immigrant mission created new paradigms of 'integration of welfare and gospel,' 'immigrant agency and reverse mission,' and 'self-reliance through business models.' However, structural limitations such as dependence on government support, inter-institutional competition, and difficulties in financial self-reliance still remain¹².

For Korean churches moving forward, systematic support at denominational levels, social solidarity, professional enhancement, and development of sustainable business models will become increasingly important.

  1. Me Too Movement and With You Theology

Since 2018, Korean churches have confronted a critical challenge: responding to sexual violence allegations and the church community's handling of these cases, particularly in conjunction with the broader #MeToo movement that swept across society. Women theologians and Christian women's movement organizations, in particular, began advocating for the necessity of 'WithYou (#WithYou)' theology that goes beyond simple victim statements to actively listen to and stand in solidarity with victims within the church. WithYou theology represented an alternative theological movement that demanded profound transformation extending from church structures and theological discourse to the very forms of ritual and worship, transcending mere victim support or pastoral care85.

Lee Eun-sun explores the possibilities of WithYou theology within the heritage of Korean traditional thought and theology. For instance, by reinterpreting Joseph in Matthew's Gospel as a "righteous person" who stands with the victim Mary, she argues that WithYou theology demands ethical participation where the community takes collective responsibility rather than remaining silent or evasive beside victims86. Furthermore, this WithYou attitude suggests a directional shift from 'hiding or concealing' women's sexuality and bodies to 'respecting and purifying' them. Lee Eun-sun views this theological transformation not merely as a matter of individual ethics but as a comprehensive project to reconstruct the entire paradigm of the church's ritual forms, worship structures, and biblical interpretation. She particularly draws attention to the "Hang-a-seol-wi (恒娥設位)" tradition advocated by Choi Si-hyeong (Haewol) of Donghak. This represented a method whereby women themselves performed rituals for their own sake, sanctifying their own bodies and histories, recognizing that the object of ritual was not the spirits of others but 'the life of myself and the universe breathing here and now'87. The spirit of this Hang-a-seol-wi tradition aligns with the central philosophy of WithYou theology, which calls for transforming the direction of sexual ethics and liturgy within the church toward victim-centered awareness and communal practice, while critiquing Western patriarchal theological order. Moreover, Lee Eun-sun emphasizes that WithYou theology should not remain confined to the dimensions of sexual violence reporting and solidarity but must expand into a theological enlightenment movement that simultaneously targets the structural problems of both 'prostitution' and 'sexual violence'88.

Given that deep-rooted misogyny and distorted understanding of sexuality in Korean society have penetrated even into religious institutions, she argues that comprehensive reform must accompany the reestablishment of all aspects of sexuality and the body, childbirth and care, education and communal life at the level of church ethics and institutions. In this regard, WithYou theology aims not merely to restore women's 'victimization' but to reorganize both church and society on the foundation of life ethics that respects human bodies, sexuality, and life itself.

Through this contemporary reinterpretation of traditional practices, Lee Eun-sun presents three directions that WithYou theology should pursue:

  1. Recovering the nobility of sexuality and introducing symbolic worship and ritual forms that purify bodily existence

  2. Restructuring theology and worship structures centered on victims and the vulnerable

  3. Practicing communal awareness and ethical action regarding the structural conditions that generate sexual violence

She advocates that WithYou theology should become an integrative resistance theology and practical theology that merges Korean traditional thought with Christian theology, encompassing everything from Pestalozzi's communal reform education to the Donghak tradition of self-ritual89.

  1. Increase of Cannan People

From the 2010s to the early 2020s, a new faith group called "Canaan People" rapidly increased within Korean Christianity. These are believers who believe in Jesus but do not attend church, maintaining their faith while distancing themselves from institutional churches. This phenomenon goes beyond simple church attendance decline and connects to structural changes facing Korean society and churches, generational value conflicts, and broader societal secularization trends.

Multiple factors contributed to the background of the Canaan Saints phenomenon. Since the 2000s, various irregularities within Korean churches have been exposed, including pastoral misconduct, church financial problems, and materialistic tendencies centered on megachurches, leading to a significant decline in trust. Many believers felt that churches had lost their original identity as spiritual communities and developed skepticism toward churches. The younger generation, in particular, began to reject hierarchical and exclusive church structures. Millennials pursued "autonomous faith" and showed a clear tendency to continue their faith life without belonging to a church. In fact, 44% of Christian youth in their twenties answered that they have "no church to attend," showing the spread of attitudes that prioritize personal spirituality90.

Social environment also accelerated this phenomenon. The 2015 Population and Housing Census showed that 56.1% identified as non-religious, demonstrating weakening religious affiliation. After 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the routine of in-person worship and normalized online worship and non-contact faith practices. This further weakened the meaning of church attendance and accelerated the individualization of faith.

In a 2023 survey, "COVID-19 impact" emerged as the second major cause. This phenomenon is actively studied in domestic and international academic journals. A 2019 study titled "Research on Effective Evangelism Strategies According to Church Departure Characteristics of Canaan Saints" defined Canaan Saints as a "Christian-non-religious boundary group" and emphasized the need for new evangelism strategies. A 2022 paper on "Millennial Generation Canaan Saints Phenomenon in Their 20s and 30s" analyzed how young people's pursuit of self-determination leads to church departure. Comparative studies with the UK pointed out that Korea's Canaan Saints phenomenon stems from "internal secularization" caused by the collapse of internal church authority, while the UK experiences "external secularization" due to social structural changes. English journals explain through the concept of "Believing Without Belonging" how the phenomenon of rejecting institutional religion while maintaining personal spirituality appears similarly to Korea92.

Thus, the increase in Canaan Saints represents not merely a decline in church attendance but a fundamental shift in religious practice methods. Going forward, churches will need new response strategies including relationship-centered small group ministry, building digital spirituality communities, and developing participatory worship programs targeting the 2030 generation. The prevailing forecast is that without structural reform of churches, this phenomenon will spread further.

Key Theological Issues

  1. Conservative-Progressive Clashes During the Park Geun-hye Impeachment Crisis

The impeachment crisis of President Park Geun-hye from 2016 to 2017 was far more than a mere political event. This incident served as a catalyst that intensified the ideological and theological divisions between conservatives and progressives that had been deeply embedded within Korean Protestantism, creating significant ripple effects on the Korean church's political role, faith identity, and social credibility.

Historical Background

Korean Protestantism developed under the unique historical conditions of colonial rule, war, and the Cold War. During the Japanese colonial period, it aligned with the nationalist movement, and after liberation, it was positioned as a force opposing communism, becoming closely tied to American-centered liberal democracy and anti-communism. Starting in the 1950s, pastors and believers who fled from the North shared a collective trauma regarding communism, which solidified into the formula of 'anti-communism = faith.' Consequently, conservative Christianity embraced nationalism, authoritarianism, anti-communism, and pro-Americanism as if they were sacred values. In contrast, progressive Christianity that emerged after the 1970s, influenced by Minjung theology and liberation theology, formed a movement that emphasized human liberation, justice, and peace93.

Park Geun-hye, Choi Soon-sil, and the Shock of Pseudo-Religion

In 2016, public outrage erupted when it was revealed that President Park Geun-hye had entrusted state affairs to Choi Soon-sil, a shadowy power broker, and operated through manipulated speeches, personnel decisions, and policies. What made the shock even more devastating was the presence of Choi Soon-sil's father, Choi Tae-min. Although he had once served as a pastor, he led a pseudo-religious movement that blended Christianity with shamanism and revelatory faith, wielding influence through his close relationship with Park Geun-hye. He claimed to be "one who receives God's revelations" and called Park Geun-hye his "spiritual daughter"94. When these facts became known, the majority of citizens experienced the collapse of religious trust beyond mere political accountability regarding the president, which soon led to massive candlelight vigils.

Candlelight Vigils and Taegeukgi Rallies

The candlelight vigils from late 2016 to early 2017, in which millions of citizens participated, represented peaceful civic action demanding the restoration of democracy and the elimination of corruption. Progressive Christian organizations and believers actively participated, holding 'square worship services' and 'prayer meetings for national affairs,' presenting new forms where politics and faith converged. They emphasized that civic action for democracy was itself the practice of faith95. However, in opposition to this, conservative Christian forces interpreted Park Geun-hye's impeachment as spiritual warfare and organized "Taegeukgi rallies." They came to Gwanghwamun carrying Korean flags, American flags, and crosses, shouting slogans like "Park Geun-hye is a leader established by God" and "The Moon Jae-in administration consists of communists." Most of these participants were elderly conservative Christians who rallied around ideological anxiety, North Korean nuclear threats, homosexuality issues, and anti-Islamic sentiment96.

Jeon Kwang-hoon, the Korea Christian Council, and the Rise of Far-Right Christianity

During this period, the Korea Christian Council (KCC) became radicalized toward far-right politics with the emergence of Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon. Jeon Kwang-hoon called the president a 'red' and demanded his resignation, proclaiming that "God will bring down Moon Jae-in" and "If Moon Jae-in becomes president, South Korea will be ruined." He equated the 'Kingdom of God' with 'liberal democracy,' spreading far-right Christian nationalist political theology. While his statements resonated with many Christians, multiple denominations classified his remarks as heretical or officially withdrew from the organization97.

Internal Church Reactions and the Deepening of Polarization

Internal church reactions were divided into three main streams. First, some churches actively participated in Jeon Kwang-hoon's movement and Taegeukgi rallies, regarding the protection of conservative government as a 'duty of faith.' Second, progressive churches participated in candlelight vigils, presenting the path of public theology that emphasized justice and repentance98. Third, the majority of churches remained silent or experienced internal divisions in the face of this extreme polarization, suffering from declining membership, youth exodus, and decreased credibility99.

This division went beyond mere differences in political views, stemming from fundamental theological principles including biblical interpretation, ecclesiology, views of the state, and eschatology.

Literalist Theology vs. Contextual Theology

Conservative Christianity has traditionally emphasized biblical inerrancy and literal interpretation, strongly supporting heresy vigilance and moral order. In the 2010s, conservative pastors strengthened their eschatological worldview, making "spiritual warfare" against homosexuality, Islam, and pro-North Korean ideology central sermon topics. Based on Genesis 2:24's principle of "one man and one woman," they regarded opposition to same-sex marriage as a faith obligation. In contrast, progressive Christianity embraced historical criticism and liberation theology, emphasizing contextual understanding of the Bible. They emphasized solidarity with marginalized neighbors such as sexual minorities, immigrants, female pastors, and irregular workers, redefining the church's role in society. This interpretation was rooted in texts that explicitly mention 'justice and mercy,' such as the Book of Amos or Matthew 25.

Political Messianism vs. Prophetic Faith

The conservative camp understood the Park Geun-hye administration as 'authority established by God' and interpreted the impeachment crisis as "Satan's work." Pastor Jeon Kwang-hoon and the Korea Christian Council, in particular, defined Taegeukgi rallies as battlegrounds of spiritual warfare, claiming that "the Moon Jae-in administration consists of communists and must be expelled." This rhetoric, combined with the eschatological view of the state found in Isaiah and Daniel, led to interpreting Korean politics as 'spiritual combat.'

Conversely, progressive churches followed the tradition of prophetic faith, emphasizing criticism of unjust power. During the 2016-2017 candlelight vigils, over 1,000 Christians issued the 'Christian Declaration for the Realization of Justice' and proclaimed the 'mission of salt and light' through street worship services and prayer meetings. They warned that the moment religion embraces power, the Gospel of Jesus becomes corrupted.

Anti-Communist Trauma vs. Peaceful Unification Theology

The conservative camp theologically internalized memories of the Korean War and anti-communist ideology. They interpreted North Korea as the 'Antichrist' and attacked progressive churches' unification movement as "pro-North Korean theology." Particularly during the 2018 inter-Korean summit, some denominations issued statements saying, "do not be deceived by deceptive peace shows."

The progressive camp, centered on Isaiah 2:4's symbol of peace to "beat swords into plowshares," regarded unification and reconciliation as theological missions. They argued that viewing division as "God's order" was itself a distortion of faith.

Clash of Ecclesiology

Conservative churches understood the church as a "holy realm separated from the world" and perceived external demands for change as infringement upon faith. They regarded resistance to quarantine guidelines, sexual minority rights demands, and gender sensitivity education as matters of faith protection. The Sarang Jeil Church cluster infection incident was interpreted as a consequence of this worldview. Progressive churches, however, understood the church as a "public community that responds to the world's suffering" and actively engaged in mask sharing, emergency relief, and care ministry during COVID-19. They believed that when the church abandons social responsibility, the public nature of the Gospel fades.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

In 2010s Korean society, the anti-Christian movement expanded beyond simple religious criticism into an organized and sustained socio-cultural movement. This phenomenon was triggered by the convergence of rapid internet proliferation, the popularity of anti-culture, and aggressive Protestant evangelism. The movement explosively spread through cyberspace using crude mockery and hate expressions such as 'gae-dok-gyo' (dog-Christianity), 'meok-sa' (eating pastor), and 'gae-jip' (dog house), representing a fundamentally different form of cultural resistance from past anti-Christian movements—one that was non-physical and language-centered100.

The roots of the anti-Christian movement can be traced to the 1920s socialist religious eradication theory and anti-Christian discourse. However, the modern movement centered around cyberspace rather than print media, with ordinary citizen netizens as the primary actors rather than party officials or intellectuals101. The 1999 Dangun statue vandalism incident served as a symbolic starting point, with Protestant exclusivity manifesting as destruction of traditional culture, sparking genuine social backlash. Subsequently, "carpet evangelism," street loudspeaker preaching, Buddha statue destruction, temple intrusions, and demands for worship in public institutions stimulated widespread social antipathy102. The 2007 Afghanistan hostage crisis further strengthened anti-Christian discourse. This incident, where a Protestant missionary team was kidnapped in a Muslim region resulting in fatalities, imprinted the dangers and irresponsibility of "aggressive overseas missions" across Korean society, providing the foundation for anti-Christian claims that "Christian evangelism is violence"103. During the same period, succession controversies, sexual scandals, and financial corruption that erupted in megachurches such as Yoido Full Gospel Church, Sarang Church, and Myeongseong Church played a decisive role in solidifying the image of Christianity as a corrupt power group104.

These criticisms spread through organized online communities. Representative sites such as 'Club Anti-Christianity,' 'Citizens' Movement Against Christianity (Ban-gi-ryun),' 'Anti-Bible,' and 'Anti-Jesus' boasted tens of thousands of members and conducted regular offline meetings, publishing, and legal petition campaigns. They criticized biblical violence, Jesus's historicity, and church authority structures, even filing petitions to designate the Bible as harmful literature105.

The ideological foundation of this movement rested on two main pillars. First, cultural nationalism regarded Christianity as a remnant of Western imperialism and defined it as a destroyer of traditional culture and ethnic identity. This was related to opposition to Dangun statue removal, criticism of temple vandalism, and movements to reevaluate shamanism and traditional thought. Second, secular humanism viewed Christianity as an anti-rational, exclusive, and undemocratic religion, criticizing it based on standards of human rights and rationality. They particularly opposed creation science, attacks on evolution, bans on New Age music, and mandatory chapel attendance, targeting churches as symbols of anti-intellectualism106.

The targets of such criticism can be generally categorized as follows: 1) Moral corruption of clergy and leadership (sexual scandals, embezzlement, succession),2) Exclusive behavior toward other religions and cultures (Dangun statue and Buddha statue vandalism), 3) Irrational responses to popular culture and science (creation science, music censorship), 4) Lack of consideration for the vulnerable (criticism of disabled people and sexual minorities), 5) Politicization and empowerment of churches (conservative political involvement), etc107. Protestant responses were divided between conservative and progressive camps. The conservative camp formed a frame of "Christian eradication forces," focusing on training 'internet missionaries,' legal responses, and organized counterattacks. Conversely, the progressive camp accepted this as a social warning and mirror of reflection regarding corruption and incompetence within Christianity, moving toward emphasizing moral responsibility and social participation. Pastor Jang Seok-hyeon interpreted this as "God's rebuke sent to the church," urging church self-reform108.

Thus, the anti-Christian movement of the 2010s was not simple Christian hatred, but a complex phenomenon where social structures and power, cultural identity, ethical issues, and rational beliefs intersected. This movement clearly revealed the crisis of trust and moral bankruptcy faced by Korean Protestantism, providing an opportunity that pressured churches to answer fundamental questions of ethical recovery, social responsibility, inclusive dialogue with other religions and traditional cultures, and rational faith recovery beyond simple 'counter-responses'109.
