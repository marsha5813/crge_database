Korea: Context (2000-2010)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

From the early 2000s, the necessity of taxation on religious personnel's income began to be raised in South Korea, but at the time, there were no explicit provisions regarding religious personnel's income in the Income Tax Act. The National Tax Service had maintained the practice of deferring taxation or leaving it to the autonomy of religious communities since announcing in 1992 that it would "leave religious personnel taxation to autonomy." In 2006, civic groups filed charges against the head of the National Tax Service for dereliction of duty, but the prosecution dismissed the case, stating that "not taxing as a matter of practice cannot be considered dereliction of duty," causing legislative discussions to lose momentum.1

The National Assembly proposed amendments to the Income Tax Act several times, including in 2006 and 2008, but failed to pass due to strong opposition from religious communities (particularly Protestant churches) and the passive attitude of the political establishment. The government and National Tax Service also delayed substantive measures under the pretext of emphasizing social consensus with religious communities. As a result, religious personnel taxation was not legislated or implemented until the mid-2010s, and actual enforcement only began through the 2015 Income Tax Act amendment and its 2018 implementation.

The controversy surrounding the enactment of anti-discrimination legislation was also a major issue of conflict between religious communities and the government. In 2007, when the Ministry of Justice announced legislative notice for an anti-discrimination law prohibiting discrimination based on gender, disability, age, sexual orientation, religion, and other factors, conservative Protestant communities strongly opposed it, raising concerns that anti-homosexuality sermons could be subject to punishment.2 Religious communities worried that if anti-discrimination laws were enacted, anti-homosexuality sermons could be punished as discrimination and the autonomy of religious institutions in hiring and operations could be infringed upon. Conversely, bill sponsors and human rights experts argued that anti-discrimination laws would not directly regulate doctrine, internal religious education, or freedom of worship and sermons, targeting only discrimination in public spheres (schools, social welfare, etc.).²'⁶ Constitutionally, specific religious education is prohibited in principle at national and public schools, and while religious education is permitted at private (religiously affiliated) schools according to their founding principles, there exist court precedents and educational office guidelines requiring the guarantee of students' religious freedom and right to choose. If anti-discrimination laws were enacted, acts by educational institutions such as religiously affiliated schools that force specific religions or create disadvantages based on religion would likely be considered discrimination.

From 2000 to 2010, the government pursued policies providing financial and administrative support to specific religious communities such as Buddhism and Catholicism under the pretext of culture, tourism, and traditional culture preservation. However, these policies were criticized as violations of the principle of separation of church and state (Article 20, Paragraph 2 of the Constitution), and Christian communities strongly opposed them, arguing that "using taxpayer money for specific religions is unjust."³ The 2010 Daegu Palgongsan Historical and Cultural Park development project, which was promoted with a Buddhist focus but was suspended due to strong protests from Christian communities, serves as a representative example.3

From the mid-2000s onward, incidents of pastoral ethical decline, including financial embezzlement by mega-churches and sexual ethics problems, occurred successively within Korean churches. These incidents significantly reduced the social credibility of churches and raised voices of self-reflection within church circles.4 The growth of emerging religions such as Shincheonji and heresy controversies shook Christian communities, and the division of united bodies due to heresy resolution controversies and money-based election problems within the Korea Christian Council caused social repercussions.

After the launch of the Participatory Government in 2002, the theological conservative-progressive divide within Korean churches began to combine with political conservative-progressive opposition. From the early 2000s, as political participation by conservative Christian communities, including the New Right movement, became active, churches actively raised their voices on political issues such as opposition to the Sunshine Policy and opposition to National Security Law amendments. Such political intervention deepened progressive-conservative conflicts within churches, resulting in member exodus and community division.

North Korea's Unification and Religious Policies in the 2000s

North Korea's unification and religious policies during the 2000s continued to operate within a framework of dual strategy premised on regime preservation. While Pyongyang publicly advocated reconciliation and cooperation in inter-Korean relations through economic collaboration projects such as the Kaesong Industrial Complex and Mount Kumgang tourism, it simultaneously intensified religious and ideological control internally, revealing the contradictory nature of its approach.

The June 15, 2000 Inter-Korean Joint Declaration proposed a "low-level federation" model that presupposed coexistence between the two Korean systems, representing a more pragmatic retreat from previous classical federal unification concepts. This policy materialized through economic cooperation projects including the Kaesong Industrial Complex (operational from 2004) and Mount Kumgang tourism (1998-2008). However, following the July 2008 shooting incident involving a South Korean tourist at Mount Kumgang, North Korea demanded the withdrawal of South Korean personnel from the Kaesong Industrial Complex, citing "hostile acts by South Korean authorities," thereby undermining the cooperative foundation it had helped establish. The comprehensive suspension of inter-Korean exchanges following the May 24, 2010 measures imposed after the Cheonan incident effectively brought North Korea's unification policy to a standstill, demonstrating that external peace cooperation rhetoric served merely as a tactical instrument to ensure regime security5.

Internally, North Korea strengthened its military capabilities based on the Songun (military-first) ideology while prioritizing regime stabilization. The 2009 constitutional revision codified Kim Jong-il's position as Chairman of the National Defense Commission as the "supreme state post," consolidating military power. The November 2010 artillery bombardment of Yeonpyeong Island served to demonstrate military threat capabilities both domestically and internationally, reflecting North Korea's strategic prioritization of regime maintenance over inter-Korean relations improvement6.

North Korea's duplicity became particularly evident in its religious policies. Despite constitutional amendments in 1992 explicitly guaranteeing religious freedom, persecution of Christians continued throughout the 2000s. According to a 2001 U.S. State Department report, 400 Christians were executed in 1999 alone, with testimonies indicating that 1,000 Christians were martyred following the 2000 inter-Korean summit7. In 2010, the American missionary organization Open Doors designated North Korea as the "world's worst religious persecution state," reporting that 40,000-60,000 Christians were imprisoned in political prison camps. These individuals faced punishment not only for religious activities such as possessing Bibles or conducting secret worship services, but their families also suffered under the yeon-jwa-je (guilt-by-association) system, subjected to torture and biochemical experimentation8.

While North Korean authorities operated showcase churches—Bongsu Church (established 1988) and Chilgol Church (built 2006)—to demonstrate "religious freedom" to foreign visitors, these served merely as propaganda facades. German publication Frankfurter Rundschau reported in 2002 that North Korean authorities conducted three public executions of Christians in November 2001. Amnesty International noted in May 2002 that "thousands of Christians are being tortured in forced labor camps," exposing the purely formal nature of North Korea's religious freedom9. Kim Jong-il's 1998 special directive demanding citizens to "choose either Christianity or me" exemplified the regime's insistence on loyalty to leadership over religious conviction10.

These religious and unification policies were interconnected, reinforcing North Korea's regime maintenance logic. Externally, the regime projected a peaceful image through expanded economic cooperation while internally suppressing Christianity as an "imperialist remnant" to maintain ideological purity. The extent of religious oppression was evident in surveys showing that 99.6% of North Korean defectors in the mid-2000s reported having "no freedom of religious activity"11. The suspension of Mount Kumgang tourism in 2008 and the Cheonan incident in 2010 demonstrated that North Korea's South Korea policy sought regime stability through heightened tensions rather than cooperation, with religious persecution serving as another instrument of regime control. These tendencies intensified further following the establishment of the Kim Jong-un regime12. The evidence suggests that North Korea's 2000s policies represented a calculated approach where external diplomatic engagement served as a tactical cover for intensified internal control mechanisms, particularly regarding religious minorities. The regime's willingness to sacrifice economic benefits from inter-Korean cooperation projects when they potentially threatened political control underscores the primacy of regime preservation over genuine reconciliation or religious tolerance.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

Korean Christianity from 2000-2010 faced internal and external crises and hostility, including social and religious conflicts, heretical infiltration, declining public trust, and divisions within denominational organizations. Yet simultaneously, efforts toward social integration, national reconciliation, and church reform were also underway.

During this period, Shincheonji (Shincheonji Church of Jesus, the Temple of the Tabernacle of the Testimony) deeply infiltrated universities and existing churches, causing divisions within church communities. From 2002 onward, Shincheonji deployed strategies centered on universities in the Honam region, mobilizing "harvesters" and other agents to recruit existing believers and weaken church organizational structures. This infiltration spread the collapse of trust within churches and heightened social vigilance, becoming the backdrop for heretical controversies within Korean churches and broader social distrust.13

In this context, Korean Christianity faced a severe crisis of declining credibility due to pastors' financial misappropriation, sexual ethics violations, and various scandals and disputes involving megachurches. According to a 2005 Statistics Korea survey and a 2007 report by the Institute for Korean Church Social Issues, social trust in Korean churches plummeted dramatically. Media outlets intensively reported on church corruption and collective selfishness, spreading critical public opinion against churches, while even believers openly expressed disappointment and criticism toward their churches.14Furthermore, internal conflicts within church unity organizations exacerbated the hostile public sentiment of this period. In 2010, the Christian Council of Korea (CCK) faced social distrust due to controversies over heretical group reinstatements and vote-buying scandals, resulting in the fragmentation of church unity organizations. Consequently, churches were perceived as symbols of division and conflict rather than social integration, creating significant obstacles to trust recovery.15

However, even amid these crises, Korean churches engaged in social and religious unity activities for North-South reconciliation and unification. The Korea Sharing Movement and inter-denominational cooperation manifested in practical actions for national integration, including humanitarian aid to North Korea, Peace and Unification Prayer Sundays, and advocacy for separated family reunions. The 2010 "Korean Church Declaration for Peace and Unification on the Korean Peninsula" issued by the National Council of Churches in Korea (NCCK) symbolized the church's commitment to serving and sharing with society's vulnerable and marginalized groups while leading national reconciliation efforts, representing aspirations for social trust recovery and an integrative role.16

Unity attempts between conservative and progressive denominations were also noteworthy. Until the mid-2000s, joint Easter services and integration discussions between CCK and NCCK developed as attempts to promote social integration and reconciliation beyond ideological and theological differences. However, some attempts failed to achieve complete integration, and inter-denominational conflicts persisted.17 Within churches, voices of self-reflection emerged through ethical practice movements, church renewal movements, and enhanced financial transparency initiatives. Particularly, organizations like the Christian Ethics Movement of Korea promoted reforms directing churches toward practicing social responsibility and seeking trust recovery with society.18

In conclusion, Korean Christianity from 2000-2010 faced social criticism and hostility due to heretical infiltration, internal corruption, and divisions within unity organizations, yet efforts toward integration through North-South reconciliation, social responsibility practice, and church renewal were simultaneously undertaken. During this period, Korean churches were seeking complex paths while carrying the dual challenges of social trust recovery and national integration.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. Christianity and South Korea's Regional Diplomatic Relations

China

South Korea's relationship with China during this period exemplified the tension between economic pragmatism and religious conviction. While bilateral trade flourished and cultural exchanges deepened, the question of religious freedom created significant diplomatic friction. The year 2000 witnessed a watershed moment with the Elijah Evangelical Mission incident, where Chinese authorities detained thirty-seven Korean missionaries in Jilin and Harbin provinces, charging them with conducting "illegal religious activities"19. This event served as a sobering reminder of the risks inherent in cross-border evangelical work. The challenges intensified over the decade, culminating in 2017 when approximately 170 South Korean missionaries faced expulsion from China, effectively dismantling missionary networks that had been carefully cultivated over years20. Despite these setbacks, Korean evangelical communities demonstrated remarkable resilience and adaptability. Rather than retreating, they developed increasingly sophisticated covert mission strategies, targeting not only the Han Chinese majority but also ethnic minorities and Korean-Chinese communities who maintained cultural and linguistic ties to the peninsula³. This persistence reflected a deeper theological conviction among Korean evangelicals that China represented one of the most significant mission fields of the twenty-first century. The spiritual burden for China's 1.3 billion souls motivated continued investment in mission work despite diplomatic costs and personal risks to missionaries.

Japan

The South Korea-Japan relationship during the 2000s remained overshadowed by unresolved historical grievances, territorial disputes over Dokdo/Takeshima, and competing narratives about wartime atrocities. Prime Minister Junichiro Koizumi's annual visits to Yasukuni Shrine from 2001 to 2006 particularly inflamed Korean sensibilities, as the shrine honored war criminals alongside Japan's war dead. In this context of political tension, Korean Christian communities emerged as unexpected agents of reconciliation. Evangelical and mainline Protestant churches organized joint prayer vigils and ecumenical dialogues specifically aimed at fostering historical healing and mutual understanding21. These initiatives, while modest in scale, represented a significant departure from the typical nationalist rhetoric that dominated bilateral discourse. Korean missionaries and churches in Japan focused their efforts on diaspora ministries, particularly serving Korean immigrants and other marginalized communities22. This work took on special significance given the historical context of forced labor and discrimination that had shaped the Korean experience in Japan. Christian outreach became both a form of spiritual ministry and a means of preserving cultural identity within the Japanese social fabric.

North Korea

The landmark 2000 Inter-Korean Summit between Presidents Kim Dae-jung and Kim Jong-il opened unprecedented opportunities for Christian humanitarian engagement with North Korea. South Korean Christian organizations, including established NGOs such as Good Neighbors, World Vision, and the Nanum Movement, rapidly mobilized to provide food assistance and medical supplies to their northern compatriots. This humanitarian surge was accompanied by a powerful theological vision that captured the imagination of Korean evangelicals: "unification under the gospel"23. This concept envisioned not merely political reunification but spiritual transformation, where a unified Korea would serve as a launching pad for Christian mission throughout East Asia. Prayer movements, mission conferences, and evangelical literature consistently promoted this vision throughout the decade. However, the harsh reality of North Korean religious persecution created a sobering counterpoint to these evangelical hopes. Despite official churches like Bongsu Church in Pyongyang serving as showcase institutions for foreign delegations24, underground Christian believers continued to face systematic persecution, including arrests, torture, and execution. These circumstances only intensified South Korean Christian prayers and advocacy for religious freedom in the North. The disconnect between humanitarian access and religious freedom highlighted the complex nature of Christian engagement with totalitarian regimes. While food and medicine could cross the DMZ, Bibles and evangelical materials remained strictly prohibited, forcing Korean Christians to develop more subtle approaches to spiritual outreach.

Mongolia and Central Asia

The post-Cold War opening of Mongolia and Central Asian republics provided Korean Christianity with its most dramatic mission success story of the period. Mongolia's transformation was particularly striking: from merely four known Christian believers in 1989 to over 40,000 by 2008, this growth represented one of the most rapid Christian expansions in modern Asian history25. Korean missionaries played a central role in this transformation, establishing not only churches but entire theological seminaries, humanitarian clinics, and social service organizations. The Korean approach to Mongolian missions demonstrated remarkable cultural sensitivity, incorporating traditional Mongolian values while introducing Christian faith.

In Central Asia, Korean missions faced the dual challenges of government restrictions and resistance from Muslim-majority populations. Missionaries developed innovative strategies, using educational centers, Korean-language schools, and cultural programs as platforms for building relationships and sharing faith26. These approaches reflected hard-learned lessons about the importance of cultural engagement and long-term relationship building in cross-cultural missions.

  1. Engagement with Christian-Majority Nations

The American Connection

The United States continued to serve as the primary model and partner for Korean evangelical development throughout the 2000s. Korean-American churches, now numbering in the thousands, became crucial bridges between Korean and American evangelicalism. These congregations provided both financial support for global missions and training opportunities for future Korean missionary leaders. The partnership extended to shared advocacy on issues such as North Korean human rights, where Korean and American evangelical organizations collaborated on awareness campaigns and policy initiatives27. This cooperation reflected the broader alignment between Korean and American evangelical worldviews, particularly regarding global evangelization and social justice concerns.

The Philippines: Catholic-Protestant Collaboration

The Philippines, with its overwhelming Catholic majority, presented unique opportunities for Korean evangelical missions. Missionaries like Rev. Lee Seung-hoon pioneered urban ministries and youth outreach programs in impoverished communities28, demonstrating how Protestant missions could complement rather than compete with existing Catholic infrastructure. The growth of Korean diaspora churches in the Philippines paralleled increasing educational and business migration, creating natural networks for both community support and evangelical outreach. This pattern would be replicated in other countries experiencing significant Korean immigration during this period. Following its 1990s shift to religious freedom, Mongolia became a showcase for Korean evangelical missions. Churches, Bible schools, and humanitarian clinics—largely staffed by Korean Christians—transformed the religious landscape29.

  1. Nationalism, Patriotism, and Christian Identity

The decade witnessed a remarkable surge in Korean nationalism, fueled by multiple factors including ongoing historical disputes with Japan, periodic North Korean provocations, and South Korea's emergence as a significant global economic power. The press, educational institutions, and public commemorations actively promoted narratives emphasizing national unity, historical suffering, and contemporary achievement30.

Within Korean Christianity, this nationalist sentiment found expression in distinctly evangelical terms. Slogans such as "Gospelizing the Korean nation" and "Mission Korea to unify the peninsula"31 reflected the fusion of patriotic fervor with Christian mission vision. This synthesis was not merely rhetorical but represented a genuine theological conviction that God had chosen Korea for a special role in world evangelization. Prominent evangelical leaders like Rev. Kim Jin-hong and Rev. Kang Won-yong emerged as vocal advocates for this vision, organizing massive prayer rallies and mission conferences that explicitly linked national revival with global gospel outreach. Their message resonated powerfully with Korean Christians who sought to understand their nation's rapid development and growing international influence through the lens of divine providence.

However, this period also witnessed significant challenges to traditional notions of Korean ethnic homogeneity. Increasing labor migration, international marriages, and multiculturalism began to complicate simplistic narratives of Korean identity. Some conservative Christian sectors responded by reinforcing ethno-nationalist rhetoric, positioning the Korean church as a guardian of cultural purity against foreign influences32. This tension between ethnic nationalism and Christian universalism would continue to shape debates within Korean Christianity, particularly as the nation became increasingly multicultural and globally connected. The challenge for Korean evangelicals was to maintain their distinctive identity and mission vision while embracing the global, multicultural nature of the Christian faith.
