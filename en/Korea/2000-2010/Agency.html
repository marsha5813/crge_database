Korea: Agency (2000-2010)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. 김대중 金大中 Dae-jung Kim

01/06/1924 ~ 08/18/2009

President Kim Dae-jung's (1998-2003) political leadership was deeply rooted in his Catholic faith, which influenced his policy foundations and social initiatives. Bearing the baptismal name Thomas More, he emphasized, "I am a person of faith before I am a democrat, and my political beliefs stem from my faith in human dignity and justice"1. This faith extended beyond personal conviction to become a political philosophy supporting public values such as inter-Korean reconciliation, human rights, and social integration.

In June 2000, Kim made the first historic visit to Pyongyang, meeting with Chairman Kim Jong-il and adopting the "June 15th Joint Declaration," which carried forward the spirit of the "July 4th South-North Joint Statement." In his Nobel Peace Prize acceptance speech, he declared that "human dignity and the spirit of reconciliation originated from Christian faith," clarifying that his "Sunshine Policy" toward North Korea was an ethical decision based on faith rather than merely political calculation2. This moral and philosophical foundation received support from progressive Protestant circles. The Catholic Priests Association for Justice, the National Council of Churches in Korea (NCCK), and Minjung Theology advocates collaborated with government policies, sharing commitments to human rights, unification, and protection of the vulnerable. Kirsteen Kim evaluates that Korean Christianity's public theology was a driving force for social change, analyzing that "Christianity provided a vision for a new society and supplied the energy to realize it"3.

In contrast, conservative Protestant groups maintained sharp opposition to the Kim Dae-jung administration. On March 1, 2004, the Christian Council of Korea (CCK) organized large-scale demonstrations demanding "elimination of leftists" and maintaining "anti-North Korean and anti-communist" stances, defining the government's North Korea policy as "anti-national acts"4. Dae Young Ryu compares these groups to the American Religious Right, explaining that "conservative Christian forces engaged in ideological reaction centered on absolute anti-communism, pro-Americanism, and an apocalyptic worldview"5.

Economic policies also generated conflict. Following the 1997 financial crisis, the Kim administration implemented structural reforms under the IMF system, which negatively affected Christian-operated educational and welfare institutions. Edward J. Baker points out that Kim Dae-jung's "economic democratization" and chaebol reform clashed with conservative Christian circles that prioritized free-market economics6.

Conservative Christian groups also criticized the Roh Moo-hyun administration's policies for building an equal society as having "socialist tendencies," gradually focusing their efforts on anti-homosexuality movements and moral restoration. From the mid-2000s, conservative factions centered around megachurches sought to preserve "Christian national identity" through active political participation, a trend that strengthened after the inauguration of the Lee Myung-bak administration in 2007. Simultaneously, progressive Protestant groups continued the Minjung Theology tradition, attempting to restore public welfare through solidarity with social minorities including migrants, the poor, and people with disabilities. This theological differentiation expanded beyond doctrinal differences into ideological conflicts throughout Korean society, with religion becoming not just a realm of faith but a political actor7.

In conclusion, Kim Dae-jung's Christian leadership became a driving force for social integration, democratization, and inter-Korean reconciliation, while also intensifying divisions and conflicts within Korea's ideologically polarized Christian community. His faith extended into public ethical practice, and the Christian movements that either supported or opposed him played crucial roles in shaping the religious and political landscape of 21st century Korean society.

  1. 이명박 李明博 Myung-bak Lee

12/19/1941 ~ Present

The Lee Myung-bak administration (2008-2013) marked a period in modern Korean history when the alliance between conservative Protestantism and political power was most prominently manifested. Lee himself had a long history of faith as an elder at Somang Church, and during his tenure as Seoul Mayor in 2004, he employed religious language in public policy discourse, stating he would "dedicate Seoul to God" and interpreting the Cheonggyecheon restoration project as "an answer to prayer." This religious identity became even more evident during the 2007 presidential election. On July 8 of that year, Pastor Kim Hong-do of Geumran Church delivered a politically charged sermon, declaring that "a Christian elder, Elder Lee Myung-bak, must become president," which eventually resulted in a guilty verdict for violating election laws. Pastors from Korea's largest Protestant denominations, including Pastor Cho Yong-gi of Yoido Full Gospel Church, also publicly endorsed Lee from their pulpits and encouraged voting. This signaled the formal entry of Korean Protestantism, which had traditionally advocated separation of church and state, into the path of becoming a "Christian right political force."8

This movement extended beyond mere electoral support to include conservative Protestant involvement in personnel appointments and policy decisions after the administration's launch. Shortly after Lee's presidential inauguration in February 2008, the Blue House and cabinet were filled with connections from large churches such as Somang Church, Sarang Church, and Onnuri Church, sparking controversy over what was called the "GSY (Korea University, Somang Church, Yeongnam) network." A prime example was when a high-ranking Blue House official revealed that "evangelizing government departments is my dream." Indeed, during this period, criticism arose that state institutions were being organized according to the values of a specific religion.9

This Christian-centric personnel approach provoked backlash from other religions, especially Buddhists. When incidents of omitting temple information from public transportation navigation systems and Ministry of Land, Transport and Maritime Affairs records occurred repeatedly from 2008, Buddhist groups critically remarked, "Let's call the Blue House the 'Blue House Church,'" and in 2009, the situation escalated to the point where the head of the Jogye Order made a protest visit to the Blue House.10

Behind this religion-politics merger was the conservative Protestant church's sense of crisis regarding democratization and the emergence of progressive administrations since the 1990s. Protestant Christianity, which had led social reform as part of the democratization movement in the 1980s, experienced a sharp decline in social status after the 1990s due to secularization, decreasing membership, and various scandals and moral controversies. In response, conservative Protestants sought to regain influence by allying with neo-conservative civic groups like the New Right to pursue political empowerment, with the Lee Myung-bak administration representing the pinnacle of this effort. Throughout this process, Protestant church networks functioned as powerful electoral organizations and exercised influence by applying direct pressure on the government regarding specific issues.11

A prime example was the 2008 decision to resume imports of U.S. beef and the subsequent candlelight protests. The Lee administration aimed to accelerate the passage of the Korea-U.S. FTA by resuming U.S. beef imports, which triggered large-scale candlelight protests in opposition. Conservative Protestant groups categorized these protests as "anti-American leftist instigation" and held counter-prayer meetings to fully support the Lee administration.12 Another case was the 2011 discussion to introduce Islamic finance through the Sukuk Bill. At that time, pastors of major churches strongly opposed it, threatening, "If the Sukuk Bill is pursued, we will launch a presidential resignation movement," and the bill was ultimately abandoned.13 This was a symbolic event demonstrating the Protestant community's influence extending beyond politics into the legislative domain.

The Lee administration's conservative Protestant-centered approach received criticism for politicizing faith and instrumentalizing religion, exacerbating inter-religious conflicts. When President Lee stated at a Christmas lighting ceremony that he "stood here in the name of Jesus Christ," it sparked controversy over religious bias and reignited social conflict. In 2009, tensions with Buddhist communities intensified, leading to continued criticism that religious neutrality and the principle of separation of church and state had been compromised.14

In conclusion, Lee Myung-bak's Christian activities in the 2000s-2010s represented a period where conservative Protestantism and political power merged to maximize the social and political influence of Protestantism. However, these actions also left negative legacies including undermining religious neutrality in the public sphere, deepening conflicts with Buddhist communities, and decreasing social trust. The Lee administration is recorded as a case of aligning state operations around a specific religion by mobilizing Christian language and networks, and is evaluated as having posed structural challenges to religious pluralism and democratic development in Korean society.15

  1. 김수환 추기경 金壽煥 Stephen Kim Sou-hwan

07/02/1922 ~ 02/16/2009

In the 2000s, Cardinal Stephen Kim Sou-hwan transcended his role as a mere religious leader, serving as a moral compass for Korean society through his impactful statements and actions. He exerted powerful moral influence both within and outside the Church, focusing on themes of respect for life, social justice, interfaith reconciliation, and political responsibility. During this period, Cardinal Kim's activities expanded upon the values he had practiced throughout his life, including the democratization movement of the 1970s-80s, advancement of human rights, and protection of the socially vulnerable. As a symbolic figure of the democratization movement, he continued his role as a social elder even after his retirement from priesthood.

On January 26, 2001, while celebrating Mass for death row inmates at Seoul Detention Center, he emphasized that "human life is God's gift that no one can violate." He clearly opposed both capital punishment and abortion, stating that both positions stemmed from the same ethical principle of preserving human dignity. This statement served as a powerful ethical warning to Korean society, where insensitivity toward life was prevalent, and symbolized the Church's role as a guardian of public bioethics16. His philosophy of respecting life extended to movements supporting rural communities and ecological/environmental causes in the 2000s. He emphasized both human dignity and the value of natural ecosystems, advocating for practices that combined bioethics with social justice.

In a time when religious conflicts still existed in society, Cardinal Kim actively practiced reconciliation and solidarity with Buddhism. On December 14, 1998, he attended the opening ceremony of Gilsangsa Temple in Seongbuk-gu, Seoul, to deliver congratulatory remarks. In response, on February 24, 1999, Monk Beopjeong gave a lecture at Myeongdong Cathedral. Such mutual visits went beyond formal courtesy, demonstrating to Korean society the possibility of understanding and coexistence between religions17. Throughout the 2000s, he continued to serve as a symbolic figure of interfaith dialogue and harmony, maintaining communication with various religious leaders. This is regarded as a case that deeply imprinted the values of social responsibility and coexistence of religions in Korean society.

Cardinal Kim also expressed prudent yet firm convictions on political issues. In February 2001, he criticized politicians, saying "politicians have failed to serve the people as they became engrossed in their desire for power." This went beyond simple criticism, serving as a moral admonition reminding politicians that their position should be one of service to the people. Notably, in 2004, when progressive forces strongly demanded the abolition of the National Security Act, he advocated caution, stating that "the National Security Act should not be hastily abolished at this point," which drew criticism from progressive circles. However, he presented his position from the perspective of national community safety and order rather than political bias, reflecting his consistent stance that the Church should not become a tool for any particular faction18. Thus, Cardinal Kim practiced moral leadership by maintaining a cautious and balanced voice on socially contentious issues while remaining a symbolic figure of the democratization movement.

In the 2000s, he continued his writing activities, maintaining spiritual and moral communication with the public. Works such as "To Live Truly as a Human Being," "That We Love One Another," and "For You and For All" were not just meditations for believers but messages of reflection asking Korean society about standards for humane living and the value of the common good. He particularly emphasized that "the way to live as a human being is to be with neighbors and to care for the weakest in society first"19. These messages aligned with Korean society's common good, protection of the vulnerable, and advancement of human rights, and as symbolized by his pastoral motto "For You and For All," presented moral standards for society as a whole beyond religious boundaries.

On February 16, 2009, Cardinal Kim passed away at Seoul's Gangnam St. Mary's Hospital. Approximately 400,000 citizens visited the memorial altar set up at Myeongdong Cathedral during the funeral period, demonstrating his establishment as a symbol of national moral consciousness beyond religion. Subsequently, to inherit his life and faith, the "Fool's Sharing" public foundation was established in 2010, an attempt to continue his humble and dedicated lifestyle through social practice20. His passing resonated deeply throughout Korean society, and his influence as a moral leader continues to be evaluated.

In June 2024, the Vatican approved the "Nihil Obstat" (No Obstacle) necessary to initiate Cardinal Kim's beatification process, and he was officially given the title "Servant of God" (Servus Dei). This was a historical milestone clearly showing that he is remembered as an example of faith, justice, love, and forgiveness not only in the Korean Catholic Church but also in the universal Church21. Thus, Cardinal Kim established and practiced fundamental values in modern Korean history such as respect for life, social justice, interfaith reconciliation, moral leadership, and protection of the socially vulnerable, leaving a profound and lasting influence on Korean society.

  1. 정진석 추기경

12/07/1931 ~ 04/27/2021

Cardinal Nicholas Cheong Jin-suk (1931-2021) left a profound mark on the Korean Catholic Church and society while serving as the Archbishop of Seoul from 1998 to 2012, particularly between 2000 and 2010. In the early 2000s, he convened the Seoul Archdiocese Synod—the first in about 80 years since 1922—to gather diverse opinions within the church and explore a transition to a pastoral system centered on the faithful. The Synod became a crucial turning point for increasing transparency in diocesan operations and expanding lay participation, as Cardinal Cheong continued his efforts for pastoral reform and church renewal. True to his pastoral motto "All Things to All People" (Omnibus Omnia), he dedicated himself to diocesan self-reliance, establishing pastoral systems, priest formation, communication with the laity, and fulfilling the church's social responsibilities. Since his time as Bishop of Cheongju, he made "life" a central theme of his pastoral work. During his tenure as Archbishop of Seoul, he focused on the life-sharing movement, missionary work for the North Korean church, and the spiritual growth of the faithful, serving as a stepping stone guiding our society toward a new path22.

Cardinal Cheong was also pioneering in the life movement and organ donation practice. After the stem cell research fraud scandal in 2005, he concentrated his efforts on the life movement, establishing the Seoul Archdiocese Life Committee and creating the "Mystery of Life Award." In 2006, at the "Day of Priestly Sanctification" event, he publicly signed a pledge to donate his organs after death, with 600 priests joining this commitment23. He handwrote a request that "if organ donation is not effective due to age, please donate at least my eyes for research use," demonstrating a practical example of respect for life. This life movement resonated widely both within and outside the church. Cardinal Cheong continued to emphasize the church's social responsibilities, including bioethics, social evangelization, preferential option for the poor, national reconciliation and peace, and sanctification of families.

Cardinal Cheong was also renowned as an expert in canon law. After specializing in canon law at the Pontifical Urbaniana University in Rome in 1968, he launched the Canon Law Translation Committee in 1983 and published a Latin-Korean bilingual edition of the Code of Canon Law in 1989. He spent nearly 20 years translating the Code of Canon Law and writing commentaries, completing a total of 15 volumes of canon law commentaries by 200224. His publications and translations exceed 60 volumes, as he worked to spread faith education and church knowledge with the conviction that "the laity leave behind children, but we leave behind books." His "Commentary on Canon Law," the first complete collection of Catholic Latin canon law commentaries written in an East Asian language, is recognized as a rare achievement worldwide.

The financial management and modernization of the Seoul Archdiocese are also considered significant accomplishments of Cardinal Cheong. He purchased the Hynix building in Gangnam, Seoul, to generate rental income and constructed the new Gangnam St. Mary's Hospital, leading the stabilization and modernization of diocesan finances25. He established a sustainable foundation for the diocese's growth by introducing a Mass stipend sharing system to eliminate wage disparities among priests. This financial stability provided the foundation for the church to fulfill its social responsibilities and carry out various welfare and educational projects.

Cardinal Cheong's activities evoked diverse reactions both within and outside the church. While he gained the trust of the faithful through his reformist pastoral approach and life movement, he also faced controversy and criticism, particularly regarding his comments on the Four Rivers Project in 201026. His conservative stance on social issues and support for government policies created conflicts with progressive believers and priests. In particular, his statement about the Four Rivers Project, saying "let's judge after seeing the results," provoked strong opposition from progressive forces within the church, including the Catholic Priests' Association for Justice, and led to controversy over the alleged misrepresentation of the decisions made by the Catholic Bishops' Conference of Korea.

Cardinal Cheong's appointment as cardinal (2006) was deeply connected to the Vatican's international political strategy, beyond mere religious honor. The Holy See focused on the issue of religious freedom in East Asia, particularly in China and North Korea, viewing Korea as a "middle base" for stable democracy, church expansion, and international image improvement. His appointment reflected the Vatican's intention to position the Korean Church as a bridgehead for church diplomacy in Asia. The Korean government also played a certain role in his appointment as cardinal, with media reports confirming government-level diplomatic efforts and correspondence related to the papal election in 2005.

Cardinal Cheong successfully led mass gatherings as the chairman of the Mass Preparation Committee during the Pope's visit to Korea in 1984, and coordinated the policies and direction of the entire Korean Church as the president of the Catholic Bishops' Conference of Korea from 1998 to 2004⁸. He contributed to improving the spiritual maturity of the Korean Church by leading large-scale faith movements such as the "Bible Centennial Movement." Theologically, he maintained traditional doctrinal interpretation and a conservative line, holding firm positions on bioethical issues such as opposition to abortion and euthanasia, and respect for life. He influenced the formation of moral discourse in Korean society by emphasizing ethical values and the public role of religion through media activities, lectures, and columns27.

In his relationship with state power, Cardinal Cheong preferred internal institutional reform and cautious language, but he also strengthened his social statements, such as when he strongly criticized the controversy over the abolition of the National Security Act in 2004, calling it "a decision that shakes the foundation of the state"28. This garnered support from conservative believers and contributed to elevating the status of the Korean Church within the Vatican's East Asian strategy.

Cardinal Cheong's appointment as cardinal also carries symbolic meaning in terms of generational change and succession within the church. He served as a mediator, embracing modern changes such as youth ministry and strengthened lay education, alongside liturgical solemnity. His pastoral direction is well illustrated by parish community-centered ministry, small community movements, harmony between spiritual training and practical life of believers, and principled statements on social issues. In his relationships with Protestantism and other religions, he emphasized the pursuit of the common good of society and peace among religions, and showed deep interest in rebuilding the North Korean church and inter-Korean religious exchanges.

Ultimately, Cardinal Cheong's appointment and activities in the 2000s-2010s were an occasion for the Korean Church to become aware of its responsibility and status within the global church, and the Vatican expected a greater role for the Korean Church through him. Cardinal Cheong also endeavored to respond to the challenges of the times, including lay education, spiritual maturity, social responsibility, respect for life, and reconciliation between North and South Korea.

  1. 조용기 목사 趙鏞基 David Yonggi Cho

02/14/1936 ~ 09/14/2021

From 2000 to 2010, Pastor David Yonggi Cho, as the senior pastor emeritus of Yoido Full Gospel Church, exerted continuous influence on Korean churches and society through his sermons and social service activities. His contributions can be analyzed across three primary dimensions: theological messaging and preaching ministry, Pentecostal movement and public influence, and institutional integration and status transformation within church and society.

Pastor Cho's preaching ministry was characterized by his "message of hope." Amid historical despair—including the Japanese occupation, Korean War, and IMF financial crisis—he centered his ministry on "delivering absolute hope to people in absolute despair." His sermons were structured around the Fivefold Gospel (regeneration, fullness of the Holy Spirit, divine healing, blessing, and the Second Coming), the threefold salvation (salvation of spirit, body, and material prosperity), and the four-dimensional spirituality (thoughts, faith, dreams, and words). These theological frameworks transcended mere doctrinal systems to address practical problem-solving and emotional healing for his audience. His confession, "I would even hang upside down if it could benefit and bless you," reflects his attitude as a preacher of his era. Indeed, Cho captivated audiences with sermons emphasizing the "goodness of God" and "theology of hope," drawing from his personal experiences overcoming tuberculosis, poverty, and war-related despair. This message of hope functioned beyond mere consolation, transforming despair into spiritual affirmation and challenge.29

Second, Pastor Cho emerged as a public religious leader aligned with Korea's rapid industrialization, urbanization, and welfare developments. He actively utilized media platforms—television, radio, newspapers, and publications—to disseminate the gospel, profoundly influencing the urban poor and general public whom traditional conservative Presbyterian denominations had failed to reach. His influence thus expanded beyond specific denominations to establish him as a national religious leader, evidenced by his receipt of the "Family of Man Medallion" from the New York Christian Council in 2005. During the 2000s, he continued to focus on various social engagement ministries including domestic and international evangelistic meetings, missionary work, social services, the groundbreaking of a cardiac specialty hospital in Pyongyang, North Korea, and the establishment of the "Love and Happiness Sharing Foundation." During this period, Yoido Full Gospel Church grew to become the world's largest single congregation, and Cho elevated Korean Christianity's international profile as a key figure in global Pentecostal and charismatic movements through international evangelistic rallies and church growth seminars.30

Third, Pastor Cho and Yoido Full Gospel Church became fully integrated into the mainstream Korean Christian establishment during this period. While conservative denominations had previously designated him as heretical—with the Presbyterian General Assembly formally prohibiting fellowship in 1983—Yoido Full Gospel Church gradually gained theological legitimacy from the late 1990s through the early 2000s by participating in united organizations such as the Christian Council of Korea and joining the NCCK. By the 2000s, Cho had established himself as the symbolic leader of the Korean Pentecostal movement by serving as a keynote speaker at global Pentecostal conferences, Holy Spirit revivals, and international missionary conferences.31

Notably, in 2008, Cho transferred his senior pastoral position to Pastor Lee Young-hoon and was appointed pastor emeritus. This succession process represented a rare democratic transition—not nepotistic—in Korean megachurches, following transparent church procedures including elder voting and lay member approval. In his retirement sermon, Cho publicly promised, "I have transferred all personnel and operational authority of the church and will not interfere in the future." This event symbolized democratization within the church and an institutional transformation of leadership.32

Pastor Cho's activities during this period receive somewhat ambivalent evaluations. Positively, he served as a messenger of hope proclaiming the gospel in desperate circumstances and significantly contributed to popularizing Korean Christianity and expanding its missionary endeavors. However, criticisms persisted regarding his charismatic leadership centered on individuality, ethical issues arising from secular adaptation processes, and inconsistencies in his theological framework. While his Fivefold Gospel and threefold salvation message became mainstream across Korean denominational boundaries, concerns about individualistic prosperity theology and ethical controversies remained.33

In summary, Pastor Cho during 2000-2010 reigned as a spiritual comforter and national preacher in post-IMF Korean society through his "message of the Holy Spirit and hope," completing the institutionalization and popular establishment of the Korean Pentecostal movement. This also symbolizes a transitional period in Korean Christianity, shifting from "Presbyterian-centered Reformed tradition" to charismatic popular faith.34

  1. 이만열 李萬烈 Man-yeol Yi

05/08/1938 ~ Present

Professor Yi Man-yeol is a prominent historian and public intellectual who has harmonized academic depth with social practice through his integrated research of modern Korean history and Christian history. Born in Haman, South Gyeongsang Province, into a Christian family that had maintained its faith for three generations, he embarked on the path of a historian after experiencing the Korean War, which made him realize that "the pain of the nation became the driving force for historical research". While studying Western history and religious history at Seoul National University's Department of History, he became aware of his ignorance about Korean history during his military service and redirected his research. He established a unique methodology that fused Kim Cheol-jun's structural history with Han Woo-geun's positivism35.

The turning point in his academic career came when he was forcibly dismissed from Sookmyung Women's University by the new military regime in 1980. Afterward, he collected approximately 1,200 previously undisclosed documents from missionaries who had come to Korea while at Princeton Theological Seminary in the United States, establishing a new foundation for Korean Christian historical research36. After returning to Korea, he founded the 'Korean Christian History Research Association' led by historians in 1982, expanding church history research from a theological focus to a social history dimension. His belief that "church history research is not a relic of the past but a mirror of the present" was realized through the compilation of "The 100-Year History of Korean Christianity"37. Through this work, he empirically demonstrated that Christians played a leading role in about 38% of the nationwide uprising areas during the March 1st Movement of 191938.

In the 2000s, as the Chairman of the National Institute of Korean History (2003–2006), he focused on restoring the public nature and balance of historical education. Under the belief that "historical truth must be verified in the lives of the people," he resisted historical distortion and voluntarily bridged the gap between academia and education39. In particular, as the first chairman of the Compilation Committee for the Dictionary of Pro-Japanese Figures, he built a database of 2.5 million individuals and completed the three-volume "Dictionary of Pro-Japanese Figures" in 2009, earning praise for "laying the foundation for national reconciliation through historical settlement"40.

While his faith was rooted in the tradition of the Koshin Presbyterian Church, he did not hesitate in progressive social engagement. He participated in the North-South Sharing Movement and led the "Dictionary of North Korean Christian History" (scheduled for completion in 2025), continuing practical activities that connected issues between the two Koreas and the historical responsibility of the church41. Quoting from the Book of Acts, he emphasized that "nation is an instrument of divine providence," a position repeated in his book "I Am the Center of History"42. In "Korean Christianity and the National Movement" (1986), he interpreted that Christian consciousness between 1894 and 1945 developed from anti-feudalism to independent sovereignty to anti-Japanese national consciousness, and interpreted that "the national movement is the social realization of the gospel"43.

Some scholars criticized this nation-centered interpretation as risking the universality of Christianity44, but Yi responded in "A Study on the History of Korean Christianity Acceptance" (1998) that the educational work of Western missionaries contributed to nurturing nationalist intellectuals, and that "national subjectivity is manifested in the process of voluntary acceptance of the gospel"45.

At 85 years old in 2023, the elderly scholar still participates in weekly demonstrations in Seoul's Gwanghwamun, practicing his creed that "a historian's responsibility lies in the field"46. His life leaves a unique footprint in Korean intellectual history through the harmony of academic rigor and social practice, conservative faith and progressive participation47.

  1. 강원용 姜元龍 Won Yong Kang

07/03/1917 ~ 08/17/2006

Rev. Kang Won-yong was a prominent figure who practiced "dialogue" and "reconciliation" across the boundaries of faith and society in Korean modern history, where division and confrontation had become normalized. His activities during the 2000s-2010s represented a period when his lifelong theological and social practices were concentrated, and his influence left a profound resonance throughout Korean society beyond the church. His theology of "dialogue" and "humanization," which he pursued throughout his life, was materialized in various areas, including the Christian Academy movement in the 1960s, his ministry at Kyungdong Church, and the establishment of the Peace Forum in the 21st century, remaining a valid model of social practice even in the early 21st century.48

In the early 2000s, Kang Won-yong focused on establishing the Peace Forum, a non-profit organization dedicated to creating a platform for peace on the Korean Peninsula, reconciliation between North and South Korea, and communication transcending religion and ideology. This Peace Forum inherited the spirit of dialogue and humanization from the Korean Christian Academy, founded in 1965, and was a practical movement seeking peaceful unification through cooperation between the two Koreas and neighboring countries, as well as dialogue among various religions and ideologies. He received international recognition for his achievements, winning the Niwano Peace Prize in 2000 and the Manhae Peace Prize in 2002. Until his passing in 2006, he continued to lead international conferences for establishing peace on the Korean Peninsula, putting his lifelong beliefs into practice.49

Kang Won-yong's theology was rooted in Reinhold Niebuhr's "Christian Realism" and Paul Tillich's "Theology of Grace." He emphasized that while maintaining the essence of the Christian faith, the church should not escape from the world or remain in historical pessimism but should participate responsibly in society. This theological stance is encapsulated in his famous motto, "Between and Beyond." In other words, it was an attitude that rejected extreme opposition or dogmatism and sought ways of coexistence and reconciliation through dialogue. This thinking was materialized in the Christian Academy's practical programs such as "dialogue meetings" and "middle group education" in the 1960s-70s, and continued into the 2000s through the Peace Forum, interfaith dialogue movements, and social practices.50

His theology and practice were intensively manifested in the concrete pastoral field of Kyungdong Church. He did not adhere to traditional worship forms but led the church to actively communicate with society and become an open space embracing various traditions and cultures through various attempts such as introducing Korean identity-filled vestments, publishing hymnals, providing medical services for foreign workers, and exchanging worship services with the Anglican Church. Kyungdong Church was a base for democratization and human rights movements in the 1970s-80s, and in the 2000s, it operated various programs for the socially disadvantaged, including medical clinics for foreign workers and support for multicultural families. This was the practice of Kang's concept of "Total Mission," which states that "evangelism, social service, education, and justice realization are all essential to mission."51

Kang Won-yong's representative social practice is well demonstrated in the "Christian Academy" movement. Founded in 1965, this organization was a platform for dialogue, education, and middle group movement encompassing various classes, ideologies, religions, and generations both within and outside the church. Inspired by the German academy movement, he developed the academy movement for democratization of Korean society, cultivation of civic consciousness, and resolution of conflicts across society. Numerous civil society leaders and democratization activists were produced from here. Particularly during the military regime in the 1970s-80s, the Christian Academy was a "refuge" and "training ground" for the democratization movement, and in the 2000s, it developed into a practical network for peace on the Korean Peninsula and inter-Korean dialogue, such as the Peace Forum. Kang emphasized that "without dialogue, there is no democracy, no peace, and no future for the church."52

Kang Won-yong was a representative Korean leader of the international church unity movement, or the ecumenical movement. He actively participated in World Council of Churches (WCC) activities since the 1960s, introducing the spirit of "Unity in Diversity" to the Korean church. This was a belief that while acknowledging differences in denomination, ideology, ethnicity, and religion, one should unite for greater common good and peace. He believed that "in the 21st century, religions will be divided into open religions and closed religions," and only open religions can play a role in protecting peace, life, justice, and human rights in a pluralized society. This belief was materialized in inter-Korean reconciliation, interfaith dialogue, and international peace movements.53

The Presbyterian Church in the Republic of Korea (PROK) denomination, where he was active, had long had two conflicting streams: social mission (Minjung church) and church growth (Sungpoonghoe). He saw this polarization as harmful to both church and society. So he dreamed of "a community that overcomes opposition and conflict, closely unites, and complements each other." His "Total Mission" and "Theology of Dialogue" were the keys to overcoming this polarization. He believed that the church should not ignore the pain and division of reality but should pioneer "impossible possibilities" through dialogue, reconciliation, and practice. Kang emphasized that "evangelism and social participation, individual salvation and social justice, inner faithfulness and external practice of faith can never be separated."54

Kang Won-yong also engaged in deep dialogue with "Minjung Theology," a unique theological tradition in Korea. Minjung Theology sees God's justice and love realized in the liberation of the oppressed people and in the field of their suffering. While acknowledging the importance of the radicality and field-orientation of Minjung Theology, Kang cautioned against the church becoming an exclusive camp logic or a tool of movement. He emphasized that "true liberation is not a repetition of revenge or hostility, but overcoming hatred with love and achieving change in relationships and reconciliation."55

Entering the 2000s, Kang Won-yong established the Korean Peninsula Peace Forum and focused on inter-Korean reconciliation and international peace movements. He believed that "religion is the last bastion of values of peace, reconciliation, and life." Even after his passing in 2006, his spirit continues at the Christian Academy, Peace Forum, Kyungdong Church, and others, remaining an important legacy of the Korean church, civil society, democratization movement, and peace movement. In 2010, at the Kyungdong Church International Academic Seminar, his theology and practice were reinterpreted as the 'foundation of unification theology for the 21st century,' influencing new approaches to North Korean human rights issues.56

Kang Won-yong's life and thought were continuous adventures trying to practice universal values of "dialogue," "reconciliation," and "humanization" while crossing boundaries between church and society, faith and reality, individual and community, progressives and conservatives, South and North, religion and religion. He personally demonstrated that "filling the gap, turning confrontation into reconciliation, and changing the impossible into the possible" is the mission of the church, religion, and civil society.57

  1. 이용희 Yong-hee Lee

12/18/1958 ~ Present

The Esther Prayer Movement led by Professor Lee Yong-hee emerged between 2000 and 2010 as a transformative force within South Korea’s conservative Christian circles, blending socio-political activism with charismatic theology to reshape the church’s role in public life.58 Faced with stagnating membership growth (annual 0.6% increase) and widespread youth disengagement, the movement capitalized on 24/7 prayer systems imported from the International House of Prayer (IHOP) in the U.S., establishing 18 regional “Unification Square Prayer Meetings” by 2009 to address North Korean human rights and refugee support.59 This strategic fusion of spiritual discipline and political advocacy enabled the group to mobilize 12,000 phone protests against the 2007 Sexual Orientation Anti-Discrimination Bill and secure participation from 40 National Assembly members in its events by 2009.60

Professor Lee’s academic rigor, demonstrated through 16 papers on North Korean issues compiled in his Collected Works on North Korea (2024), provided theological legitimacy to the movement’s cross-border activism.61 The 2023 North Korea Freedom Week saw the movement organizing press conferences opposing China’s forced repatriation of defectors, further solidifying its international profile.62 However, its alliance with U.S. New Apostolic Reformation leader Lou Engle during the 2011 Jesus Army Conference exposed doctrinal controversies, including unorthodox practices like “demon-binding prayer” and marathon overnight vigils linked to health crises among adherents.63

By 2012, the Korea Christian Heresy Countermeasures Coalition formally denounced the movement’s teachings on “apostolic authority” and “identificational repentance,” marking a turning point in its influence.64 These disputes revealed deeper tensions within Korean Protestantism, as conservative churches struggled to reconcile evangelistic zeal with theological accountability in an increasingly secular society.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

  1. Korean Church Women United (KCWU) and Korean Association of Women Theologians (KAWT)

From the late 1980s through the 2010s, Christian women and their groups—especially the Korean Church Women United (KCWU) and Korean Association of Women Theologians (KAWT)—played a crucial role in bringing the Japanese military "comfort women" issue out of silence and into a global human rights movement. Their activism, deeply rooted in their faith and commitment to justice, shaped how both Korea and the world responded to this wartime sexual violence.

The movement began with the groundbreaking work of Jeongok Yoon and Hyojae Lee, both pastors' daughters with strong Christian backgrounds. Yoon dedicated her life to researching this issue while Lee provided organizational leadership that helped bring the "comfort women" issue into public awareness. Their Christian upbringing, which connected faith with national independence and protecting the vulnerable, really drove their activism. We see this same spirit in survivors like Haksoon Kim and Wonok Kil, whose faith gave them courage to break decades of silence. Kim's brave testimony in 1991, which she described as something God preserved her to do, became a turning point that inspired other survivors to speak up, shifting the movement from victimhood to survivor-led activism. Kil, who served as a church deaconess, not only shared her story but devoted her life to helping others, truly living out Christian ideals of compassion and justice.65

The KCWU, started in 1967, was actually the first organization in Korea to formally demand an apology from Japan about the "comfort women" issue—long before the Korean Council for the Women Drafted for Military Sexual Slavery by Japan (the Korean Council) formed in 1990. Throughout the '70s and '80s, KCWU fought against sex tourism, stood with atomic bomb victims, and advocated for women on the margins. In 1988, KCWU hosted the international meeting where Yoon first publicly revealed the reality of "comfort women," which led to creating a research committee and, eventually, the founding of the Korean Council. KCWU provided essential support to the Council, and church women organized the first Wednesday protests in 1992, which became an enduring symbol of the movement.66

The 1990s were filled with persistent efforts to hold Japan legally accountable, not just seeking compensation. The Korean Council, with KCWU's leadership, pushed for six key demands: revealing documents, acknowledging the crime, officially apologizing, providing legal reparations, creating memorials, and teaching this history in schools. As Japan kept denying legal responsibility, activists stepped up their efforts, including filing criminal complaints in Japanese courts (which were rejected) and pursuing international arbitration. Though Japan blocked these efforts, they highlighted the movement's commitment to framing this as a crime against humanity under international law.67 During this time, Korean activists, many from Christian organizations, built alliances with survivors and advocates across Asia and beyond, speaking at the United Nations and making sure this issue was included in major international human rights declarations and conferences.68

All these efforts culminated in the Women's International War Crimes Tribunal on Japan's Military Sexual Slavery, held in Tokyo in December 2000. This "people's tribunal," organized by NGOs including the Korean Council with support from KCWU and KAWT, was created because post-war tribunals had failed to prosecute sexual slavery. KCWU helped bring survivors and evidence to the tribunal, while KAWT provided theological context, describing the tribunal's work as fulfilling biblical justice and incorporating survivors' voices into church services and prayers. With international legal experts serving as judges, the tribunal found Emperor Hirohito and other Japanese leaders guilty of crimes against humanity and declared that Japan was responsible for reparations. Though not legally binding, the verdict was groundbreaking: it publicly established command responsibility for the sexual slavery system and set a precedent for recognizing wartime sexual violence as a crime against humanity.

In the 2000s, both advocacy and theological engagement deepened. KCWU and KAWT continued pushing for legal accountability in international forums, supported the creation of the Butterfly Fund to help global survivors of wartime sexual violence, and helped establish the War & Women's Human Rights Museum. KAWT developed feminist theological interpretations that connected the "comfort women" struggle to biblical stories of justice and liberation, and created worship resources to bring this issue into the spiritual life of Korean churches.69 Their activism transformed the "comfort women" issue from a local concern to a universal human rights issue and a core matter of Christian justice, inspiring global solidarity and ongoing efforts for truth, justice, and reparations.

  1. Christian Council of Korea (CCK)

The 2000s were a turning point for the Christian Council of Korea (CCK), marking when conservative Christian voices really started clashing with society and sometimes joining forces with political powers. During this time, the CCK became more than just a church coalition – they transformed into active political players who weren't shy about taking strong positions on social issues.

In the early 2000s, when President Kim Dae-jung was in office, the CCK really didn't trust his progressive government. They took hard-line conservative stances, like fighting against the repeal of the National Security Law. By 2004, they were publicly opposing President Roh Moo-hyun's private school law reforms, calling it "religious persecution." During this period, CCK's messaging focused on morally and religiously condemning government policies. They repeatedly used their religious authority to warn about the country's direction, calling for "prayer and repentance"70.

Everything changed when Lee Myung-bak took office in 2007. The CCK's attitude completely flipped as they welcomed what they called a "Christian president" with enthusiasm and showered his administration with support and validation. They even framed the controversial Four Major Rivers Project as "a mission to restore God's creation," giving it their full backing71. When the "Mad Cow Disease candlelight protests" erupted, the CCK labeled the protesters as troublemakers disrupting the government and called for a return to conservative order. Their official statements went beyond policy commentary, using religious language like "let's pray for the president" and "beware of those threatening national security" – effectively blending nationalism with faith72.

In 2009, when various groups were issuing "statements on the state of the nation," the CCK pushed back, claiming these were attempts to "politicize the church." They harshly criticized the National Human Rights Commission and the Religious Freedom Policy Institute, labeling them as "forces oppressing Christianity." They even made extreme claims that "Christian persecution worse than during Japanese colonization, the Yushin regime, or the Fifth Republic is happening now" – revealing how the CCK positioned itself as either a "historical victim" or "defender of faith" in their political theology73.

In the late 2000s, the CCK ramped up their responses to various social issues like homosexuality, anti-discrimination laws, and taxation of religious leaders. They defined homosexuality as "a sin that destroys families and damages the created order" and opposed anti-discrimination legislation as "legalizing Christian persecution." They were particularly vocal about the Queer Culture Festival held in Seoul Plaza after 2008, calling it a "disorderly event harming public order" and demanding strong action from both the central government and local authorities74. Meanwhile, internally, the CCK was dealing with major trust issues after scandals involving Pastor Yonggi Cho's succession controversy and embezzlement allegations. Rather than addressing these issues head-on, the CCK defensively framed them as "defamation by outside forces" while emphasizing denominational autonomy75.

Throughout the 2000s, the CCK was clearly focused on leveraging their close relationship with certain administrations to generate political discourse and strengthen conservative Christianity's influence in Korean society. As a result, their language operated as an ideology protecting traditional values through concepts of "nation," "ethnicity," "family," and "faith" – which ultimately contributed to amplifying social conflicts76.

  1. Catholic Priests' Association for Justice

During the 2000s, the Catholic Priests' Association for Justice went beyond mere religious declarations and drove social change through concrete, persistent actions in major Korean social conflicts. In 2006, when protests against the expansion of U.S. military bases in Pyeongtaek intensified, the priests visited Daechuri village personally to celebrate Mass with residents, demonstrating their belief that "peace can only be achieved through peaceful means."77 They declared themselves "nonviolent peace shields" against military deployment, spent nights with the villagers, and helped mobilize nationwide opposition to the base expansion.

When protests against U.S. beef imports swept across the country in 2008, the priests took to the streets again. On June 30, they led an emergency Mass in front of Seoul City Hall, gathering around 300 priests and 20,000 believers and citizens.78 The Association announced, "We will pitch tents here until the wounds of the people heal, conduct an indefinite overnight prayer fast, and hold Mass every day at 7 PM"—and they actually followed through with these Masses, fasts, and candlelight marches throughout downtown Seoul, including at Myeongdong Cathedral. Throughout this process, the priests emphasized nonviolent peaceful protest principles against the government's harsh crackdowns and encouraged citizens to voluntarily join in Masses and silent marches.79 The priests maintained that "the power protecting the candlelight is nonviolence" and directly helped maintain order at demonstrations to prevent them from turning violent.

When the Ssangyong Motor layoff crisis dragged on, the priests stayed with the laid-off workers at the memorial altar in front of Daehanmun Gate, continuously performing "street Masses."⁶ These weren't just for comfort—they were symbolic actions that branded the message "mass layoffs are social suffering" into Korean society. Priests who led labor ministry emphasized that "staying with those who suffer, praying with them, and protecting them is the Catholic Church's mission" as they mourned the deaths of laid-off workers and their families while calling for social solidarity.80

In the movement against the Four Rivers Project, the priests traveled nationwide to lead life and peace Masses and prayer meetings. In 2010, they held a "National Priests' Prayer Meeting Urging the Suspension of the Four Rivers Project" in front of the National Assembly, strongly criticizing the government's unilateral development policy. The Association declared that "destroying the order of creation is a challenge to faith," hung protest banners in churches nationwide, and built solidarity with dioceses, religious orders, and other religious groups. 81Opposition to the Four Rivers Project spread into a nationwide religious and civil society movement through cooperation with the official Catholic Justice and Peace Commission.

The Association's work wasn't just about being present at these sites—it was about standing with the socially vulnerable and suffering people, using religious language to root values of social justice, life, peace, and human rights in Korean society. Through Masses, prayers, fasts, silence, and marches with citizens, they proved that "justice is an attribute of God" in real life and showed that religion can go beyond private faith to become a place of public responsibility and action. Their practices played a decisive role in laying the foundations for democracy, human rights, peace, and solidarity at every turn in modern Korean history.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Environmental Discourse Between Catholicism and Protestantism in Korea's Four Rivers Project

In the late 2000s, the Four Rivers Project pursued by the Lee Myung-bak administration sparked intense debates within religious communities about theological positions on the environment, relationships with political power, and how religions should engage with society. Both Catholic and Protestant churches issued statements opposing the Four Rivers Project and took action, but their approaches, discourse structures, and internal responses differed significantly.

The Catholic Church formed a consistently eco-centric theological discourse and participated in the opposition movement with a unified message, coordinated by the central organization, the Committee for Environment of the Catholic Bishops' Conference of Korea, along with dioceses, priests, and religious orders nationwide. Textual analysis shows that Catholic statements centered on the concept of "God's created order," portraying rivers not simply as national territory but as the "womb of the community of life." This choice of language implied a theological critique of human-centered developmentalism and redefined the Four Rivers Project not just as an environmental issue but as a matter of faith.

The Catholic Church's discursive practices were based on Catholic social teachings that have traditionally emphasized social participation and justice implementation. The Church organized statements, sermons, life Masses, and regional prayer meetings led by clergy together with believers. Particularly in 2009 and 2010, Catholic priests participated in site visits, one-person protests, and relay Masses, transforming on-site resistance into faith practices. These social actions aligned with the three principles of "justice, peace, and preservation of creation" that the Korean Catholic Church had developed since the Second Vatican Council.

In contrast, Protestantism found itself in a complex discursive landscape. While voices opposing the Four Rivers Project existed within Protestant circles, these were limited to discourses centered around minority progressive groups like the National Council of Churches in Korea (NCCK) and the Christian Environmental Movement Solidarity, failing to form a consensus position for Protestantism as a whole. Some Protestant statements did use concepts like "destruction of God's created order" and "damage to public interest," but unlike Catholicism, they exhibited a coexistence of diverse languages and claims rather than 'one central theological concept.'82

Discursive practice analysis shows this mixture directly connected to theological fractures within Protestantism. The progressive faction attempted to construct discourse based on ecological theology and theories of justice, while the conservative faction, centered around megachurches, maintained a prosperity theology discourse that viewed development as 'God's blessing' amid close ties with the government. This conflict hindered the expansion of the anti-Four Rivers movement as a faith-based action for all of Protestantism.

From a social practice perspective, progressive Protestant organizations adopted strategies that strengthened mass movement characteristics through solidarity with civil society and other religious groups. Green church movements, life-saving lectures, and ecological sermon campaigns were conducted, but without the official participation of megachurches and denominations, they fell short of the nationwide impact achieved by the Catholic Church. Some conservative Protestants even showed favorable attitudes toward the project on the grounds that they "should cooperate with government policies for national development," clearly demonstrating theological conflicts over church-state separation and social participation within the religion.

Ultimately, the Four Rivers opposition movement of the 2000s provided a turning point for both Catholics and Protestants to theologize environmental discourse. However, in terms of practice and discourse formation, the Catholic Church achieved nationwide joint action based on the strengths of its central organization and consistent theology, while Protestantism failed to unite as a collective religious movement due to organizational dispersion, theological heterogeneity, and political divisions. These differences provide an important case study on how environmental movements in Korean religious communities can be institutionalized and transformed into public theology.

  1. Life-Giving Movement

The Life-Giving Movement emerged in the early 2000s as a profound theological response to growing concerns about ecological crisis and social injustice in Korean society. When the Presbyterian Church of Korea (Tonghap) launched this initiative in 2002, it represented much more than just another church program—it was a fundamental reimagining of the church's mission through the lens of life, justice, peace, and community.

The movement's early years were characterized by establishing theological foundations through publications like the Life Ministry Guidebook and creating educational frameworks that would shape pastoral approaches. Church leaders recognized that meaningful change required both conceptual clarity and practical tools, leading them to develop comprehensive resources for congregations across Korea. As the movement matured, its scope naturally expanded beyond church walls. Connections with international partners, particularly the World Council of Churches, helped position Korean churches within global ecumenical conversations about life and sustainability. These relationships proved valuable in bringing fresh perspectives and solidarity to domestic efforts. One of the movement's most significant evolutions was its growing emphasis on peace-building, particularly regarding inter-Korean relations. Recognizing that life flourishes best in contexts of reconciliation and justice, advocates developed theological frameworks for addressing division on the Korean peninsula. This work reflected a deepening understanding that environmental concerns could not be separated from broader social and political realities.

Mid-way through the decade, reflective evaluation became essential. Leaders acknowledged both strengths and limitations in their approach, particularly noting the gap between organizational programming and grassroots implementation. This honest assessment prompted a strategic shift toward more practice-centered initiatives, emphasizing local church engagement and community-based action.

The latter years saw increased collaboration with civil society organizations like YMCA and various environmental groups. These partnerships enabled more effective public advocacy and demonstrated the movement's commitment to working across traditional boundaries. Through joint campaigns and forums, the religious and secular dimensions of life-preserving work found meaningful integration. Perhaps the movement's most enduring contribution came in 2010 with the Peace Charter for Life. This theological declaration articulated a vision of life not merely as biological existence but as the foundation for creating just and peaceful communities. It represented the intellectual and spiritual culmination of years of reflection and practice. As the formal decade concluded in 2012, comprehensive reporting highlighted both achievements and ongoing challenges. Rather than marking an end, this milestone pointed toward future directions—sustainability, deeper local church engagement, and continuing theological reflection on what it means to be life-giving communities in a changing world.

Throughout these ten years, what began as a denominational initiative gradually transformed into a multifaceted movement touching issues of spirituality, ecology, social justice, and peace. Its legacy continues in the ongoing work of churches and communities committed to nurturing life in all its fullness.

  1. Multicultural Mission Movement

The turn of the millennium brought profound changes to Korean society. Foreign workers began arriving in greater numbers, women from other countries married Korean men and made new homes here, refugees sought safety, and international students pursued their dreams in Korean universities. This wave of immigration fundamentally altered the landscape of Korean Christianity, prompting Protestant churches to look inward rather than outward for their missionary calling.

For decades, Korean churches had focused their missionary efforts abroad, sending pastors and lay workers to distant lands. But as immigrants settled in Korean communities, church leaders realized that the mission field had come to them. They began calling this new approach "multicultural mission," and it quickly gained momentum across denominations.

Three distinct groups emerged to lead this movement. The major denominations—including the Presbyterian Church of Korea, the Methodist Church, and the Kosin denomination—established formal programs through their missionary organizations. The Kosin General Assembly World Mission took a particularly systematic approach, creating an entire department dedicated to domestic immigrant ministry. Meanwhile, local churches and their lay members rolled up their sleeves and got to work on the ground. The numbers tell the story: in 2003, 96 churches were actively involved in immigrant ministry, but by 2008, that number had more than doubled to 19983. Alongside these church-based efforts, specialized NGOs and private organizations like Pyeonggang Church and the Multicultural Network Together brought their own expertise to the movement84. The work itself took many forms, all aimed at meeting immigrants where they were. Churches and organizations established support centers and shelters across the country—155 of them by 2003—offering everything from medical care and legal advice to temporary housing for those in crisis85. Sunday mornings became multilingual affairs as congregations held services in English, Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Mongolian, and other languages, creating spaces where immigrants could worship in their mother tongues86.

Beyond spiritual care, churches recognized that practical needs demanded attention. They offered Korean language classes, helped immigrant children with their education, and organized cultural exchange events that brought Korean and immigrant families together. The work often required collaboration, leading to partnerships between different denominations, churches, NGOs, and even local government agencies. Organizations like Gimhae YMCA, Jinju YWCA, and various immigrant cultural centers became key allies87. Perhaps most significantly, some churches began to see immigrants not just as people to serve, but as partners in ministry. They started training immigrant theology students and pastors, experimenting with shared leadership models that gave immigrants real voice and authority in church governance88. This shift represented more than just a change in strategy—it marked a fundamental transformation in how Korean churches understood their mission. The concept of "reverse mission" emerged, acknowledging that God was working through immigrants to teach Korean Christians about faith, community, and service. Mission work, once the domain of specially trained professionals heading overseas, became something ordinary church members could practice in their own neighborhoods89.

The movement also reflected broader changes in Korean society as the country grappled with becoming genuinely multicultural. Rather than expecting immigrants to simply assimilate into Korean culture, many churches embraced a model of mutual respect and relationship-building. They sought to learn from immigrants even as they served them90. For many immigrants, especially those living in legal limbo as undocumented workers or marriage migrants facing institutional barriers, these church programs provided a crucial safety net. When government services fell short or bureaucratic hurdles seemed insurmountable, church volunteers and staff members stepped in to fill the gaps91. Looking back on this decade, it's clear that Korean Protestant churches accomplished something remarkable. They didn't just adapt to demographic change—they embraced it as an opportunity to live out their faith more fully. The multicultural mission movement expanded the very definition of what mission could be, moving beyond simple evangelization to encompass human rights advocacy, social integration, and community building. As one scholar observed at the time, "multicultural mission is a God's kingdom movement of our time that achieves the democratization of missions, interculturalization, and barrier-free realization of all related existence"92. These words capture the ambitious vision that drove thousands of Korean Christians to see their immigrant neighbors not as foreign challenges to overcome, but as fellow travelers on the journey of faith, bringing their own gifts and insights to enrich the Korean church. The story of multicultural mission in Korea reminds us that sometimes the most profound transformations happen not when we venture into distant lands, but when we open our hearts to the strangers who have made their way to our doorstep.

  1. Protestant Military Mission Movement

Korean Protestant military mission in the 2000s underwent a remarkable transformation with the launch of the "Vision 2020 Practical Movement." This initiative didn't just continue the military evangelization efforts of previous decades—it systematically built upon them while developing into a more strategic and nationwide movement. Rather than simply focusing on spreading the gospel within military ranks, this period aimed to establish the military as both the frontline of youth evangelization and a strategic foothold for national evangelization93.

This transformation had deep roots. The groundwork was laid through the "Total Military Evangelization Movement" of the 1970s, followed by inter-denominational cooperative ministries in the 1980s, and collaborative projects throughout the 1990s. Back in the 1970s, one of the early strategic moves involved deploying military chaplain assistants to battalion-level units and below, with military chaplains leading worship services94. The 1980s saw genuine cooperation emerge as multiple denominations—including the Presbyterian Church in Korea (Tonghap and Hapdong branches)—began hosting joint military mission seminars and retreats. By the 1990s, the Korea Christian Military Mission Association was established, creating the administrative framework for unified ministry efforts95.

The "Vision 2020 Practical Movement" launched in the 2000s as the natural culmination of this historical progression. What made this movement distinctive was its scope—it wasn't limited to a single organization or denomination but represented a pan-denominational mission effort involving the entire Korean church. The strategy targeted over 100,000 military personnel annually for evangelization. The results were impressive: in 2004 alone, approximately 112,000 soldiers were baptized through churches and organizations cooperating with the Military Mission Association96. This achievement reflected the participation of roughly 1,900 churches nationwide that had established sister relationships with military units, either supporting military worship services or operating mission centers. One concrete example was the "Military Church Evangelization Center" established at a unit under the 6th Infantry Division in Cheorwon, Gangwon Province. This center operated as a joint venture between the Military Mission Association and a large metropolitan church. It provided comprehensive services including Sunday worship, discipleship training, baptisms, new believer care, and Bible studies for trainees. Soldiers visited this center every weekend for worship and fellowship, and the structure naturally connected them to local churches after their discharge, creating lasting effects from military evangelization97.

The Vision 2020 movement also institutionalized cooperation between military chaplains and civilian lay ministers. For instance, a military chaplain at Capital Defense Command invited three former soldiers from civilian churches who were attending his unit to co-lead mid-week Bible studies. These individuals assisted with counseling and visiting trainees, providing baptism guidance, and became a practical support base for military mission work98. What's particularly noteworthy about Vision 2020's ministry was its emphasis on quantitative analysis and administrative support systems. Annual baptism records and member church participation rates were systematized, and ministry performance reports were collected from each military church. According to the 2004 ministry report, there were 1,980 registered member churches in the military mission network, with over 1,300 of them providing weekly meal service, material support, Bible studies, and worship leadership to military units99. These activities went beyond simple missionary work—they became an opportunity for Korean churches to recover their public role. By directly communicating with the younger generation within the state organization of the military and providing spiritual and psychological care, churches created a vital connection between the church and society. The structure connecting discharged soldiers to local churches functioned as a pathway for passing on faith to the next generation, particularly strengthening the faith foundation among vulnerable young adults100.

In conclusion, military mission work from 2000-2010 evolved into a practical movement with sophisticated strategies and extensive cooperative systems built upon previous efforts. The military mission work conducted under the "Vision 2020" banner went beyond simple evangelism or volunteer activities—it represented public theological ministry that simultaneously prepared for youth evangelization and the future of Korean churches. This laid the foundation for expanding the long-term influence of military mission into youth ministry, university ministry, and faith management for veterans throughout the 2010s101.

  1. Anti-LGBTQ Movement

Since the mid-2000s, interest in LGBTQ+ issues has increased within Korea's conservative Protestant circles, resulting in the development of systematic and organized discourse on homosexuality. Particularly centered around the Korean Association for Sexual Science (KASS), established in 2014, approaches emerged to examine homosexuality through biological and psychological frameworks under the designation of "sexual science." This represented an expansion beyond existing theological and moral perspectives, incorporating scientific methodology into the discourse.

Around the 2007 legislative notice for anti-discrimination law, conservative Protestant organizations began responding to legislation that included sexual orientation as grounds for anti-discrimination. They expressed their positions through various channels including rallies, petition campaigns, and press conferences, subsequently establishing civic and research organizations focused on these issues. This development evolved into discourse formation utilizing sexual science as a knowledge framework from the mid-2010s.

KASS approaches homosexuality as resulting from "acquired choices or psychological factors" rather than being biologically determined, while highlighting correlations between certain sexual practices among male homosexuals and HIV transmission rates. These perspectives were presented to address public health considerations regarding LGBTQ+ issues. Operating under premises connecting homosexuality to health concerns, they extended their involvement into youth protection and education sectors. For instance, a 2016 KASS publication discussed "potential risks of youth exposure to homosexual behavior" and examined the role of legal and cultural frameworks in addressing these concerns102.

KASS employs analytical frameworks that examine gender roles within heterosexual-centered binary systems. Masculinity and femininity are conceptualized as roles corresponding to biological sex, while homosexuality is analyzed as a deviation from these patterns. Baek characterizes this discourse as "an approach that positions sex and gender as matters of 'nature' distinct from social and political considerations"103. This methodology creates a knowledge structure that integrates religious perspectives with scientific language. In public policy contexts where theological discourse may have limited application, scientific frameworks serve as primary analytical tools, seeking to establish evidence-based foundations for policy positions. As noted in the research, "science functions not as a replacement for religious discourse, but as a complementary framework that enhances social acceptability"104.

Throughout this period, KASS developed media strategies, including programming focused on "transformation testimonies" through collaboration with Christian broadcasting channels such as CTS and KHTV, and creating educational materials for youth audiences. Programs like "Homosexuality STOP-Talk Talk for You" presented narratives suggesting that sexual orientation can be modified through counseling and intervention programs105. These approaches have generated academic discussion regarding their alignment with mainstream scientific consensus. Materials referenced by KASS from organizations such as NARTH (National Association for Research & Therapy of Homosexuality) have been subject to scrutiny within the international psychology community, and certain research cited, including Dean Hamer's 1993 study on genetic factors, has undergone academic revision106.

Conservative Protestant perspectives on LGBTQ+ issues encompass a range of positions and methodological approaches. Baek describes this as a "multilayered discourse structure incorporating frameworks of theology, health, science, and law," indicating that conservative Protestant engagement with homosexuality issues operates as a form of cultural and political discourse that extends beyond purely religious considerations107.

In summary, discourse on homosexuality within Korean conservative Protestant contexts has developed as a comprehensive framework incorporating scientific research, educational policy, legal analysis, and media strategies. Through this process, conservative Protestant organizations have worked to establish evidence-based foundations for their positions within broader social and policy discussions.

Key Theological Issues

  1. Protestant Church Scandals

The 2000s marked a turbulent period for Korean Protestant churches, characterized by widespread clerical succession controversies and misconduct scandals. During this era, leaders of megachurches and denominations either passed their power to their children or became embroiled in sexual and financial scandals that shocked society. The process and aftermath left deep scars on both the religious community and Korean society, unfolding like a dramatic saga.

The Kwanglim Methodist Church Succession Case

The Kwanglim Methodist Church succession incident occurred on March 25, 2001, at the flagship Methodist church located in Seoul's Apgujeong-dong district. Pastor Kim Sun-do transferred his senior pastor position to his eldest son, Pastor Kim Jung-seok. This sparked controversy over the hereditary nature of denominational power, prompting Christian unity organizations and other religious groups to launch anti-succession campaigns. However, Kwanglim Church justified the succession by arguing that "Pastor Kim Jung-seok knows the church situation well and can cooperate with the former pastor, ensuring continued church growth and preventing chaos after the leadership change."108 This incident became the catalyst for church succession to emerge as a serious social issue and established a precedent for megachurch successions.109

The Myungsung Church Succession Controversy

The Myungsung Church succession controversy gradually surfaced in the late 2000s. Pastor Kim Sam-hwan designated his son, Pastor Kim Ha-na, as his successor at Myungsung Church, beginning formal succession procedures after his retirement in 2014. In November 2017, Myungsung Church officially proceeded with the father-to-son succession. This process led to congregational opposition and lawsuits, and on August 5, 2019, the Presbyterian Church of Korea (PCK) General Assembly Court ruled the "Myungsung Church Pastor Kim Ha-na Appointment Resolution" invalid. The Myungsung Church succession was not merely a personnel matter but sparked a massive controversy over the inheritance of church property and power, as well as the damage to denominational public accountability.110

Widespread Megachurch Successions

The 2000s also witnessed a surge in successions at other megachurches and among denominational leaders. Churches such as Guro Central Church, Gangnam First Church, and Kyunghyang Church in the metropolitan area saw father pastors passing their senior pastor positions to their sons. Between 2000 and 2010, 62 churches carried out successions. Notably, nearly half of these churches were led by pastors who had served as denominational general assembly presidents, Methodist bishops, or Korea Christian Federation presidents. Church succession was no longer an exceptional phenomenon but had become routine, intensifying internal church conflicts and social criticism.111

Sexual Misconduct and Financial Scandals

Concurrently, cases of pastoral sexual violence and financial misconduct erupted repeatedly. According to the Christian Women's Counseling Center, of the 108 cases of sexual violence within churches reported between July 1998 and October 2005, over 90% involved pastors. Victims ranged from female laypeople to teenagers and children, with serious crimes including rape, sexual assault, and sexual harassment. After 2010, several high-profile cases emerged, including Methodist Pastor Jun Jun-gu's sexual assault charges, Presbyterian Pastor Jeon Byeong-wook's sexual harassment of congregants, and Pastor Lee Dong-hyeon's sexual violence against teenagers. These incidents revealed that sexual crimes committed by pastors under the guise of power and faith represented a deep-rooted pathology within the religious community.112

Pastoral Sexual Misconduct and Scandals

From the 2000s onward, pastoral sexual misconduct severely shocked both churches and society. High-profile cases included Missionary Kim's child sexual abuse in the Philippines, Pastor Jeon's sexual harassment at Samil Church, Pastor Lee's youth sexual assault at Rise Up Korea, and sexual scandals involving Pastor Kim, known as the "father of migrant workers." According to Christian Women's Counseling Center statistics, 84 of 91 church sexual violence cases between 1998 and 2003 involved pastors, with diverse forms including rape, sexual assault, and sexual harassment. The actual number of incidents was estimated to be much higher than reported cases, hidden by the closed nature of church communities. Pastoral sexual misconduct seriously damaged trust in churches and left devastating wounds on victims, their families, and church communities.113 The causes of sexual misconduct involved complex factors including pastors' psychological deficiencies, patriarchal gender discrimination culture, sexual addiction, and spiritual stagnation. Particularly, pastors' abuse of power, hierarchical relationships with congregants, and male-centered church structures facilitated sexual crimes. Sexual misconduct was defined as "betrayal of trust" and "abuse of power," making churches' ethical and institutional responses an urgent priority.

Social Impact and Structural Changes

The succession controversies and misconduct scandals of the 2000s and 2010s dealt a fatal blow to the moral authority and public accountability of Korean Protestant churches. Churches lost their status as trusted institutions, and the derogatory term "dog-religion" (개독교) emerged in popular discourse. Denominational debates over succession pitted justifications of "church stability" and "God's will" against criticisms of "anti-theological actions lacking biblical, ethical, and missional foundations." While institutional and civil society responses followed, including the enactment of succession prohibition laws and the formation of the Coalition Against Church Succession, restoring public trust in churches remains a distant goal

Anti-succession movements in the 2000s were led by minority evangelical organizations including the Christian Ethics Movement, Church Reform Action Solidarity, and Newsnjoy. They criticized succession as "anti-theological acts lacking biblical, ethical, and missional foundations" and as "church privatization and idolatrous sin." However, megachurches and denominational leaders defended or ignored succession by invoking "church stability" and claiming "lack of theological grounds." The 2012 Methodist succession prohibition law and the 2013 Presbyterian Church of Korea (PCK) anti-succession resolution represented achievements of these civil movements. Nevertheless, succession continued to transform and expand through various methods including son-in-law succession and succession through mission organizations and enterprises

  1. Debate of Anti-Homosexuality Discourse

From the early 2000s, Korean society witnessed the public emergence of homosexuality-related issues through Hong Seok-cheon's coming out (2000) and the increased visibility of sexual minority rights movements. In response, conservative Protestant churches regarded this as a moral threat contrary to biblical values and order, leading to debates within the ecclesiastical community.114

In 2003, the Korea Christian Council (KCC) issued a statement defining homosexuality as "a sin against God's creative order" in opposition to the National Human Rights Commission's recommendation to remove homosexuality from the Youth Protection Act enforcement decree.115 This incident served as a starting point for the consolidation of anti-homosexuality discourse among conservative factions within the ecclesiastical community.116

The debate within the ecclesiastical community was divided into two main streams:

  • The conservative faction condemned homosexuality as sinful based on biblical grounds (Leviticus, Romans, etc.) and regarded it as "a danger that would destroy family and national order." They strengthened their position through denominational statements, anti-homosexuality rallies, seminars, and media contributions. Some churches (Presbyterian Church in Korea-Integrated, Presbyterian Church in Korea-Joint) implemented institutional measures such as "prohibiting admission to theological seminaries for homosexuals and their supporters."117

  • The progressive and moderate factions emphasized the church's inclusiveness and love, criticizing discriminatory attitudes toward homosexuals as contrary to the essence of Christianity. They engaged in supporting comprehensive anti-discrimination laws, advocating for sexual minority rights, and developing inclusive theological discussions within the ecclesiastical community (queer theology, liberation theology, etc.).118

Following the legislative notice of the Anti-Discrimination Act during the Roh Moo-hyun administration in 2007, conservative Protestant circles organized large-scale anti-homosexuality movements. Within the ecclesiastical community, voices criticizing the Anti-Discrimination Act as violating religious freedom and threatening the foundation of Christian faith clashed with those arguing for the necessity of anti-discrimination provisions including homosexuality. Some claimed that "the anti-discrimination law would promote homosexuality and throw society into chaos," while others countered that "Christianity should bear the mission of protecting the vulnerable."119

The mobilization of sexological knowledge, particularly centered on the Korean Association for the Study of Sexuality, intensified ecclesiastical debates. They supplemented religious logic with scientific evidence claiming that "homosexuality is an acquired choice that has harmful effects on society."120 This strategy strengthened the consolidation of conservative factions within the ecclesiastical community while receiving criticism from progressive theologians and ecclesiastical intellectuals as "unscientific and biased discourse."121

The anti-homosexuality debate within the ecclesiastical community during 2000-2010 ultimately expanded into conflicts over faith and social position within the complex context of Korean society's secularization, democratization, the spread of sexual minority rights movements, and the church's crisis (hereditary succession, corruption, tax evasion, etc.).122

  1. New Religious Movement Controversies

The controversies surrounding new religious movements in Korea such as Shincheonji, Daesoon Jinrihoe, and Darakbang should not be understood merely as social issues, but rather as tensions concerning the doctrinal identity and religious authority of conservative-evangelical Christianity. Unlike the trends toward liberal theology and secularization in Europe and North America, Korean Protestantism has maintained a strong evangelical character and continues to uphold the tendency to establish the essence of church and faith upon biblical authority.

Shincheonji monopolized the interpretation of Revelation according to their own system, deified Lee Man-hee as the 'Counselor' and 'Second Coming Jesus,' and directly denied traditional Christology and soteriology123. Such doctrines were received as seriously undermining the biblical inerrancy and the uniqueness and perfection of Christ that are fundamental to evangelical Christianity. Particularly, their evangelistic method of disguised proselytizing ('harvesters') targeting existing churches was viewed with alarm as potentially undermining churches from within124. Therefore, Korean evangelical denominations (Presbyterian Church in Korea-Hapdong, Tonghap, Kosin, etc.) strengthened doctrinal heresy designations alongside education, vigilance training, and legal responses125.

Daesoon Jinrihoe, centered on the revelations of Kang Jeungsan, combined Korean nationalism with syncretic religious elements (Confucianism, Buddhism, Taoism, shamanism, etc.), challenging Christianity's monotheism and soteriology126. The deification of Park Hangyeong and internal denominational divisions not only undermined organizational credibility but were also regarded from an evangelical standpoint as a threat to traditional Christian faith that salvation exists only through Jesus Christ. Evangelical denominations particularly perceived Daesoon Jinrihoe's doctrines and activities not as mere social phenomena, but as extensions of spiritual warfare against gospel truth.

While Darakbang developed evangelistic movements emphasizing pneumatology, soteriology, and spiritual warfare, several denominations within the early Korean church establishment designated their doctrines as heretical127. However, the subsequent lifting of heretical designations by some denominations (such as Presbyterian Church in Korea-Hapdong) was because they recognized that Darakbang's doctrines did not completely deviate from evangelical theology (particularly from a Reformed perspective). Nevertheless, some evangelical pastors and churches continue to maintain vigilance toward Darakbang, upholding a cautious stance for maintaining internal church order and defending biblical truth.

Some critics argue that Korean Protestantism's anti-heretical movements have degenerated into tools for maintaining ecclesiastical authority and political interests128. However, from an evangelical perspective, such responses can be understood not as simple authority maintenance, but as theological and practical necessities for church holiness based on Scripture, defense of truth, and protection of believers129. Since evangelicalism holds biblical authority, the uniqueness and salvation of Christ, and church purity as core values, they do not view the doctrines of Shincheonji, Daesoon Jinrihoe, or Darakbang merely as different interpretations, but rather regard them as spiritual errors and satanic deceptions requiring active response.

Critically speaking, Korean Protestantism's maintenance of evangelical identity while responding firmly to new religious movements has been a factor that helped maintain distance from secularization and liberal theology compared to Western churches130. However, this process has also revealed problems such as excessive stigmatization, academic inadequacy, and political interest interference131. Therefore, criticism of new religious movements should acknowledge the necessity of evangelical doctrine and truth defense while approaching the matter in ways that are more biblical, fair, and academically justified.

  1. New Right Movement/Controversies

The New Right (뉴라이트) movement emerged in South Korea in the early 2000s as an attempt to overcome the limitations of the existing conservative camp, primarily led by former progressive activists from the 1980s and 1990s who had undergone ideological conversion. These individuals were motivated by disillusionment with the North Korean regime, resentment toward the political dominance of progressive forces, and critical self-reflection on the regressive behavior of traditional conservatives. The movement began with the establishment of the Liberal Solidarity in 2004, followed by the creation of the New Right National Alliance in 2005, marking its formal organizational development. While advocating pragmatic centrism, some members shifted toward extreme conservatism, and several figures entered the National Assembly during the 2008 general elections. However, following the inauguration of the Lee Myung-bak administration, internal divisions and organizational dissolution accelerated, with most organizations bearing the New Right label eventually disappearing132.

The New Right promoted liberal democracy and neoliberal economic policies as its core platform. However, the movement emphasized economic liberalism, particularly neoliberalism, over political liberalism. From an elitist perspective, they critically viewed participatory democracy and equality while advocating for overcoming nationalism, yet simultaneously promoting Korea-centered "political nationalism." Their criticism of North Korea evolved beyond traditional anti-communism to incorporate universal norms such as human rights and democracy. Nevertheless, despite attempts to differentiate themselves from traditional conservatives, evaluations suggest they failed to demonstrate concrete distinctions in policies toward North Korea and the United States. Additionally, criticism arose that the New Right's claims of economic differentiation lost persuasiveness since the governments of the time (Kim Dae-jung and Roh Moo-hyun administrations) also implemented neoliberal economic policies133.

The New Right generated controversy through its historical perspectives. Most notably, they attempted to reframe the Japanese colonial period and comfort women issues by replacing existing narratives of "resistance" and "exploitation" with "terrorism" and "export," and "sex slaves" with "prostitutes." While claiming empiricism and using colonial administration statistics as evidence, they faced criticism for ignoring the structural context of coercive exploitation. Their historical views were also criticized for resembling the logic of Japanese far-right groups134. Furthermore, the New Right argued for designating August 15, 1948, as "Foundation Day," attempting to deny the legitimacy of the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea. This conflicted with the constitutional preamble stating that "the Republic of Korea inherits the great spirit of independence declared to the world through the March 1st Movement of 1919 and now reconstructs a democratic independent state," generating controversy over historical legitimacy135.

The New Right is evaluated as having succeeded in popularization and specialization unlike traditional conservatives. Various civic organizations and expert groups were formed, securing public influence through broadcasting, publishing, lectures, and other channels. However, following the Lee Myung-bak administration's inauguration, internal divisions and organizational dissolution accelerated, weakening the movement's sustainability. While the New Right contributed to promoting innovation within conservative circles and diversifying the ideological spectrum in Korean society, it faces significant criticism for deepening social conflicts through ideological radicalization and historical controversies136.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Media Representations of Christianity and Shamanism

Korean society in the 2000s was a period characterized by the intersection of economic instability following the IMF financial crisis and the flow of political democratization, leading to widespread skepticism toward existing authority structures and confusion in values. During this period of social transition, Protestantism began to be perceived by some segments of the public as a "vested interest religion" due to its past collusion with the Park Chung-hee and Chun Doo-hwan regimes, the secularization of megachurches, pastoral corruption, and controversies over church succession137. Simultaneously, shamanism, as a traditional religion, was re-examined as a cultural code for daily comfort and identity exploration amid the limitations of institutional religion and personal crisis consciousness138.

Within this social context, media functioned as an important site for religious representation by constructing diverse discourse structures rather than simply reflecting specific religions in a linear manner. Negative representations of Christianity appeared repeatedly through popular culture content such as films, dramas, and documentaries, closely related to the emotional structure of Korean society. For instance, the film Lady Vengeance (2005) satirically depicted the falseness of formal religion through church scenes inserted into the revenge narrative, while the character Pastor Baek's crime cover-up raised questions about pastoral morality and the credibility of religious institutions139. In the film Secret Sunshine (2007), scenes repeatedly emphasized the disconnect between religion and reality by showing how Christian doctrine centered on "forgiveness" failed to provide adequate solutions for individual trauma. The long-take scene where protagonist Shin-ae throws stones at a cross was interpreted as a symbol visualizing internal rebellion against religious authority140. Additionally, in Tazza: The High Rollers (2006), a church elder appears operating a fraud organization while quoting biblical passages. This can be viewed as a case that reinforced the negativity of Christian representation in media, intersecting with actual controversial issues of the time such as church financial corruption and pastoral succession141.

The background to this repeated negative representation of Christianity lay in the gap between public ethical expectations of institutional religion and reality. Some media content sought to generate reflective discourse about specific doctrines or religious institutions by representing this reality. According to Foucault's knowledge/power theory, media is not merely a passive tool reflecting religion but an active agent that deconstructs power and reconstructs discourse142, and from Williams' concept of structure of feeling, this represented a structured expression of the emotional responses of society at the time143.

Meanwhile, shamanism emerged as another narrative in popular culture within the gaps of this critical discourse. The documentary The Way of God, The Way of Man (SBS, 2008) criticized Christianity's exclusivity while illuminating traditional religion as life wisdom, and the film Goongdu (2004) evoked cultural interest in Korean mysticism through supernatural events144. Furthermore, the spread of digital media enabled the popular reconstruction of traditional religious knowledge. Fortune-telling and tarot cafes, and shamanic content on YouTube that emerged from the late 2000s created new consumption trends that could be called "digital shamanism," particularly among younger generations145.

However, it should be considered that this phenomenon represents a hybrid cultural aspect formed under new temporal conditions rather than a recovery of traditional religion. The phenomenon of religious experiences or mysticism being consumed as cultural content sometimes carries the risk of commercialization, and indeed, fraud cases involving impersonation of shamans were reported in the media in 2009146. Therefore, the rise of shamanism should be viewed not merely as traditional revival or spiritual enhancement, but as a complex cultural phenomenon combining the crisis of institutional religion with anti-authoritarian temporal sensibilities.

In conclusion, Korean media from the 2000s to 2010s functioned as a complex discursive space that constructed new emotional structures and social meanings by representing the different religious systems of Christianity and shamanism in cross-referential ways. Critical representations of Christianity operated as a means of revealing tensions between social ethical demands and the reality of institutional religion, while shamanism appeared not as an alternative to institutional religion but as a cultural option combined with changing temporal sensibilities. Both trends call for reconsideration of the meaning and role of religion in modern Korean society while suggesting the impact of media representation methods on social perception.

  1. Anti-Christian Sentiment in South Korea: Key Historical Incidents and Their Social Impact

The massive candlelight vigils that erupted across South Korea in 2008 initially stemmed from public concerns over the resumption of U.S. beef imports, but gradually expanded to encompass broader criticisms of the Lee Myung-bak administration's policies and structural social issues. As President Lee openly displayed his Christian faith and filled key positions in the Blue House and cabinet with fellow Christians, controversies arose regarding the government's religious bias. The administration's conduct of worship services within the Blue House, the concentration of Christian events in public institutions, and the exclusion of Buddhist personnel became sources of significant social controversy. The Buddhist community responded with a large-scale rally in August 2008, strongly condemning the government's religious favoritism.147

Within this context, candlelight vigil participants and civil society groups openly expressed distrust toward the perceived collusion between the government and conservative Protestant forces. Conservative Protestant groups' defense of government policies and critical stance toward the protests intensified resentment against what was perceived as "Christianity allied with political power" among vigil participants. Consequently, the candlelight vigils transcended simple policy opposition to become a catalyst for reexamining Christian (particularly Protestant) privilege and religious bias within Korean society, serving as a significant trigger for the social spread of anti-Christian sentiment.148

  1. Temple Vandalism Controversies and Anti-Christian Sentiment

The 2000s witnessed repeated incidents of damage to Buddhist temples, statues, and religious symbols through arson, graffiti, and destruction. A notable case occurred in 2020 at Sujin Temple in Namyangju, Gyeonggi Province, where a Protestant believer committed arson. Prior to the crime, the perpetrator had repeatedly engaged in violent behavior at the temple, shouting "Believe in God" and "Hallelujah" while setting fire to banners. During police investigation, the individual claimed to have "received divine revelation."149 Additional incidents involving Protestant believers destroying Buddhist statues and Catholic icons, motivated by religious convictions, became significant social issues. Repeated media coverage of Buddhist statue vandalism, temple arson, and church disruptions prompted official apologies from Protestant organizations including the Korean Council of Churches, demonstrating the substantial social impact. These incidents reinforced perceptions of Protestant exclusivity and aggression toward other religions, particularly among Buddhist communities and civil society. The recurring pattern of temple vandalism and Buddhist disparagement generated collective resentment within Buddhist circles and broader social wariness, contributing to the spread of anti-Christian sentiment. This phenomenon received in-depth coverage by outlets including News & Joy and Yonhap News.150

  1. University Anti-Christian Sticker Campaigns and Anti-Christian Sentiment

During the 2000s, university campuses experienced growing criticism of Protestant missionary groups' excessive proselytizing activities, occupation of club rooms, and mandatory participation in events, which students viewed as violations of their autonomy. This prompted counter-movements including "evangelism refusal" sticker campaigns and "no proselytizing" initiatives demanding respect for student autonomy. Students perceived certain religious groups' missionary methods as infringements on personal freedom, leading to collective resistance. These campaigns, combined with the exclusive and aggressive image of Protestant campus missionary organizations, became catalysts for spreading anti-Christian sentiment among university students. This phenomenon evolved into broader social distancing and critical perceptions of Protestantism, receiving extensive discussion across various media outlets and online communities.151

  1. Buddhist 108 Prostrations Protests and Religious Bias Rallies Contributing to Anti-Christian Sentiment

Following the Lee Myung-bak administration's inauguration, the Buddhist community organized large-scale 108 prostrations protests and religious bias condemnation rallies to oppose perceived government religious favoritism, including the omission of Buddhist information from public institutions and preferential treatment of Christian events. The pan-Buddhist assembly held in central Seoul in August 2008 attracted tens of thousands of Buddhist practitioners and citizens demanding "protection of popular sovereignty and repentance from power." The Buddhist community interpreted government religious bias as "Christian (particularly Protestant) privilege," with dissatisfaction over Christian dominance in public spheres manifesting as collective resistance.152 While these protests and rallies ostensibly demanded religious equality and state religious neutrality, they effectively revealed strong distrust and resentment toward Christian (Protestant) forces. Government documents and media reports expressed concerns that "failure to address religious bias controversies could increase emotional resentment among non-Christians generally," indicating that anti-Christian sentiment had spread sufficiently throughout society during this period to serve as a significant catalyst.153
