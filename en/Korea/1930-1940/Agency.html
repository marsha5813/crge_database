Korea: Agency (1930-1940)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Namgung, Eok 남궁억 南宮 檍

    1. Birth/Death: 1863/12/27 (Seoul) ~ 1939/04/05 (Gangwodo, Hongchoen)

    2. Education: At a young age, they studied Chinese literature and later attended the Government English School in 1883, which was the first government institution responsible for foreign affairs and operated the first foreign language school. During the Independence Association Incident from 1902 to 1904, they were imprisoned along with other leaders of the Independence Association, including Lee Sang-jae. It was during this time in prison that they came into contact with Christianity and converted.

    3. Contribution: Namgung-Eok was a remarkable figure during the Joseon Dynasty who wore many hats as a scholar, diplomat, independence activist, founder of the Hwangseong Shinmun newspaper, and educator. His legacy, however, extends far beyond these official roles, particularly through his ingenious methods of preserving Korean cultural identity during the harsh period of Japanese colonial rule in the 1930s.

The rose of Sharon, known in Korean as Mugunghwa, holds deep significance in Korean culture, with mentions in ancient texts and poetry throughout Korean history. While the exact date of its designation as South Korea’s national flower remains unclear, historical records suggest that Namgung-Eok played a pivotal role in this process. According to a Chosun Ilbo article from February 25, 1969, while serving as Chilgok Busa (a local official), he consulted with Yun Chi-ho to designate Mugunghwa as the national flower. His contributions to Korean national identity also extended to music, as he served as the lyricist for the hymn “삼천리반도 금수강산” (“Samcheolli Bando Geumsugangsan”), which was later modified to become part of the South Korean national anthem.

The cultural significance of the rose of Sharon was further cemented in 1897 during a celebration organized by the Independence Association (Dongnip Hyophoe), when students at Baejae Hakdang first sang “무궁화가” (“Mugunghwaga”), later known as the national flower song. The lyrics symbolized the Korean people’s deep aspirations for independence, making the flower not just a botanical symbol but a powerful representation of resistance and hope.

Namgung-Eok’s most innovative contribution came through what became known as the Mugunghwa Distribution Movement, which he conducted through three distinct but interconnected methods. The movement began during his tenure at Baejae Hakdang, where he provided embroidery education to female students focused on the rose of Sharon. Despite Japanese authorities strictly prohibiting any use or representation of the rose of Sharon, including on students’ uniforms and hats, Namgung-Eok devised clever methods to circumvent these restrictions. He had female students embroider maps of Korea onto silk fabric, symbolizing Geumsugangsan (Mount Baekdu), with a rose of Sharon embroidered in each of the 13 provinces to represent the entirety of Korea. Through this rose of Sharon embroidery, students developed a profound sense of national identity, and their influence grew significantly, with Baejae Hakdang students playing crucial roles in the March 1st Movement (Sam-il Movement).

Following the March 1st Movement in 1919, during Governor-General Saito Makoto’s “cultural policy,” Namgung-Eok expanded his efforts through practical planting initiatives. The Japanese authorities had systematically uprooted rose of Sharon plants and replaced them with cherry blossoms, claiming the sakura as their own national flower. In response, Namgung-Eok began cultivating rose of Sharon saplings at locations where people naturally gathered, such as parks and schoolyards. He started this cultivation at Mogok School’s practice field, making significant contributions to the practical distribution of the rose of Sharon throughout Korea. When Japanese officials or police visited schools, young Mugunghwa saplings were cleverly disguised as mulberry trees to avoid detection. Through this systematic effort, thousands of Mugunghwa saplings were grown and donated annually to private schools, churches, and social organizations across the country.

Perhaps most ingeniously, Namgung-Eok transformed a Japanese children’s game into a vehicle for Korean cultural expression. When he witnessed Korean children playing the Japanese game “ダルマさんがころんだ” (“Daruma-san ga koronda”) in the streets, unable to use Korean language or engage in Korean games, he saw an opportunity for subtle resistance. He called the children over and warmly told them, “You may have no choice but to play the Japanese game, but let me teach you a way to play it differently.” He replaced the game’s original phrase with “무궁화 꽃이 피었습니다!” (“The rose of Sharon has bloomed!”). This simple yet profound change allowed Korean children to express their patriotism through play, embedding the spirit of independence into their daily activities.

This game gained international recognition when it appeared in the popular drama “Squid Game,” where participants shout “무궁화 꽃이 피었습니다!” When the drama achieved worldwide popularity, Japan attempted to claim the game as originally theirs. However, the Korean version with its rose of Sharon reference represents something far more significant than mere entertainment—it symbolizes the enduring spirit of independence of Koreans who found creative ways to resist Japanese colonial rule and preserve their cultural identity through the darkest period in their modern history.

  1. Kim, Gyo-shin 김교신 金敎臣

  1. Birth/Death: 1901/04/18 (Hamgyeongnam-do, Hamheung-gun) ~ 1945/04/25 (Hamgyeongnam-do, Hamheung-gun)

  2. Education: Kim learned Chinese literature and entered Tokyo Seisoku English School (正則英語學校) in 1919, followed by Tokyo Secondary Teacher School (now the University of Tsukuba) in 1922. While he was a student at Seisoku school, on the evening of April 16, 1920, while passing through downtown Tokyo, Kim was deeply moved by a street sermon given by Matsuda, a student at the time at the Oriental Missionary Society Bible Institute. As a result, he started attending the Holiness Church from April 18 (Sunday) and purchased the New Testament for the first time, thus embracing the Christian faith. In the early stages of his faith journey, he considered Christian teachings, especially in terms of ethics, to be much deeper and nobler than Confucianism. On June 27, 1920, he received baptism from Pastor Shimizu at the Holiness Church. During his time in Tokyo, he learned the Bible and was deeply influenced by the Christian thinkers Uchimura Kanzo (内村鑑三), who opposed Japan’s militarism and advocated for a Bible-centered non-denominational Christianity, and Yanaihara Tadao (柳原堂太郎). Kim switched his major from English literature to geography, while simultaneously pursuing theology.

  3. Contribution: Kim was a religious leader, philosopher, publisher, educator, and independence activist who propagated Koreanized Christianity. He was a teacher and educator who aimed to transform young students, who had lost hope in Korea, into honest and diligent citizens to prepare for the country’s future. After graduating, he took on the role of a teacher, consistent with his background in teacher education.

His journey in education began when he took a teaching position at Hamheung Yeongsang Girls’ High School upon his return to Korea in April 1927. However, his tenure as a teacher there lasted only about a year. Nonetheless, this period is significant because it was during this time, in July, that the magazine “Seongseo Joseon” (성서조선), a critical part of his life, was founded. Kim’s life was intrinsically linked with “Seongseo Joseon.” As a teacher, his primary goal was to use education as a means to establish “Joseon” (Korea) upon the foundation of the Bible and Korean spirit.

Another crucial phase of his life was spent at Yangjeongbo Tong High School (now Yangjeong High School). He was there from the early 1920s until 1940, turning 40 years old. His ultimate purpose in life was to establish “Joseon” (Korea) upon the Bible with a fervent love for his nation. Although his profession as a teacher was secondary, the school environment provided the necessary support for the publication of “Seongseo Joseon,” the nurturing of countless disciples through its teachings, and the embodiment of his beliefs and spirit by his students.

During his time at Yangjeong High School, Kim was involved in various activities related to “Seongseo Joseon.” He assisted Jung Sanghun, the editor-in-chief, with the publication of “Seongseo Joseon” starting in 1928. From 1930 onwards, he took on the role of chief editor and publisher of “Seongseo Joseon.” He organized Bible study groups, various gatherings, and conducted in-depth Bible research. In 1933, he published a study on the Sermon on the Mount, and he also served the sick on Sorok Island. In 1939, he mentored his disciple, Ryu Dallyeong, in creating and publishing “Choi, Yongshin” (최용신), which featured the character from the novel “Sangnoksu” by Shim Hoon.

In March 1940, he resigned from Yangjeong Tong High School to dedicate himself to gospel evangelism. In April of that year, he published “Uchimura Kanzo and Joseon” (內村鑑三과 朝鮮) in collaboration with Ham Seokheon. However, at the request of his senior and former classmate, the then-principal, Iwamura, he began teaching at Jeil High School (currently Kyunggi High School) in September of the same year. His time as a teacher in Kyunggi was relatively short-lived, as he was eventually expelled after gaining attention as a questionable figure. In October 1941, he returned to Songdo High School at the recommendation of a relative.

In March 1942, the “Choe-wa” incident, initiated by an article he wrote, led to the closure of “Seongseo Joseon,” and Kim was arrested, marking the end of his 15-year teaching career. His educational journey spanned various institutions, but it can be summarized as a true mission to lay the foundation for the Korean spirit through the development of a genuine national identity, avoiding the politically charged nationalist education of the time.

Koreanized Christianity was at the core of Kim’s efforts to transform Koreans into Bible-based people who could renew Korea. He placed great importance on Korean culture, morals, and the Korean spirit, believing these were the foundations of a new Korea. He emphasized the significance of Hangul and considered history’s essence to be rooted in faith. Although he pursued a Christian-based Korea, he was well aware of the limitations of traditional Korean culture, ethics, and customs. He believed that many aspects of these were impure and needed to be reformed and renewed.

Kim firmly believed that Christianity was not just a business or movement; it was faith itself. He acknowledged that traditional Korean culture, ethics, and customs contained some value but were primarily tainted and dominated by impurities. Therefore, he advocated for the purification of these elements and the creation of a new order. He believed that Christ’s purifying fire could transform all things of the flesh into eternal, spiritual entities. Kim maintained that through faith in the Bible and Christianity, Korea could be reborn. He also had an unwavering belief in the eternal nature of “Seongseo Joseon,” despite the transient nature of other ideologies such as Science Korea, Agricultural Korea, Industrial Korea, and Communist Korea.

Kim’s strong faith-centered approach in his life and work aligned with the cultural movement theory of the 1920s and 1930s. Despite some indirect criticism of the overall authoritarian nature of Japan during the publication of “Seongseo Joseon,” he was passive regarding political independence movements. Kim did participate in the March 1st Movement in 1919 during his time at Hamheung Public Agricultural School. However, according to records from the early 1940s, he did not support the organization of political societies by Christians.

In the 1920s, the term “Korean Studies” began to gain prominence, and there were active efforts to understand national history as cultural history. During that time, Kim’s Korean Studies were of a non-political or anti-political nature. However, around 1930, there emerged a movement that pursued a different meaning of Korean Studies, which eventually developed into the Korean Studies Movement in 1934. Kim’s orientation aligns closely with the Korean Studies of the 1920s and, particularly, the Korean Studies Movement of the 1930s. His emphasis on Korean culture, the accumulation of the Korean spirit throughout history and culture, and the unique Korean sentiment through Hangul all indicate this alignment. Kim believed that the essence of history lay in faith. He believed that Korea could only be reborn through faith in the Bible. Furthermore, Christianity was not seen as a business or a movement but as faith itself. His faith-centered approach highlighted the limitations of the social and cultural movements within the Korean Christian community during the 1920s and 1930s. Kim’s Life and Mission: Faith, Education, and Korean Identity

Kim was a multifaceted figure who served as a religious leader, philosopher, publisher, educator, and independence activist, dedicating his life to propagating what he called “Koreanized Christianity.” His overarching mission was to transform young Korean students who had lost hope in their nation into honest and diligent citizens, preparing them for Korea’s future through education rooted in both Christian faith and Korean cultural identity.

Kim’s educational journey began in earnest when he returned to Korea in April 1927 and took a teaching position at Hamheung Yeongsang Girls’ High School. Although his tenure there lasted only about a year, this period proved pivotal in his life’s work. It was during this time, in July 1927, that he founded the magazine “Seongseo Joseon” (성서조선), which would become the cornerstone of his life’s mission. From this point forward, Kim’s existence became intrinsically linked with “Seongseo Joseon,” as he sought to use education as a means to establish “Joseon” (Korea) upon the foundation of both the Bible and Korean spirit.

The most significant phase of Kim’s career unfolded at Yangjeongbo Tong High School (now Yangjeong High School), where he served from the early 1920s until 1940, reaching his fortieth birthday during this tenure. While his profession as a teacher remained secondary to his ultimate purpose, the school environment provided essential support for his true calling: the publication of “Seongseo Joseon,” the nurturing of countless disciples through its teachings, and the embodiment of his beliefs and spirit in his students. His fervent love for his nation drove his ultimate goal of establishing “Joseon” upon biblical foundations.

During his time at Yangjeong High School, Kim immersed himself deeply in activities related to “Seongseo Joseon.” Initially, he assisted Jung Sanghun, the editor-in-chief, with the magazine’s publication starting in 1928. By 1930, he had assumed the roles of chief editor and publisher, taking full responsibility for the publication’s direction and content. His work extended beyond publishing to include organizing Bible study groups and various gatherings, conducting in-depth biblical research, and serving those in need. In 1933, he published a significant study on the Sermon on the Mount, and he also dedicated time to serving the sick on Sorok Island. His mentorship extended to the next generation when, in 1939, he guided his disciple Ryu Dallyeong in creating and publishing “Choi, Yongshin” (최용신), featuring the character from Shim Hoon’s novel “Sangnoksu.”

The trajectory of Kim’s career took several turns in his later years. In March 1940, he resigned from Yangjeong Tong High School to dedicate himself fully to gospel evangelism. That April, he collaborated with Ham Seokheon to publish “Uchimura Kanzo and Joseon” (內村鑑三과 朝鮮). However, circumstances led him back to teaching when his senior and former classmate, then-principal Iwamura, requested his services at Jeil High School (currently Kyunggi High School) in September 1940. His time at Kyunggi proved short-lived, as he eventually faced expulsion after gaining attention as a questionable figure in the eyes of authorities. In October 1941, he moved to Songdo High School at a relative’s recommendation, but this too would not last long.

The end of Kim’s educational career came abruptly in March 1942 with the “Choe-wa” incident, triggered by an article he had written. This event led to the closure of “Seongseo Joseon” and Kim’s arrest, effectively ending his 15-year teaching career. Despite spanning various institutions, his educational journey can be understood as a unified mission to lay the foundation for Korean spirit through the development of genuine national identity, deliberately avoiding the politically charged nationalist education that characterized his era.

At the heart of Kim’s philosophy lay his concept of Koreanized Christianity, which he viewed as essential for transforming Koreans into Bible-based people capable of renewing their nation. He placed tremendous importance on Korean culture, morals, and the Korean spirit, considering these elements fundamental to creating a new Korea. His emphasis on the significance of Hangul and his belief that history’s essence was rooted in faith reflected his deep commitment to Korean cultural identity. However, Kim was not naive about traditional Korean culture; he recognized its limitations and believed that many aspects of traditional Korean culture, ethics, and customs contained impurities that required reform and renewal.

Kim’s approach to Christianity was profound and uncompromising. He firmly believed that Christianity was not merely a business venture or social movement but represented faith itself in its purest form. While he acknowledged that traditional Korean culture, ethics, and customs contained some value, he maintained that they were primarily tainted and dominated by impurities that needed purification. He advocated for creating a new order through Christ’s purifying fire, which he believed could transform all things of the flesh into eternal, spiritual entities. Through faith in the Bible and Christianity, Kim maintained that Korea could experience genuine rebirth. He held an unwavering belief in the eternal nature of “Seongseo Joseon,” viewing it as transcendent compared to other transient ideologies such as Science Korea, Agricultural Korea, Industrial Korea, and Communist Korea.

Kim’s faith-centered approach aligned closely with the cultural movement theory that gained prominence in the 1920s and 1930s. While “Seongseo Joseon” occasionally included indirect criticism of Japan’s authoritarian nature, Kim remained notably passive regarding political independence movements. Although he had participated in the March 1st Movement in 1919 during his time at Hamheung Public Agricultural School, records from the early 1940s indicate that he did not support the organization of political societies by Christians, preferring instead to focus on cultural and spiritual transformation.

The intellectual context of Kim’s work becomes clearer when viewed against the backdrop of the emerging “Korean Studies” movement of his era. The 1920s saw the rise of efforts to understand national history as cultural history, and Kim’s approach to Korean Studies was notably non-political or even anti-political in nature. Around 1930, a movement emerged that pursued a different meaning of Korean Studies, eventually developing into the Korean Studies Movement of 1934. Kim’s orientation aligned closely with both the Korean Studies of the 1920s and the Korean Studies Movement of the 1930s, as evidenced by his emphasis on Korean culture, his focus on the accumulation of Korean spirit throughout history and culture, and his celebration of unique Korean sentiment expressed through Hangul.

Kim’s legacy lies in his unwavering belief that the essence of history was rooted in faith and that Korea could only achieve true rebirth through biblical faith. His vision of Christianity as faith itself, rather than merely a business or movement, highlighted the limitations he perceived in the social and cultural movements within the Korean Christian community during the 1920s and 1930s. Through his life’s work, Kim demonstrated how education, faith, and cultural identity could be woven together in service of national renewal, creating a unique synthesis that he believed could guide Korea toward a spiritually and culturally authentic future.

  2. Described in the 1910s decade.

During the 1930s, Jo Man-sik remained in Korea to support many independence activists who had gone into exile, and he devoted himself to serving at Sanjeonghyeon Church, where he invited Pastor Joo Ki-cheol to serve as lead pastor. In 1933, despite strict Japanese surveillance, he took responsibility for organizing the funeral of Baek Seon-haeng, a Christian who had dedicated her wealth to educational causes. Even when Ahn Chang-ho died after being tortured during the Su-yang-dong incident, Jo Man-sik led his funeral committee in Seoul, undeterred by Japanese oppression. He also supported the youth movement and in 1939 joined the Ewha Women’s College Support Committee. As Japanese exploitation of Korea worsened, Jo actively promoted the “Buy-Korea Campaign” to encourage people to boycott Japanese products. Scholars interpret these actions as his practical efforts to embody Christian ideals of love and justice.

In this period, Jo Man-sik analyzed the hardships faced by Koreans and identified three main causes. First, he emphasized that the daily struggle and suffering were widespread among the entire population. Second, he highlighted the heavy burden of debt, which affected not only merchants and industrialists but also farmers — with total agricultural debts alone amounting to 40 to 50 million won. Third, he noted a decline in property ownership, with the size and amount of land and houses shrinking due to natural and systemic factors. He pointed to reckless lending practices and the general lack of development for ordinary people as the main culprits. He believed that to break the cycle of poverty and rising debt caused by uncontrolled loans, policies centered on Korean interests were essential. Yet, he recognized that oppressive colonial policies were part of the problem, making genuine protection through such policies unrealistic.

Therefore, Jo stressed the need for leadership training and education for farmers as a key solution. He argued that the way forward included spreading the Christian gospel, nurturing capable leaders, improving farming methods, encouraging additional household industries, forming cooperatives, eradicating illiteracy, and fostering a stronger cultural base.

Additionally, he urged the early development of domestic manufacturing industries as another critical step. He viewed all of these activities as part of broader rural revitalization efforts and the Korean domestic goods promotion campaign — ultimately aiming to awaken the people and improve their living conditions. He passionately called on Christian youth, asking, “Do you not hear the cries of our suffering people? Do you not see how the tasks before us are like a mountain?” For Jo, transforming the lives of fellow Koreans weighed down by poverty was a way of preserving life itself and safeguarding human dignity and worth. This social reform effort to secure the right to happiness, dignity, and self-worth reflects what scholars now describe as transformative justice. From this perspective, movements like the YMCA, the living standards improvement initiatives, the activities of the post-liberation Preparatory Committee for National Reconstruction, and the opposition to trustee rule can all be seen as parts of the broader struggle to bring about this transformative justice in Korean society.

  1. Park, Gwon-jun. 박관준, 朴寬俊

  1. Birth/Death: 1875/04//13 (Pyonganbuk-do, Yongbyun) ~ 1945/03/13 (Pyongyang)

  2. Born into a prosperous family, he lost all his wealth during the First Sino-Japanese War and wandered without purpose for a time. Up until then, he had only studied classical Chinese texts. Although Western missionaries approached him to share the gospel, he instead became deeply immersed in Buddhism and Confucianism. Then, in 1905, while reading in his study, he suddenly heard a powerful voice say, “절벽(絶壁) 유위(唯危)면 혈벽입(血壁立)하라!” which means, “If the cliff is too dangerous, stand on the blood-stained wall!” Looking around, he found no one there and understood it as a divine sign to accept Christ. In 1907, he decided to embrace Jesus at Yeongbyeon Methodist Church and was baptized.

When he was thirty-seven, he prayed earnestly about how to live the rest of his life. He resolved to become a doctor who could heal both body and soul, and in 1912, he moved to Seoul to study medicine. Around that time, his wife endured multiple stillbirths but safely gave birth to their son, Young-chang, in 1915. After three years of study, he earned his medical license, returned home, purchased a building, and opened Jejung Hospital. He also founded the first church in the area, where he led worship services and preached the gospel. Although the local town lacked a proper physician, his hospital became well-known — not only because he treated physical illnesses but also because he cared deeply for people’s spiritual needs. The community trusted him, and the hospital flourished.

However, a tragic mistake occurred when he accidentally prescribed the wrong medicine, leading to a young boy’s death. With no other hope, he prayed fervently — and, miraculously, during the boy’s funeral three days later, the child came back to life. He later testified to this miracle in church, giving thanks to God. After that, he continued to care for the sick and to preach the gospel while traveling to underserved villages.

In 1934, he opened the Red Cross Clinic in Gaechon-eup, Pyeonganam-do, and was appointed the first elder of Gaechon Church.

Around 1935, Park Gwan-jun experienced another vivid vision. While praying in front of the pulpit at church, he saw a figure dressed in white, who asked, “From now on, I will choose Christ’s elite soldiers. Who will shed blood for me?” Without hesitation, Park answered, “I will shed my blood.” Looking more closely, he saw the figure holding a scroll listing about 40 to 50 names — and at the top was his own, ‘Park Gwan-jun.’

Soon after, he read in the newspaper that three schools in Pyongyang — Sungsil Technical School, Sungsil Middle School, and Sungsil Girls’ High School — were under threat of closure because of the issue of forced ancestral shrine worship. Determined to resist, he went to the Pyongyang provincial office to confront the Japanese education chief, boldly condemning the unjust practice. The American Presbyterian Mission Board decided they would rather shut down the schools than comply with such rituals.

Park Gwan-jun also petitioned the Governor-General’s Office, warning that Japan’s oppression would lead to its downfall and declaring that shrine worship was idolatry, which the church could never accept.

Around that time, Pyongyang Theological Seminary learned that the Pyeongbuk Presbytery had decided to permit shrine worship. The seminary students were outraged, and their last hope rested with the General Assembly. On September 10, 1938, as the General Assembly convened, Japanese police officers carrying swords surrounded the venue, intimidating pastors and elders into approving shrine worship. Park Gwan-jun, Pastor Joo Ki-cheol, and other believers resisted, but the Japanese authorities had already imprisoned the key dissenters beforehand.

The night before the Assembly, while imprisoned, Park Gwan-jun prayed and saw another vision: a communion table appeared before him, but it suddenly overturned, spilling its white rice, which became dirty in the soil. This led him to sense that the Assembly’s decision would end in failure, and he mourned deeply.

After being released, Park met Ahn Yi-suk, who had resigned from her teaching post at Seoncheon Girls’ School for refusing shrine worship. Together, they resolved to travel to Japan to speak directly to Japanese Christian leaders about the injustice and to seek support for the Korean church. On March 24, 1939, during a session of the Imperial Diet (Chōteikai) where a new religious law was under debate, Park Gwan-jun suddenly stood in the gallery and cried out, “This is the great mission from the Lord God!” He then hurled a large envelope containing a written warning for Japan into the assembly below. The chamber erupted in chaos, and they were immediately arrested and jailed.

After about forty days in a Japanese prison under close watch, the three returned to Korea. Park Gwan-jun was soon arrested again, tortured severely, and spent six years in prison. After his release, he devoted himself to fasting and prayer but ultimately passed away. His final words were, “Do not bow to shrine worship. Keep your faith in Jesus with all your heart. Our country will become as the word of Isaiah 11:10–16 says. Hold firmly to your faith until the end — and let us meet again in the glorious kingdom of heaven.”

  1. Lee, Hyo-deok 이효덕 李孝德

  1. Birth/Death: 1895/01/24 (Pyongannam-do, Yonggang) ~ 1978/09/15 (Seoul)

  2. Education: Born into a wealthy yangban (noble) family, she and her entire family converted to Christianity when she was just 5 years old. Thanks to her parents’ strong emphasis on education, despite being the daughter of a yangban family, she attended missionary-founded schools like Jungjin Elementary School and Sungeui Girls’ Middle School. After graduating from middle school in 1911, she became involved in missionary work and, while actively participating, taught subjects such as science and Korean history at Sungeui Girls’ Middle School where she attended.

  3. Contribution. A renewed focus on Lee Hyo-deok in the 1930s highlights her unwavering dedication to the temperance movement, women’s education, and the promotion of economic self-reliance, especially through rural development. In 1929, she stepped into the role of secretary-general for the Korean Christian Women’s Temperance Union. She traveled nationwide delivering lectures that drew audiences in the thousands. That same year, when the Japanese authorities arrested leaders of Geunwoo-hoe, a women’s nationalist group, Son Me-rye — who was both a member of Geunwoo-hoe and the Union’s traveling secretary-general — was forced to resign. This led to Lee Hyo-deok, then a teacher at Gijeon Girls’ School in Jeonju, being unexpectedly appointed to fill the post. She had already demonstrated strong leadership through her work with the Taehwa Women’s Association and the YWCA.

Lee worked tirelessly to establish new Temperance Union chapters across the country, emphasizing their importance so strongly that she declared, “A church that does not have a Temperance Union cannot be called a church at all.” Thanks to these efforts, the Temperance Union grew rapidly — from just six local branches with about 1,500 members at its founding to 53 chapters with over 3,200 members by 1928, and eventually reaching 116 chapters by 1934.

She also led an active campaign against state-regulated prostitution, arguing for the liberation of women trapped in such work. By the 1930s, the Christian-led temperance movement had grown beyond its church-based beginnings into a national campaign that involved the general public. With urbanization fueling the spread of vice and prostitution even into rural communities, Lee’s work to abolish prostitution became a broader women’s enlightenment effort, encouraging women to protect their own dignity and moral integrity.

From 1933, she was convinced that teaching temperance needed to start early, so she founded “Children’s Temperance Clubs” and “Youth and Girls’ Temperance Clubs” to instill these values from a young age. In a New Year’s message published on January 1, 1934, she outlined her goals: to educate Korean women and girls on the risks of abortion; to carry on the abstinence movement; to support agriculture and raise productivity; to recruit new members for the Temperance Union regardless of gender or nationality if they agreed with its mission; to expand youth clubs for continued temperance education; to inspire parents to raise their children responsibly; and to stage at least twelve public temperance marches each year to shape public opinion.

Despite colonial repression, she continued her lecture tours until her final presentation on April 15, 1936, in Ongjin, Hwanghae Province. Due to declining health and a six-month suspension from lecturing, she eventually had to stop and stepped down as secretary-general of the Temperance Union in 1937.

  1. Choi, Yong-sin 최용신 崔容信

  1. Birth/Death: 1909/08/12 (Hamgyongnam-do, Deokwon-gun) ~ 1935/01/23 (Gyeonggi-do, Suwon)

  2. Education: Her hometown, Deokwon, had a long history as an early site for Christianity and modern schools. Her grandfather, an education reformer, established a local school with his own resources, and her father was also committed to education. Raised in a modernized, well-off household, Choi Yong-shin attended Sunday school from an early age, which nurtured her Christian faith. She first studied at a private school in her village, then transferred in 1918 to Lucy Girls’ Ordinary School in Wonsan, operated by American Methodist women missionaries. After completing that program, she went on to Lucy Girls’ High Ordinary School, where her concern for rural life and social issues grew stronger. In her writings from that time, she questioned whether young women with an education should simply long for urban comforts or instead “join hands and run to the countryside to wipe out illiteracy.”

In 1928, she graduated top of her class and enrolled at Hapchung Women’s Theological School. There, she met Professor Hwang Ae-deok, a nationalist and former teacher at Pyongyang Sungui Girls’ School, who had led the underground group Songjukhoe and been jailed for her role in the Korean Patriotic Women’s Association. The principal at the time, missionary A.B. Chaffin, who was deeply concerned about rural poverty, launched a new “Rural Leadership Training Course” and entrusted its direction to Hwang Ae-deok upon her return from studying in the U.S.

Although she lived only to the age of 25, Choi Yong-shin left a remarkable legacy as an educator and champion of the rural enlightenment movement. She did not finish her theological studies but, supported by the YWCA, moved to Samgol (now Bonodong, Sangnok-gu, Ansan) in Banwol-myeon, Hwaseong-gun, Gyeonggi Province on October 10, 1931. When she arrived, she prayed that her whole life would be used up to light a path for the village. She began by gathering children and teaching them at Cheongok Training Center, attached to Cheongok Church.

At first, the villagers misunderstood her efforts and treated her coldly, but her commitment paid off — by May 1932, the center received official recognition. Student numbers steadily grew, so she held morning and afternoon classes, then offered evening sessions for local women, covering literacy, arithmetic, sewing, and handicrafts. Hoping to build a larger facility, she organized a special Chuseok event, the “Hangeawi Feast.” On that day, children performed songs, dances, and plays they had prepared, delighting parents and neighbors. Moved by what they saw, residents immediately formed the “Cheongok Academy Building Committee” and started construction just five days later on land donated by a local elder. Four months later, in January 1933, they held the academy’s dedication ceremony.

Samgol gradually transformed into a more vibrant, cooperative community. Women’s and youth groups were created to promote better living standards and household industries, laying the groundwork for shared prosperity. When the village initiative took hold, Yong-shin felt that more knowledge and new ideas were needed for the movement to spark change in other rural areas too, so she went to Japan to study. However, within six months she fell seriously ill. Though she had planned to recuperate at home, villagers begged her to stay, promising she would not have to work — only be with them. She returned but was soon faced with the YWCA cutting all support for her work. She appealed for help, but few responded.

In 1935, at just 25 years old, she passed away. Yet her story lives on through the novel Sangnoksoo and its film adaptation, which continue to remind people of the realities and significance of the Korean rural enlightenment movement.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

YMCA and YWCA

The Korean student YMCA movement experienced a transformative period beginning around 1929, coinciding with the Gwangju Student Movement, when ideological and cultural challenges began to reshape the organization’s identity and purpose. During this time, Christian schools witnessed a significant increase in non-religious students, leading to a corresponding surge in associate members among non-religious students within the student YMCA. This demographic shift fundamentally altered the organization’s character, as the influx of members who did not share the group’s foundational Christian values led to some neglect of the youth group’s traditional responsibilities and spiritual focus.

The changing membership composition occurred alongside broader social upheavals that further complicated the student YMCA’s mission. Disruptive student behavior began emerging as a pressing social issue, while socialist movements actively opposed religious organizations, creating ideological confusion within Christian youth groups. These anti-religious movements posed direct challenges to the YMCA’s Christian foundation, demanding new strategies to maintain relevance and effectiveness in an increasingly secular and politically charged environment.

In response to these mounting pressures, student Christian leaders recognized the urgent need to counteract anti-religious influences and strengthen their organizational identity. They identified several key objectives: reconciling faith with science, rationalizing religious belief, reviving sacrificial religious spirit, and actively resisting the prevailing pleasure-seeking culture that they saw as undermining Christian values. These goals reflected a sophisticated understanding of the intellectual and cultural challenges facing Christian youth in a rapidly modernizing Korea.

The crystallization of these concerns led to concrete action during the Korea Student Christian Youth Association Presidents’ Conference held in Seongbuk-dong, Seoul, in March 1929. This gathering proved pivotal in establishing new directions for the movement, as attendees passed comprehensive resolutions designed to intensify devotion among members and address the organizational challenges they faced. The resolutions aimed to unify both mind and ideology among members, promote abstinence from alcohol and tobacco, encourage self-control regarding worldly pleasures, foster proper understanding of Christianity, and strengthen individual and collective faith. These measures represented a deliberate effort to reassert Christian identity in the face of secular and socialist influences.

Beyond policy changes, organizational innovations emerged to provide more focused guidance for student youth groups. Leaders established what they called “Inner Circle” organizations designed to offer internal direction and support. A prominent example was the “Student Youth Friendship League,” founded in Seoul in 1928 under the inspiring slogan “Carry the Cross.” This league consisted of approximately twenty Christian students from specialized schools throughout the city and was led by Choi Bong-chik, a notable figure in the YMCA student movement and member of the Su-yang Club. When the Christian Cooperative Front was established, the Student Youth Friendship League appeared to join this broader coalition, which advocated for the popularization and practical realization of Christianity as an alternative to Marxist ideology.

The emergence of “Christianity” as a distinct ideological framework became a defining theme in the student Christian movement, representing a conscious effort to articulate a Christian social philosophy that could compete with other contemporary ideologies. This development built upon earlier initiatives such as the Christian Rural Research Association and reflected the movement’s growing sophistication in addressing social and political questions from a Christian perspective.

The question of organizational identity reached a critical juncture during the 4th Korea Student Christian Youth Association Joint Presidents’ Conference, held from August 21 to 27, 1930, at Naegumgang Jangansa. This conference featured extensive discussions on spirituality, ideology, economic movements, and contemporary social issues, demonstrating the movement’s engagement with the full spectrum of concerns facing Korean youth. Particularly significant were the heated debates surrounding “socialism” and the “rational problem,” which revealed the fundamental tensions within the student youth movement regarding its proper role and philosophical orientation.

This same conference marked an important institutional milestone with the official establishment of the Korea Student Christian Youth Association Federation as the unified organization representing both student YMCA and YWCA groups. The federation assumed responsibility as the representative body for the student movement, electing an executive committee of ten members—five male and five female—to oversee major activities including the Presidential Assembly, Joint Presidents’ Conference, World Prayer Day, and external affairs. This structural development reflected the movement’s growing organizational maturity and its commitment to gender equality within Christian leadership.

The federation’s activities continued to expand and evolve, as demonstrated by the 5th Joint Presidents’ Conference held from August 22 to 28, 1931, at Sambongsan Doseonsa. This gathering attracted representatives from twenty-three schools, with over fifty male and female students participating in discussions covering faith issues, social science questions, gender concerns, rural work initiatives, and youth organization activities. The breadth and depth of these discussions illustrated the movement’s comprehensive approach to addressing the challenges facing Korean Christian youth.

However, the federation’s promising trajectory faced an abrupt and devastating interruption with the outbreak of the Manchurian Incident in September 1931. The Japanese authorities’ immediate response included imposing severe restrictions on student organizations, creating unexpected and insurmountable challenges for the federation’s joint activities. These limitations affected not only social action initiatives but also the basic ability to hold joint conferences, effectively crippling the organization’s core functions and communication networks.

The impact of these restrictions became apparent in 1932 when the federation was forced to suspend its activities entirely for a full year. Although efforts were made to revive the organization, the 6th Joint Presidents’ Conference held at Uijeongbu Hoeryongsa in August 1933 proved to be the federation’s final major gathering. The combination of changing internal and external circumstances, including growing tensions within the broader Christian community, contributed to the significant decline in the student federation’s influence and effectiveness.

The movement’s decline accelerated as key leadership figures departed for other roles within the church structure. By 1935, Lee Dae-wi, who had been instrumental in managing the federation’s student work, had shifted to different positions within the church hierarchy. The student YMCA movement suffered another crucial blow when Shin Hyung-woo, who had served as interim representative of Lee Seung-man, resigned from his position as YMCA secretary due to complications arising from the Active Faith Movement in 1935. These leadership changes left the organization without experienced guidance at a critical moment in its development.

The final phase of the Korean student YMCA movement’s decline unfolded within the broader context of Japanese colonial policy. In 1938, the Korea YMCA Federation was absorbed into the Japanese YMCA system, effectively ending its independent existence and autonomous activities. This institutional absorption represented more than administrative reorganization; it marked the end of indigenous Korean Christian youth leadership and self-determination within the YMCA structure.

The movement’s complete dissolution came in 1941 when the remaining student YMCA-YWCA organizations, which had managed to maintain their individual school-based identities despite the broader federation’s demise, were forcibly integrated into the National Federation of Youth Organizations. This final administrative action eliminated the last vestiges of the independent Korean student Christian movement, bringing to a close a remarkable twelve-year period during which Korean Christian youth had attempted to navigate the complex challenges of maintaining religious identity, social engagement, and organizational autonomy under increasingly restrictive colonial rule.

Joseon Women’s Christian Temperance Union

The temperance movement, which had been active since the early 1920s, gained significant traction during the 1930s. This surge in activity contributed to either a decline or stabilization in the consumption of alcohol, tobacco, opium, and the prevalence of prostitution-related businesses. Key drivers of this progress were the efforts of the Korean Women’s Temperance Society and various church communities.

At the height of the movement, substantial drops or steadying trends were observed in the use of alcohol, tobacco, and opium, as well as in prostitution-related activities. Notably, alcohol consumption was closely tied to revenue from alcohol taxes. As the movement gathered steam — particularly after the Women’s Temperance Society’s increased involvement and Tinning’s arrival in 1923 — alcohol consumption fell by roughly 20 percent. For example, while revenue from alcohol taxes rose from 3,689.2 million yen in 1921 to 4,252.5 million yen in 1922, it dropped to 3,439.3 million yen in 1923. In the early 1930s, when the Temperance Movement Association was launched, a similar downward trend continued for three consecutive years. Likewise, tobacco tax revenue — which included the sales tax — was 8.22 million yen in 1921, dropped to 6.995 million yen in 1922, and fell further to 6.509 million yen in 1923. By 1931, tobacco tax revenue stood at 23.871 million yen, but this declined to 17.893 million yen in 1932, 19.98 million yen in 1933, and 18.851 million yen in 1934.

The March 1st Movement of 1919 also brought the opium issue to the forefront. This led to dramatic changes in the following years. The area under opium cultivation shrank dramatically from 2,308.2 acres in 1919 to just 94.7 acres by 1920, and production similarly dropped from 2,022.94 kilograms to only 41.56 kilograms. Although production began to rise again from 1921 onward, it never returned to the 1919 levels, underscoring the impact of the anti-opium activism that accompanied the independence movement. Japanese opium output peaked in 1919 before sharply declining. In terms of the sex industry, there were 72,762 Korean hostesses and establishments in 1920, alongside 6,974 Japanese. By 1925, the Korean figure had fallen to 62,151, while the Japanese figure stayed around 7,050. This decline continued, with Korean numbers dropping to 55,187 in 1930 and to 50,604 by 1935, while the number of Japanese in the industry increased to 7,473 and then to 9,880 over the same period. This pattern mirrored the intensification of the nationwide campaign for temperance, spearheaded by the YWCA, the Temperance Movement Association, and the Women’s Temperance Society. Interestingly, while Korean participation steadily declined, the number of Japanese in the same sector rose.

Although multiple explanations could be proposed for the drop in alcohol and tobacco use, opium production, and sex work, the Christian temperance movement attributed these trends directly to its efforts. Periods of notable reduction — particularly around 1923–24 and again in 1932 — aligned with peaks in temperance activities. The gradual decline in opium cultivation and prostitution from about 1920 onward is likewise linked to this Christian-led activism.

In addition, measures like the Minor’s Prohibition and Minor’s Abstinence Acts were pursued. In September 1929, a coalition comprising the General Assembly of Elders, the North and South Presbyterian Churches, and the Korean Sunday School formed the “Alliance for the Implementation of the Minor’s Alcohol and Smoking Prohibition Act.” This group, headquartered at the main church in Keijo (Seoul), promoted the physical and spiritual well-being of the younger generation through these commitments:

Prioritizing the physical and spiritual welfare of youth.

Rationalizing and expanding temperance activities.

Avoiding locations where violations might occur during student gatherings and addressing any infractions afterward.

Conducting confiscations only when clear violations occurred.

Separately, at the First Eastern, Central, and Western Conferences on temperance held in June 1931 by the Korean Presbyterian Church, the Temperance Department passed resolutions concerning youth. These included refusing to hire teachers for primary departments in church-run schools if they were found to be non-compliant and integrating lessons on abstinence from alcohol and tobacco into Bible study curricula. They also vowed to intensify campaigns for abstinence among students.

Japanese authorities, however, were reluctant to enforce the Minor’s Abstinence Act, partly for political reasons. The Christian temperance advocates nevertheless continued to press for its adoption, even proposing community-led self-enforcement. In Japan, a Minor’s Smoking Prohibition Act and a Minor’s Abstinence Act had already been passed in 1899 and 1922 respectively, and there were calls to raise the legal definition of a minor to age 25 under the influence of temperance efforts. By contrast, these debates were largely neglected in Korea.

Finally, in March 1938, the Japanese government enacted and issued enforcement orders for the “Minor’s Abstinence from Smoking Act” and “Minor’s Abstinence from Drinking Act.” These laws can be viewed as further outcomes of the persistent temperance movement. Although more comprehensive measures such as a full Abstinence Act, a ban on opium cultivation, or the abolition of prostitution were not achieved, these laws represented the most feasible alternatives at the time.

Korean Student Association of North American 北美留學生總會

Before 1910, only about thirty Korean students were studying in the United States. However, after the March 1st Movement, their numbers began to grow, sparking a push to bring these students together under a single network. The main organization that fostered this unity was the Korean Student Association.

The Korean Student Association operated with backing from the North American YMCA’s Overseas Student Relations Committee and published an English-language monthly, The Korean Student Bulletin, from December 1922 until April 1940. As the largest student group spanning multiple states and institutions, the Association played a leading role in organizing and representing Korean students in America. Its influence extended internationally, too — the Association sent representatives to gatherings like the 1932 International Student Aid Conference in Buffalo and the 1935 Oriental Student Conference in Chicago, where they sought to draw global attention to Korea’s situation.

Many of the Association’s leaders in the 1920s and 1930s were practicing Christians. When they returned to Korea, they often took on leadership positions in education, the economy, and other areas, becoming key figures in both Christian circles and nationalist movements. In the U.S., they built networks centered mostly in the East and Midwest, frequently with churches providing support. Publishing was a major part of their work: in addition to The Korean Student Bulletin, they produced a Korean-language periodical called 우라키 (The Rocky). These publications introduced Korean students to Christianity, encouraged moral and religious education, and kept American Christians informed about Korea.

Their magazines covered an array of subjects — from theology and philosophy to education, social and natural sciences, economics, and literature — along with news from Korea and the U.S. They also described their own experiences studying overseas, indirectly promoting the idea of studying in America to future students.

The Korean Student Association also addressed political matters, including the relationship between the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea and the Korean Provisional Government. At their meetings, they adopted resolutions recognizing the Shanghai Provisional Government as the legitimate authority for the independence cause, supported population taxes and arms purchases, and endorsed the government’s leadership. In 1925, however, they passed a resolution backing the Provisional Government’s dissolution, revealing internal disagreements among Korean students in America at the time.

Throughout the 1930s, the Association continued to engage in debates and resolutions on the independence movement and the status of the Provisional Government in Shanghai, showing its ongoing commitment to Korea’s fight for sovereignty. By bringing Korean students together, publishing influential material, and participating in international forums, the Korean Student Association made a meaningful contribution to building solidarity among Korean students overseas and raising global awareness of Korea’s struggle.

The Commemoration Activities of 50th Anniversary of the Presbyterian and Methodist Church

In 1934-35, both the Presbyterian Church and the Methodist Church held commemorations under the name “Jubilee” to mark the 50th anniversary of missionary arrivals in Korea. These celebrations specifically honored the 50th anniversary of Korean missions for the United Presbyterian Church in North America and the Board of Missions for the Methodist Episcopal Church in the United States.

The Methodist Church’s commemorative planning was facilitated by the 1930 union between the Southern and Northern Methodist Churches, which had resulted in the formation of Kimla Church. This organizational change made arranging their commemorative events more manageable than it might otherwise have been.

The Methodist commemorative efforts encompassed spiritual, material, and historical initiatives. For their spiritual activities, they planned revival meetings, arranged for the publication of the Daedunhoe magazine, and organized lecture series. Their material goals included raising 200,000 won as the church’s basic fund by 1938. For historical documentation, they compiled records in both Korean and English, publishing papers and pamphlets about their missionary achievements, and organized Jubilee commemorative events.

The Methodist celebrations began in June 1934 with events including lectures and a play depicting the history of Methodist missionary work, starting at Jungdong Church. They culminated their year-long commemoration on April 21, 1935, with a nationwide 50th-anniversary worship service.

The Presbyterian Church and the United Presbyterian Church conducted their commemorative activities separately. In June 1934, they held the “50th Anniversary Pastor’s Conference” and engaged in extensive evangelism efforts following the conference.

However, the Presbyterian Church’s commemorative activities did not continue beyond 1935. This was due to several issues that arose within the church: the “passport problem,” the “Genesis: Moses Authorship dispute,” the “Abingdon Bible Commentary issue,” the “New Hymnals issue,” and the “Christian Century Magazine problem.” These various conflicts created theological disagreements between progressive and conservative factions within the Presbyterian Church, as well as disputes between Northwestern and non-Northwestern denominations, resulting in ecclesiastical disagreements that affected their ability to continue the commemorative activities.

The History of Christianity in Korea 2 (2021)

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The Search for Koreanized Christianity

As mentioned earlier, both Kim Gyo-sin and Seongseo Joseon were influenced by Uchimura Kanzo’s Non-Church Movement, but their ultimate goals were not Non-Church Movement itself. Non-Church Movement served as a means to establish ‘Korean Christianity.’ Just as Uchimura was a ‘nationalist faith person’ in Japan, Kim Gyo-sin was a ‘nationalist faith person’ in Korea. In the founding principles of Seongseo Joseon, they emphasized “Korean people with Korean souls” and expressed their desire to “give Korea the Bible to establish its framework and create its essence” and to “broadly and deeply study Korea to establish an eternal new Korea on the foundation of the Bible.”

Choi Tae-yong was also influenced by Uchimura Kanzo but held a different ideological stance. He initiated the ‘Spiritual Christianity’ movement within the institutionalized churches, but he faced criticism from conservative theologians and the institutionalized churches. In response, on December 22, 1935, in Seoul’s Sogyeok-dong, he founded the ‘Christianity of Korea Gospel Church’ as the ‘third church’ and announced the Three Major Principles, emphasizing the nature and direction of “Gospel-centered, life-centered faith/academic theology/Korean people’s own church.” This move was rooted in a critique of the institutionalized churches that relied on the support of foreign missionary organizations. He argued that a Western-dependent attitude hindered the development of Korean faith.
