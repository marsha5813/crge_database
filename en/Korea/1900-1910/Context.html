Korea: Context (1900-1910)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. Summary of regulations on religion in this decade

Following Japan’s establishment of diplomatic control over Korea through the Second Japan-Korea Agreement in November 1905, the colonial administration began implementing systematic religious regulations. The foundational policy document, the “Regulations on Religious Proclamation” (Governor-General Ordinance No. 45), was promulgated in November 1906 and remained the primary instrument for managing Korean religions until the implementation of the “Proselytizing Rules” in 1915.1

Primary Religious Regulation: Rules Regarding the Proclamation of Religion (1906)

Legal Framework and Text

The regulations established a comprehensive approval system for all religious activities. Below is the complete text with English translation:

종교의 선포에 관한 규칙 (Rules Regarding the Proclamation of Religion)

  • Article 1: Denominational or sectarian groups belonging to Shinto, Buddhism, or other religions within the Empire seeking to conduct missionary work must designate a Korean representative. This representative must submit a resume along with documentation covering: (1) propagation methods, and (2) supervision methods for propagators. Approval must be obtained from the Resident-General through the supervising consul.

  • Article 2: Imperial subjects (excluding those covered in Article 1) wishing to engage in religious proclamation must prepare documentation regarding the religion’s name and propagation methods, attach a resume, and obtain Resident-General approval through the supervising consul.

  • Article 3: Establishing temples, halls, synagogues, preaching venues, or lecture halls for religious purposes requires prior approval from the supervising consul in the intended location. Required documentation includes: (1) name and location, (2) religious denomination, and (3) management and maintenance procedures.

  • Article 4: Managers of denominations, propagators mentioned in Article 2, or any imperial subjects seeking to oversee Korean temples must attach necessary documents and obtain Resident-General approval through the local supervising consul.

  • Article 5: Any modifications to previously approved matters require renewed approval.

  • Article 6: Denominational managers or propagators must report the names and qualifications of their propagators to the supervising consul, including any subsequent changes to personnel.

Regulatory Significance and Colonial Strategy

This regulation served two fundamental colonial objectives: religious categorization within the legal framework and systematic registration procedures for Japanese religious organizations as tools for Korean religious assimilation.

The categorization system divided religions into Shintoism (Sect-Shinto), Buddhism, and “other religions,” based on Japanese precedents including 太政官布達 No. 19 (1884) and Article 28 of the Constitution of the Empire of Japan (1889). These earlier Japanese laws recognized Shintoism, Buddhism, and Christianity as official religions while classifying folk and new religions as pseudo-religions. Confucianism and State Shintoism were designated as non-religious—Confucianism serving as moral philosophy for public education, while State Shintoism functioned as supreme national ideology that religious practice could not transcend.

The registration system targeted Japanese religious organizations and their missionary activities in Korea. Japanese settlements had been governed under the Resident Community Law (居留民團法) since March 1905, establishing structured communities with internal governance that facilitated colonial administration2. Building on this foundation, the religious regulations focused on organizational establishment and worker registration, including buildings and worship facilities. Japan implemented the Ministry of Home Affairs Ordinance No. 41 (1899: 内務省令第41号) and the regulations concerning the establishment of corporations for the purpose of religious proclamation or conducting religious ceremonies (1900: 宗教宣布又は宗教上儀式執行ヲ目的トスル法人ノ設立等ニ関スル規定) and enforced this control system through laws and regulations3. The rules regarding the proclamation of religion (1906) extended these controls to Japanese religions in Korea.

Additional Control Mechanisms

Security Regulations (1906)

The Japanese Resident-General implemented comprehensive Security Regulations in 1906 as part of broader control measures. These regulations authorized Japanese authorities to monitor, restrict, arrest, detain, and punish individuals engaging in activities deemed subversive, particularly targeting political, religious, or social organizations viewed as potential resistance sources5.

Press Control Laws (1907-1908)

The Newspaper Law of 1907, followed by the Gwangmu Newspaper Law and Revised Newspaper Law of 1908, restricted press freedom and prevented anti-Japanese sentiment dissemination. While the 1907 law could not effectively control foreign publishers like the Protestant Yeseugyo Sinbo and Catholic Gyeonghyang Sinmun, the 1908 revisions empowered the Minister of Home Affairs to ban newspapers published abroad or by foreigners if deemed disruptive to public order or morals. These laws significantly limited religious publications that might encourage Christian involvement in independence movements6.

Christian Educational Response and Growth

Protestant churches experienced rapid expansion with minimal indigenous resistance. King Gojong and Queen Min provided missionary protection, enabling connections with home country missionary organizations. This support continued through major conflicts including the Donghak Rebellion, Sino-Japanese War, and Russo-Japanese War, as indigenous populations recognized missionary institutions (churches, schools, hospitals) as beneficial forces. The middle and lower social classes, along with merchants, actively embraced Christianity, further accelerated by the Great Revival and Million Soul Movement.

Protestant churches led modernized education initiatives, with missionaries enthusiastically establishing schools. However, this development concerned Japan due to inter-denominational competition creating self-sustaining systems with significant social influence. From 1905-1910, missionary numbers doubled, and Christian population grew from 12,000 (1900) to 26,057 (1905), demonstrating annual growth rates exceeding 100% until 1910.7 Korean Bible distribution expanded rapidly through Korean colporteurs (Maeseoin 매서인 賣書人 or Gwonseoin 권서인 勸書人).

Japanese Investigation and Control Strategy

The primary concern was the lack of Japanese or colonial government control over Christian forces. Japan’s approach involved two main strategies:

  1. Internal Investigation and Surveillance: A 1908 survey of Christian proselytization gathered statistics on missionary numbers, Korean congregation sizes, and activity scales. Officials approached foreign missionaries cautiously, explaining they were dispatched to dispel rumors and misunderstandings per central government orders while expressing interest in proselytization status. They considered charitable donations of 10-20 yen per institution to achieve “emotional harmony,” proposing a total budget of 50,000 yen.9

  2. Educational Institution Control: Despite Korean Christians’ independence aspirations, missionaries received instructions from home churches to distance themselves from Korean politics. In 1901, the Korean Protestant Church adopted non-interference policies. The colonial government, following Japanese Christian control laws from 1899, issued regulations including Governor-General’s Decree No. 45 (1906) on missionary activities and Ordinance No. 62 (1908) on private schools.

Missionary Negotiations and Implementation

Early ordinance versions were negotiated with missionaries. According to Samuel Moffett’s 1908 report, the General Council of Protestant Evangelical Missions in Korea held multiple meetings with Japanese government starting in 1907, seeking British and American diplomatic understanding while discussing with Commissioner Zennei, educational authorities, and Mr. Sammons.10 The missionary committee provided information about their educational activities’ scale and nature, requesting government regulations consider their work. They sought recognition for their schools and graduates equivalent to government schools, provided they met or exceeded government standards. The committee explicitly stated Christianity should be taught in all their schools, receiving courteous treatment with meetings promising harmonious mutual understanding between government authorities and missionary organizations.

Japan promised religious freedom while requesting Christian schools adhere to public school standards. Missionaries responded with three points:

  1. School curricula would maintain public school standards with Christianity taught as foundation of ethics and religion

  2. Government should treat their schools and graduates equally with public schools and graduates, ensuring no inferiority

  3. To align with government regulations and policies, they requested information about public school regulations and curricula while providing necessary information about their schools and policies

The ordinance excluded religious education regulatory provisions and allowed private school recognition equal to public schools regarding staffing and higher education advancement. However, regulatory provisions remained, including ministerial approval for new school establishment and six-month deadline for existing school approval from Japanese government.

Comparative Analysis: Japanese vs. Korean Regulations

Key differences between Korean (1908) and Japanese (1899) private school ordinances:

  1. Additional Articles: Korea added Articles 2 and 4. Ambiguous expressions like “improper conduct” and “social order disruption” allowed subjective Japanese government interpretation.

  2. Supervisory Authority: Japan used local officials for private school oversight; Korea assigned direct ministerial supervision. Korean Minister of Education reviewed/approved textbooks and commanded local officials, with unique authority to prohibit classes.

  3. Annual Reporting: Article 12 required annual status reports. Korean private schools faced textbook inspections and approvals unlike Japanese counterparts, significantly impacting Christian schools where Bible served as textbook, potentially enabling authorities to ban Bible education.

  4. Approval Conditions: Korean approval conditions and principal qualifications were more restrictive and specific than Japan’s, potentially limiting approvals to government-favored individuals.

These differences highlight Japanese intent for greater control and influence over Korean education compared to Japan, particularly targeting Christian schools and potential Bible education regulation.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

The decade 1900-1910 marked Korea’s tragic transformation from an independent kingdom to a Japanese protectorate and ultimately a colony. Japan’s systematic political interference in Korea, which had intensified in the late 19th century, reached its culmination during this period. The weakening Joseon Dynasty attempted to preserve its sovereignty by carefully navigating the complex diplomatic tensions among neighboring powers—Russia, China, and Japan—while simultaneously seeking closer relations with the United States through missionary connections. However, these great powers ultimately pursued their own national interests at Korea’s expense.

Diplomatic Relationships with Asian-Pacific Countries

The Treaty System: Japan’s Gradual Annexation of Korea

Japan employed a systematic approach to Korean colonization through a series of unequal treaties that progressively stripped Korea of its sovereignty. This process represented one of the most methodical examples of colonial expansion in East Asian history.

Japan-Korea Protocol of 190411

Background: When Joseon declared neutrality between Russia and Japan in January 1904, escalating conflicts over Manchuria and the Korean Peninsula led to diplomatic severance and war in February 1904. Japan unilaterally imposed this alliance under the guise of mutual defense, secretly concluded on February 23, 1904, between Lee Ji-yong (Acting Korean Foreign Minister) and Hayashi Gonsuke (Japanese Minister to Korea).

Key Articles:

  • Article I: “For the purpose of maintaining a permanent and solid friendship between Japan and Korea and firmly establishing peace in the Far East, the Imperial Government of Korea shall place full confidence in the Imperial Government of Japan and adopt the advice of the latter in regard to improvements in administration.”

  • Article II: “The Imperial Government of Japan shall in a spirit of firm friendship ensure the safety and repose of the Imperial House of Korea.”

  • Article III: “The Imperial Government of Japan definitively guarantee the independence and territorial integrity of the Korean Empire.”

  • Article IV: “In case the welfare of the Imperial House of Korea or the territorial integrity of Korea is endangered by aggression of a third Power or internal disturbances, the Imperial Government of Japan shall immediately take such necessary measures as the circumstances require… The Imperial Government of Japan may, for the attainment of the above-mentioned object, occupy, when the circumstances require it, such places as may be necessary from strategical points of view.”

  • Article V: “The Governments of the two countries shall not in future, without mutual consent, conclude with a third Power such an arrangement as may be contrary to the principles of the present Protocol.”

The Second Japan-Korea Treaty (November 17, 1905)12

Background: Emperor Gojong initially opposed this treaty that would eliminate Korea’s diplomatic independence. Japan responded by dispatching General Ito Hirobumi, who individually interrogated ministers about their positions while surrounding the royal palace with troops. Despite Gojong’s absence from the meeting, five of eight ministers voted in favor under coercion.

Key Articles:

  • Article I: “The Government of Japan, through the Department of Foreign Affairs at Tokyo, will hereafter have control and direction of the external relations and affairs of Korea, and the diplomatic and consular representatives of Japan will have the charge of the subjects and interests of Korea in foreign countries.”

  • Article II: “The Government of Japan undertake to see to the execution of the treaties actually existing between Korea and other Powers, and the Government of Korea engage not to conclude hereafter any act or engagement having an international character except through the medium of the Government of Japan.”

  • Article III: “The Government of Japan shall be represented at the Court of His Majesty the Emperor of Korea by a Resident General, who shall reside at Seoul, primarily for the purpose of taking charge of and directing matters relating to diplomatic affairs.”

The Third Japan-Korea Treaty (July 24, 1907)13

Background: Following Emperor Gojong’s secret dispatch of envoys Lee Sang-seol, Lee Jun, and Lee Wi-jong to the Second Hague Peace Conference in June 1907, Japan retaliated by forcing Gojong’s abdication. Despite active support from American missionary Homer Hulbert in publicizing Japan’s invasion through Western media, the Korean envoys were largely ignored by participating nations, leaving Korea increasingly isolated.

Key Provisions:

Korean government must receive Resident-General guidance on administrative reforms

Laws and important administrative measures require prior Resident-General approval

Judicial affairs separated from general administration

High-ranking official appointments require Resident-General consent

Korean government must appoint Japanese nationals recommended by Resident-General

Foreign employment prohibited without Resident-General consent

The 1907 Hague Secret Emissary Affair

The Korean diplomatic mission to The Hague originated from imperial rivalries following Japan’s victory in the First Sino-Japanese War (1895). Russian interference with Japanese expansion created competing influences over Korea, as both powers sought dominance while Japan advocated Korean independence from Russia to establish its own control.

When tensions escalated toward the Russo-Japanese War, Tsar Nicholas II assured Emperor Gojong of Korean independence and neutrality in December 1903 (Song, 2004). Based on these promises, Gojong declared neutrality through Minister Geren at the Chinese Embassy on January 21, 1904. However, Japan’s surprise attack on the Russian vessel Koreets near Incheon on February 8, 1904, immediately violated Korean neutrality. Japanese forces occupied Seoul within 24 hours, using the war to formalize control through successive treaties: the Japan-Korea Treaty (February 23, 1904), Treaty of Portsmouth (September 5, 1905), and the coercive Protectorate Treaty (November 18, 1905).

Throughout this period, Gojong secretly supported Russia and challenged the Protectorate Treaty’s legitimacy, arguing it lacked validity since he had not signed it. While Tsar Nicholas II initially encouraged Western powers to protest Japanese actions, Russia’s defeat weakened its position. The Portsmouth Treaty’s prohibition on Russian interference with Japanese “guidance and supervision” in Korea created legal barriers to opposition. The Protectorate Treaty’s Article 2 allowed Japan to “interfere with Korean sovereignty in consultation with the Korean government,” while Portsmouth’s Article 2 prevented Russian intervention, creating an diplomatic impasse.

Unaware of Russia’s policy shift through secret negotiations with Japan, formalized in the Russo-Japanese Agreement (July 30, 1907), Gojong viewed the Hague Peace Conference as an opportunity to restore sovereignty. When Korean representatives attempted to engage Conference Chairman Nelidov, they were refused access as only officially invited delegations could participate.

Three envoys arrived in The Hague on June 25, 1907: Lee Sang-seol (official leader), Lee Jun (carrying Gojong’s authorization), and Lee Wi-jong (spokesman and interpreter). Hulbert had departed Seoul earlier on May 8, 1907, with imperial recommendations, though Japanese Foreign Minister Hayashi monitored his activities closely. Excluded from official proceedings, the delegation pursued alternative strategies. On June 27, they distributed a French-language appeal to Chairman Nelidov, representatives, and journalists, achieving publication in the “Peace Conference Bulletin” on June 30.

The delegation’s significant success came through participation in an international journalists’ meeting organized by British correspondent Stead on July 9. They presented “A Plea for Korea,” resulting in unanimous support for a resolution stating: “While Japan speaks of peace, how can peace exist for people under machine gun threats? Far Eastern peace cannot be achieved until Korean independence and freedom are realized” (The Independent, August 22, 1907).

Despite these efforts, major powers rejected Korean appeals due to existing agreements: the US Taft-Katsura Agreement (July 29, 1905) and British Second Anglo-Japanese Alliance (August 12, 1905) had already acknowledged Japanese authority over Korea. Mission planning involved coordination with Korean youth organizations, particularly Sangdonghoe, with key figures including Jeon Deok-gi and Yi Hoi-yeong (Song, 1980). Choi Nam-seon later reflected that “Sangdong Church’s back room served as the planning center for the Hulbert mission event” (Korean Christian Youth Movement History, 1978).

This coordination emerged from broader resistance following the 1905 Protectorate Treaty, including nationwide anti-treaty movements with strong youth participation through Sangdonghoe, involving prayer meetings, demonstrations, and efforts to nullify the treaty. While the mission failed to achieve international recognition or prevent Japanese colonial control, it demonstrated Korean commitment to independence and established precedents for future resistance movements.
