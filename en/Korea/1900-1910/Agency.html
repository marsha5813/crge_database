Korea: Agency (1900-1910)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Gil Seon-ju (길선주, 吉善宙)

Gil Seon-ju emerged as one of Korea’s most influential charismatic Christian leaders, whose impact continues to resonate within Korean Christianity today. His path to Christianity was marked by personal struggle and spiritual searching. Born into poverty, Gil began working as an herb merchant at fourteen, traveling between markets for four years. Following repeated business failures, he entered the Anguksa monastery in 1892, dedicating three years to rigorous Zen Buddhist meditation and prayer. However, this intensive practice permanently damaged his eyesight, leading to his disappointed departure from Buddhism.

During this period of adversity, Gil encountered Samuel Moffett, an American missionary whose compassionate counseling and spiritual guidance proved transformative. Kim Jong-Sup provided Gil with a copy of Pilgrim’s Progress, encouraging him to discern biblical truth through prayer. Through extended prayer sessions during late night and early morning hours, Gil experienced a profound spiritual awakening, finally able to call God “Father” while recognizing himself as a sinner. This transformative experience became the foundation for his later development of early-morning prayer practices.

Following his conversion, Gil actively evangelized among his family and friends, achieving considerable success. After his baptism in 1895 and ordination as an elder in 1901, he abandoned his prosperous medical practice to become a full-time evangelist. The most significant event of Gil’s ministry occurred in 1907 when his fervent prayer, candid public confession, and passionate preaching inspired the Great Revival that began in his church. This revival marked the first nation-wide evangelization campaign in Korea and established Gil’s reputation as both a gifted orator and a person of exceptional spiritual qualities.

Gil’s innovations in Christian practice proved lasting. He was instrumental in developing tongsong gido, a form of communal prayer where participants speak their prayers in unison, which had been initiated during the Welsh Revival and became established in Korean churches through the Pyongyang Great Revival. Additionally, Gil organized the first women’s evangelist group and established Christian schools, thereby advancing women’s status in Korean society while instilling nationalistic ideals in students.

  1. Yun Chi-ho (윤치호, 尹致昊)

Yun Chi-ho’s contributions to Korean Christianity must be understood within the complex context of his later controversial pro-Japanese activities in the 1920s and 1930s. Nevertheless, his significant contributions to national reform and youth education through Christian philosophy during Korea’s enlightenment period remain historically important.

Born into a prominent Yangban family, Yun received traditional Confucian education before joining the “Gentlemen’s Excursion Party” to Japan in 1881 at age seventeen. This experience aligned him with the Progressive Party, which inherited the reform impulse of Silhak scholars. His involvement in the Gapsin coup resulted in political exile to China, where he first encountered Christianity. At the Anglo-Chinese College in Shanghai, under the influence of Young J. Allen and other American professors, Yun experienced deep religious conversion in 1887, subsequently traveling to the United States to study theology at Vanderbilt University.

Returning to Korea in 1895 at King Gojong’s request to facilitate governmental reform, Yun co-founded the Independence Club with Philip Jaisohn and served as co-editor of The Independent newspaper. His Christian faith informed his reform activities across several key areas.

Yun championed the Korean language and national identity through his support of Hangul and Korean history. The Independent was published in both Hangul and English, elevating a script previously relegated to lower classes by Confucian scholars. Working with prominent Hangul editor Ju Si-kyong, Yun promoted Hangul studies and encouraged the use of Hangul Bibles among ordinary people. Through his establishment of Kwanghaksogwan, he published a hymn book titled Ch’anmiga, featuring twelve translated hymns and three versions of a Korean national anthem he composed. This integration of Christian hymns with Korean national identity reflected Yun’s belief in the unity of church and nation.

His educational philosophy emphasized practical skills alongside academic learning. As bishop in 1902, Yun advocated teaching Koreans “the preciousness of work,” arguing that a young man who could handle both plow and pen was more valuable than one who merely quoted Shakespeare or Spencer. He established a primary school in Wonsan focusing on gardening and orchard management, collaborating with Baptist missionary Rev. Malcolm Fenwick. His attempt to establish Hanyoung Academy in Songdo in 1906 reflected his vision of occupational education that prioritized individual development over mere industrial advancement.

Yun’s commitment to institutional development was demonstrated through his steadfast support of the Independence Club. Learning from Chinese experiences about the importance of sustained commitment to educational missions, Yun maintained his involvement with the Independence Club until its dissolution, even as Jaisohn departed after only two years.

  1. Ahn Chang-ho (안창호)

Dosan Ahn Chang-ho grew up near Pyongyang during a period of significant social upheaval, including the Donghak movement’s push for independent national reform. After receiving Confucian education, he attended Guse hakdang, an American Presbyterian school in Seoul that emphasized Christian fundamentals. His conversion to Christianity occurred under the influence of teacher Song Sun-myong.

Ahn’s commitment to enlightenment began through his participation in Philip Jaisohn’s debate classes at nearby Pai-jai school and his subsequent joining of the Independence Club in 1897. He played a significant leadership role until the club’s dissolution under external pressure. Following this setback, Ahn returned to Jaisohn’s fundamental principle that modern education formed the basis for building a truly independent Korea. He established Joemjin school in Gangso county, Pyongan-do, notably accepting both boys and girls as students in coeducational classes, a striking departure from contemporary convention.

His immigration to the United States in 1902 with his wife marked them as the first married Korean couple to arrive in America. As Korean immigration increased, reaching over 7,000 by 1905, the Japan-Korea Treaty stripped Korea of diplomatic sovereignty. In response, Ahn founded the United Korean Association in San Francisco with four charter members, eventually growing to 800 active members across nine western U.S. branches, with additional branches in Manchuria and Russia. This organization promoted national consciousness and the movement to restore Korean sovereignty, successfully raising funds to purchase a three-story headquarters building in San Francisco.

Returning to Korea in 1907, Ahn formed the secret New People’s Association, dedicated to disseminating concepts of self-reliance among the Korean people through education, Korean-owned publishing and industrial ventures, and youth organizations that connected traditional culture with modern educational opportunities. His establishment of Daesong school in Pyongyang aimed to prepare a new generation of Koreans to meet contemporary national challenges. The proposed chain of bookstores opened branches in Pyongyang and Seoul, while many independence activists received their education through Daesong school and Sinminhoe.

  1. Ahn Jung-geun (안중근)

Ahn Jung-geun’s Christian faith profoundly shaped both his moral development and his activism against injustice. Born to Buddhist parents, with a father who participated in the Gapshin Coup as an enlightenment party proponent, Ahn’s spiritual journey began when his father requested the establishment of a Catholic church in their town. Through Priest Wilhelm, who became his spiritual father, Ahn and his father were baptized in 1897, with Ahn receiving the baptismal name Thomas.

According to his autobiography, written while imprisoned in Lushun, Ahn understood Biblical and Christian doctrine primarily as narratives of justice. This understanding influenced his establishment of Catholic-based schools, including Samheung School and Don-Eui School, as well as his broader educational movement. However, his activism created tension with Catholic Church leadership, which maintained an apolitical stance following serious persecutions and the Catholic Composition Agreement of 1899. After unsuccessful attempts to establish a university with church support, Ahn recognized the importance of autonomous independence from Western authority, a realization reflected in his theory of Eastern Peace.

Ahn’s theological understanding was comprehensive, as evidenced by his ability to deliver public lectures on doctrine. His autobiography reveals deep engagement with core Catholic teachings, including the nature of the soul, divine power, filial piety toward Heaven, posthumous judgment, and the resurrection of Christ. His faith remained consistent from his early church involvement through his final martyrdom.

The influence of Catholicism on Ahn manifested in multiple ways. His embrace of Catholic doctrine internalized concepts of human dignity and equality, leading him to advocate for believers facing injustice. His civil rights work constituted a form of evangelism that strengthened Catholic influence. Through his Catholic connection, Ahn further developed his understanding of civilization and enlightenment, arguing that embracing Catholicism was crucial for advancing toward civilized nationhood.

Ahn’s independence activities culminated in his assassination of Ito Hirobumi at Harbin Station on October 26, 1909. Ahn viewed this act not as isolated terrorism but as justified execution of an enemy commander under orders from Kim Du-sung and as a military officer of the Korean Army Staff Headquarters. During his trial, he used the platform to convey his political views, rejecting exploitative colonial relationships while insisting on each nation’s right to independence. He accused Ito of distorting these principles and causing harm to Korea through humiliating treaties.

Executed on March 26, 1910, at age thirty-two, Ahn requested burial near Harbin Park with eventual repatriation when Korea achieved liberation. His final message emphasized national unity and citizen responsibility in achieving independence. Initially denied recognition as a Catholic due to his act of murder, Bishop Kim Su-hwan declared in 1993 that Ahn’s actions constituted justifiable self-defense within the context of independence warfare against Japanese military aggression. A memorial Mass was held at Myeongdong Cathedral in 2010, officially recognizing his Catholic faith.

  1. 박에스더 (김점동) 金點童 Park, Esther

Esther Pak, born Kim Jeom-dong in 1877 in Seoul, became Korea’s first female Western-trained physician and left an indelible mark on the country’s modern medical and missionary history during the first decade of the 20th century. After completing her medical training at Baltimore Woman’s Medical College and overcoming severe personal hardships—including the death of her husband just before graduation—she returned to Korea in November 1900 as an officially appointed medical missionary under the Methodist Episcopal Church.

Upon her return, Park Esther began her medical mission at the Pyongyang Women’s Hospital alongside Rosetta Sherwood Hall. In her first year, she treated over 2,400 patients, personally handling more than half of these cases. Her work expanded when she served as chief physician at the Seoul Boguyeogwan, Korea’s first modern hospital for women, from 1901 to 1903. During this period, she averaged over 3,000 treatments per year while simultaneously contributing to Korea’s early nursing education through curriculum design and teaching1.

Park Esther’s medical practice extended well beyond hospital walls as she regularly made house calls in rural areas, traveling distances of 20 to 30 ri to reach patients who could not access the clinic. This was particularly significant for women who traditionally avoided male doctors due to cultural norms. Her surgical expertise, often demonstrated through collaboration with Hall, included complex ophthalmic procedures such as iridectomy and cataract removal, as well as operations for women suffering from fistulas—conditions so stigmatized that some patients had contemplated suicide before seeking treatment.

During the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), Park Esther’s commitment proved remarkable as she treated nearly 4,400 patients in one year despite social unrest, with daily patient counts reaching as high as 822. Her approach to medicine reflected a holistic philosophy that hospitals should address not only bodily ailments but also mental anguish and fear. This perspective guided her practice even after receiving a tuberculosis diagnosis in 1905, when she continued working for several years, conducting morning rounds, assisting in surgeries, and participating in summer Bible schools and local evangelism until physical limitations prevented her continuation.

The significance of Park Esther’s contributions was formally acknowledged in 1909 when she received a medal at a royal reception for her work in education and enlightenment for Korean women. Her legacy, as documented in these sources, extends beyond her role as Korea’s first female physician to encompass her pioneering demonstration of how Christian medical missions, women’s education, and modern public health could intersect to transform lives during an era of severe gender and social restrictions.

  1. Robert A. Hardie

1865 ~ 1949

Robert Alexander Hardie (R. A. Hardie) originally came to Korea as a medical missionary but is remembered as the central figure in the repentance movement that became the catalyst for the 1903 Wonsan Revival. Although Hardie had conducted missionary work in Wonsan and Tongcheon in Gangwon Province from 1901, he experienced little tangible success and came to recognize his own ‘absence of the Holy Spirit experience’ and ‘pride as a missionary’. At a missionary prayer meeting in Wonsan in August 1903, he publicly confessed his racial superiority complex as a white person, his arrogance, and his pride in viewing Koreans as an uncivilized people.

Hardie’s sincere confession shocked both missionaries and Korean believers, serving as a turning point that led to personal confessions of sin and prayers of contrition, which marked the beginning of the Wonsan Revival in earnest. Subsequently, when F. Franson of the Scandinavian Mission held evangelistic meetings, this repentance movement spread throughout the entire region. Hardie’s repentance movement transcended the personal spiritual awakening of an individual missionary to become a pivotal moment that internalized Christian faith in the hearts of the Korean people.

The flame of repentance and revival that began in Wonsan in 1903 eventually led to the 1907 Great Pyongyang Revival. Hardie played a central role in the spiritual awakening movement by promoting ‘public confession of sins’ and ‘audible simultaneous prayer’ at Bible study meetings and revival gatherings, including those at Jangdaehyeon Church in Pyongyang. His ministry went beyond merely increasing the number of believers to establish a tradition of indigenous and spontaneous faith movements within the Korean church3.

Hardie’s activities provided a crucial opportunity for Korean Protestantism to establish itself not merely as a foreign religion but as a spiritual and intellectual reform movement within the context of national crisis. This subsequently contributed to the Million Souls for Christ Movement (1909), fostering both church growth and the enhancement of national consciousness.

  1. Samuel Austin Moffett

As the twentieth century began, Samuel Austin Moffett focused his efforts on establishing and indigenizing Presbyterian missions in Korea, with Pyongyang serving as the operational center. His founding of the Pyongyang Theological Seminary in 1901 marked a pivotal moment in Korean church history, as it initiated comprehensive training programs for Korean pastors and represented a crucial transition toward ecclesiastical independence based on the principles of self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation.

Moffett’s appointment as the seminary’s inaugural principal in 1904 enabled him to develop a systematic curriculum that would prove instrumental in shaping Korean church leadership. The seminary’s effectiveness became evident in 1907 when it graduated Korea’s first seven ordained Presbyterian pastors4. These newly ordained ministers were subsequently deployed to rural communities and local congregations throughout the peninsula, where they established new churches and developed indigenous leadership networks that significantly accelerated the nationwide expansion of Korean Christianity5.

The 1907 Pyongyang Great Revival emerged as a direct consequence of Moffett’s structured biblical education programs and his facilitation of communal repentance practices. Collaborating with prominent Korean leaders including Kil Sun-ju and Han Seok-jin, Moffett guided prayer assemblies and public confession sessions that provided ordinary Koreans with a collective religious framework for addressing social uncertainties and national challenges. This revival movement subsequently served as the spiritual foundation for the March 1st Independence Movement of 19196.

The remarkable growth of Korean Christianity during this period can be quantified through the Pyongyang church’s membership, which exceeded 5,000 baptized members by the early 1900s, representing more than triple the membership of Seoul congregations7. This expansion resulted from Moffett’s integration of intensive biblical study with his innovative ‘Sarangbang Evangelism’ strategy, which emphasized personal home visitation. Through this approach, he identified and mentored indigenous leaders such as Lee Ki-pung, who later served as a missionary to Jeju Island and other previously unreached territories. Moffett also contributed to the establishment of educational institutions like Soongsil School, creating an integrated model where ecclesiastical and educational development proceeded simultaneously.

Moffett’s influence extended beyond religious spheres into Korea’s emerging nationalist consciousness. Seminary graduates and church members under his guidance became active participants in organizations such as the Shinminhoe (New People’s Association) and the YMCA, establishing the Korean church as a foundation for national identity preservation during the subsequent period of Japanese colonial administration.

Throughout this transformative era, Moffett transcended the conventional role of foreign missionary to become a pioneer of indigenization, facilitating the development of a distinctly Korean expression of Christianity that could sustain itself independently.

  1. William M. Baird

1862 ~ 1931

William M. Baird (1862–1931) served as a Presbyterian missionary in Pyongyang and Hwanghae Province during the early 1900s, establishing and operating Soongsil School (now Soongsil University), thereby playing a central role in Korean Protestant education and the national enlightenment movement. After founding Soongsil School in Pyongyang in 1897, he expanded it into Soongsil Middle School and Soongsil College in the early 1900s, providing students with an integrated education that combined Western modern scholarship with Christian faith.

Baird occupied a central position in the early revival movements of the Korean church, particularly through his leadership role in the Pyongyang Great Revival (1907) and the Million Souls for Christ Movement (1909). The Great Revival that emerged in 1907, centered at Jangdaehyeon Church in Pyongyang, awakened spiritual consciousness throughout churches nationwide, which subsequently led to the Million Souls for Christ Movement in 1909. Operating under the slogan “One Million Koreans for Christ,” this movement represented both a nationwide mass evangelization campaign and a voluntary spiritual renewal movement. Baird systematically involved Soongsil School students and graduates in this effort, enabling the school to function not merely as an institution for intellectual education but as a base for evangelization.

During this period, Baird emphasized spiritual commitment and practical gospel dissemination among his students. Beyond regular class hours, he organized prayer meetings, Bible studies, and regional evangelization teams, directing students to spread the gospel throughout rural areas in Hwanghae Province and the vicinity of Pyongyang. These activities not only expanded the school community into a stronghold of spiritual revival but also developed practical leadership capabilities among graduates that would later extend into rural enlightenment movements and independence activities8.

Baird’s contribution through the Million Souls for Christ Movement is particularly significant in that he helped the Korean church establish the principles of evangelization, national self-reliance, self-governance, and self-propagation from its early stages. This movement marked a crucial turning point as the Korean church transitioned from missionary-centered to Korean believer-centered growth. To facilitate this transformation, Baird actively secured communication and support from churches in the United States. Through these efforts, Soongsil School established itself as a model that combined Korean modern Christian education with missionary work.

William Baird’s dedication to education and mission extended far beyond school administration. Many graduates produced through Soongsil grew to become Korean church leaders, educators, and independence activists, thereby establishing the intellectual and spiritual foundation for Korean modernization and the national movement.

  1. James S. Gale

1863 ~ 1937

James S. Gale (1863–1937) was a prominent Canadian Presbyterian missionary who continued his active work in Bible translation, dictionary compilation, education, and Korean language research throughout the 1900s following his arrival in Korea in 1888. His contributions during the 1900s and 1910s were particularly significant in the academic study and dissemination of the Korean language and Hangeul, as well as in popular education and enlightenment movements.

Gale strongly criticized the Chinese character-centered textual hierarchy prevalent in Joseon intellectual society. He observed that the upper classes treated Chinese characters not merely as a means of communication but rather as objects of veneration. This perspective led him to emphasize the practicality and scientific nature of Hangeul. Gale’s observation that “these privileged classes seem to feel tremendous attraction to Chinese characters, which appear to serve as objects of worship rather than vehicles for conveying ideas” effectively illustrates this critique.

Through his work in Bible translation and Korean dictionary compilation, Gale helped establish Hangeul as the script of the common people. Building upon foundational works such as Korean Grammatical Forms (1894), he continued to develop and refine his Korean language research and lexicographical projects throughout the 1900s9.

In the educational sphere, Gale emphasized Hangeul instruction alongside English and Biblical studies at missionary schools such as Paejae Hakdang. This approach realized an integration of Western learning with native script education, enabling Koreans to acquire new knowledge while moving beyond the traditional civil service examination-centered educational system. Gale’s assessment that “Koreans revere education” reflects his recognition that the high educational aspirations of Koreans provided a substantial foundation for missionary work and enlightenment activities10.

In summary, James S. Gale’s activities during the 1900s and 1910s can be characterized as efforts that illuminated the scientific and practical qualities of Hangeul, disseminated biblical and literary texts through Korean translation, established foundational Korean language research through dictionaries and grammar books, and propagated enlightenment ideals and Western culture through educational and linguistic instruction. His work played a crucial role in helping Korean intellectual society develop recognition of the national language and script while moving away from the Chinese character-centered order.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Sangdong Church Youth Association (상동청년회)

The Sangdong Church Youth Association, along with the Sangdong Youth Academy, maintained close ties with Sinminhoe and Daehan Maeil Sinbo (The Korea Daily News), positioning itself as a significant force in Korean Christianity during this transformative decade.

The organization’s humanitarian efforts became evident in 1905 when approximately 1,033 Koreans departed for Mexico as contract laborers, lured by deceptive advertisements promising favorable working conditions and wages. Japanese businessman Genichi Taisho’s recruitment campaign depicted Mexico as a prosperous, disease-free nation near the United States, guaranteeing nine-hour workdays and substantial compensation. However, the reality proved starkly different, with workers enduring longer hours, inadequate medical care, and reduced wages. When a Chinese observer reported the deplorable conditions of Korean laborers to Korean immigrants in the United States, the information reached the Sangdong Church Youth Association. The organization mobilized other church groups and publicized the crisis in Hwangsong Ilbo, ultimately sending two investigators to Mexico on August 25. Although the Korean government dispatched Yun Chi-ho to address the situation, he diverted to Hawaii instead of proceeding to Mexico. Despite the mission’s limited success due to lack of governmental support, as reported in Daehan Maeil Sinbo on April 19, 1906, the effort heightened awareness about immigration risks among Koreans.

The association’s political engagement manifested through Pastor Jeon Duk-gi’s appeal to King Gojong regarding the Eulsa Unwilling Treaty. This initiative prompted Korean Christians across denominational lines to participate in fasting and overnight prayer vigils. The Sangdong Youth Association amplified these efforts by publishing prayer announcements in Daehan Maeil Sinbo on November 5, 1905, and promoting similar prayer meetings nationwide.

Perhaps most significantly, the organization played a crucial role in The Hague Secret Emissary Affair of 1907. Lee Jun, serving as president of the Sangdong Youth Association, joined Lee Sang-sol and Lee Wi-jong as secret emissaries to the Second World Peace Conference. The association’s members, along with Pastor Jeon and missionary Hulbert, actively supported this diplomatic initiative. While the emissaries faced rejection from major powers including Russia, Britain, France, Germany, and China, Lee Wi-jong’s multilingual advocacy and impassioned speeches to the international press garnered significant attention. His eloquent French presentation and sharp criticism of Western inaction moved the audience to unanimously adopt a resolution supporting Korea, though this diplomatic effort ultimately could not prevent Korea’s annexation by Japan in 1910.

Seoul YMCA (황성 YMCA)

The establishment of the Korean YMCA emerged from Horace G. Underwood’s informal gatherings with Korean yangban youth, which evolved into a formal appeal by 150 signatories and resulted in the organization’s official launch in 1899. The initial Seoul association comprised thirty-five Pai-jai Academy students and forty invitees from the business and missionary communities, expanding to over 900 regular members by 1907. John R. Mott’s visit to Korea during the Great Revival period led to his prophetic declaration that “Korea will be the first nation in a non-Christian world to become a Christian nation.”

The YMCA’s cultural innovation lay in its conceptualization of a new demographic category: Chongnyon (청년), referring to young men aged eighteen and above. This concept had no precedent in traditional Korean society, which recognized only adults and children. The organization’s services encompassed social networking, financial assistance, volunteer opportunities, sports, and education, while its enlightenment programs featured lectures, debates, entertainment, music, vocational training, physical activities, and rural outreach. This comprehensive approach positioned youth as the central force for building both the nation and the kingdom of God, challenging traditional hierarchies based on imperial, aristocratic, or yangban authority.

The YMCA functioned as an incubator for future leaders, carefully balancing its missionary identity with moderate political engagement to avoid colonial suppression. This strategic approach enabled the organization to nurture independence movement activists, including Rhee Syngman, through its educational programs. The organization’s founding in 1903 represented a collaborative effort between Presbyterians and Methodists, addressing the educational needs of children from largely illiterate families who attended Sunday school.

The YMCA’s economic activism became apparent through its promotion of the Gukche Bosang Undong (국채보상운동, National Debt Redemption Movement) from 1907-1908, which developed into a church-led national economic movement. The organization championed anti-feudalism, sovereign independence, enlightenment, modernization, and cultural exchange with foreign nations. In 1907, the YMCA constructed Seoul’s tallest building, symbolizing its prominence and ambitions.

Through diverse educational methods including lecture meetings, panel discussions, “magic lantern” presentations, concerts, vocational training, and physical education, the YMCA worked to dismantle restrictive traditional customs and encourage intelligent resistance to Japanese rule. These activities served as catalysts for cultural transformation and national sentiment cultivation, providing some relief from the suffering under Japanese imperialism.

Yun Chi-ho’s leadership as the first Korean General Secretary and longtime president, while later controversial due to his pro-Japanese activities, proved instrumental in defending the Korean YMCA’s independence from forced merger with the Japanese YMCA throughout the colonial period. His collaboration with Western missionaries demonstrated Christian and enlightening visions across urban and rural Korea.

신민회 New People’s Association

During the tumultuous first decade of the twentieth century, the Sinminhoe (New People’s Association) emerged as one of Korea’s most significant clandestine organizations dedicated to national self-strengthening. Founded in 1907 with its primary base in Pyongyang and the northwestern regions, the organization drew its membership predominantly from Christian leaders and progressive intellectuals who had previously participated in the Independence Club movement. Following the dissolution of the Independence Club and the intensification of Japanese control, these leaders shifted their strategic approach from direct confrontation to a philosophy of practical self-strengthening rather than armed resistance.

The Sinminhoe’s operational framework centered on the fundamental belief that educational advancement and economic development represented the most viable pathways to national restoration. This conviction translated into concrete initiatives including the establishment of modern educational institutions such as Osan School and Daeseong School, the promotion of industrial development projects, and the publication of newspapers and literature designed to cultivate national awareness. The organization’s leadership structure reflected a strong Christian influence, with many core members viewing the church as both a spiritual sanctuary and a vital institution for developing moral character and civic responsibility. These leaders systematically integrated church-affiliated youth organizations, including the YMCA and various student groups, into the broader framework of nationalist enlightenment activities.

The expanding influence of the Sinminhoe inevitably attracted the scrutiny of Japanese colonial authorities. The situation became particularly precarious following the 1907 Pyongyang Great Revival Movement, which had mobilized Christians into coordinated collective action and alarmed Japanese officials about the potential for religious gatherings to serve nationalist purposes. In response, Japanese police orchestrated the fabricated 105 Persons Incident in 1911, falsely accusing prominent Sinminhoe leaders of conspiring to assassinate the Japanese Governor-General. This orchestrated legal proceeding resulted in the systematic arrest, torture, and imprisonment of leading Christian nationalists, effectively dismantling the organizational infrastructure that had sustained the Sinminhoe’s operations.

The colonial crackdown fundamentally altered the relationship between Christian institutions and nationalist activism. Faced with the prospect of continued persecution, missionaries and certain church leaders adopted a position of “apolitical faith” and pursued institutional “depoliticization” as a survival strategy. Nevertheless, the ideological foundation established by the Sinminhoe continued to influence many Korean Christians. The organization’s emphasis on educational development and moral transformation provided crucial groundwork for subsequent movements, most notably the March 1st Independence Movement of 1919, during which Christians once again assumed prominent roles as a mobilized collective force.

The historical significance of the Sinminhoe extends beyond its immediate organizational achievements to encompass its role in demonstrating how early Korean Christianity became fundamentally intertwined with nationalist aspirations. The organization’s legacy illustrates the synthesis of spiritual revival with a comprehensive vision of civic and national regeneration through systematic self-strengthening efforts.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Early Korean Overseas Missionary Sending Movement

Between 1900 and 1910, the Korean Protestant Church took its first concrete steps toward becoming not just a mission field but a missionary-sending church — a turning point that laid the groundwork for Korea’s later identity as one of the world’s largest missionary-sending nations. Rather than highlighting individual figures in isolation, the historical evidence supports framing this period as the emergence of an “early Korean overseas missionary sending movement,” shaped by denominational definitions and evolving policies.

The Methodist Church provides the clearest example of this institutionalization. In 1903, Hong Seung-ha was officially dispatched to Hawaii to serve the first wave of Korean immigrants as a missionary. Under the Methodist policy at the time, which counted ministry to overseas Korean diaspora communities as legitimate missionary work, Hong Seung-ha is rightfully recognized as Korea’s first Protestant overseas missionary.

The Presbyterian Church’s approach reveals the complexities inherent in defining missionary status during this formative period. In 1907, the newly organized Korean Presbytery decided to send Yi Ki-pung to Jeju Island, considering it culturally distinct enough to be treated as a mission field. However, archival records and later denominational rulings clarify that Jeju was not truly foreign territory, and that Yi was subsequently reclassified as a local evangelist (Jeondo Moksa) rather than an overseas missionary. By 1917, the Presbyterian General Assembly had officially defined a missionary as “a pastor preaching to foreigners,” explicitly excluding domestic and diaspora-focused ministries from that status.

The case of Choi Kwan-heul, who was commissioned in 1909 to Vladivostok to minister to Korean immigrants in Russia’s Far East, further illustrates these definitional challenges. This assignment demonstrates that early Presbyterian records initially recognized sending ministers to Korean diaspora communities abroad as foreign mission work, though this designation was later redefined as diaspora pastoral care rather than foreign mission per se.

These denominational differences persisted well into the twentieth century. Kim Chil-sung’s research reveals that the Methodist Church continued to recognize pastors serving overseas Korean communities as missionaries until 1997, when official regulations were revised. Conversely, the Presbyterian Church had redefined its policy as early as 1917 to limit the missionary designation exclusively to those serving non-Korean foreign populations. This divergence explains why Hong Seung-ha’s work in Hawaii remains widely recognized as the legitimate starting point of Korean overseas mission, while Yi Ki-pung’s Jeju assignment is now classified as domestic outreach.

The significance of this early phase extends beyond individual cases to encompass the institutional frameworks being established. Both the Methodist and Presbyterian Churches began to systematically address fundamental questions about missionary identity: what constituted legitimate missionary work, how such endeavors should be funded, and which candidates qualified for missionary status. These deliberations and the resulting policies shaped Korea’s missionary-sending structures throughout the subsequent decades.

대부흥운동 The Great Revival

Korea experienced a remarkable flourishing of Christian faith during the early 20th century. The 1907 Pyongyang Revival stands as a testament to Christianity’s successful revitalization of Korean spiritual life. This religious awakening unfolded through three distinct phases: the initial stirrings in Wonsan (1903-1906), the peak period in Pyongyang (1907-1908), and the ambitious Million Soul Campaign throughout the Korean Peninsula (1909-1910). The movement emerged from a confluence of biblical education initiatives, political upheavals, and the implementation of Nevius missionary methodologies. Significantly, this spiritual renaissance maintained deep connections with Korea’s independence aspirations. The establishment of Bible Study Groups (Sa-gyeong-hoe) and widespread lay involvement provided the cornerstone for this transformative movement.

The Wonsan Awakening

The spiritual movement’s origins trace back to 1890, when Underwood established a biblical study group with seven participants. By 1904, approximately 60% of church members regularly participated in Bible conferences, and by 1909, the North Presbyterian Mission territory hosted 800 such gatherings with roughly 50,000 attendees. While the 1890s Bible conferences initially focused on training church leadership, their influence gradually expanded to include ordinary believers and spiritual transformation. Wonsan emerged as the first center of spiritual awakening before the more famous Pyongyang events.

Missionary Harry Andrew Rhodes documented a transformative conference experience, noting how R.A. Hardie of the Methodist Episcopal Mission served as a key leader. Hardie experienced a profound personal crisis regarding his ministry failures in Gangwon Province. His public acknowledgment of pride, spiritual hardness, and inadequate faith before both missionaries and Korean believers sparked an extraordinary spiritual outpouring among missionaries and Korean Christians in Wonsan. This confession catalyzed a week-long period of continuous sin confession among Korean believers, accompanied by rapid church membership growth.

The Pyongyang Movement

The Pyongyang Revival, widely recognized as the Great Revival, occurred despite challenges posed by Japan’s recent victory over Russia and the resulting hardships. According to missionary James Scarth Gale’s observations, post-war Koreans in the Pyongyang region enjoyed relative prosperity and peaceful circumstances. However, beneath this surface tranquility lay a profound spiritual yearning and inner anguish that motivated earnest prayers for revival beginning in 1906.

Importantly, this revival movement stemmed primarily from genuine spiritual conviction rather than desires for economic advancement or liberation from Japanese rule, though nationalistic elements certainly influenced the broader context. Some missionaries even viewed Japanese governance as potentially beneficial for Korea. The revival’s initial manifestations appeared in Wonsan before spreading to Pyongyang, where both Methodist and Presbyterian congregations conducted numerous worship services.

These gatherings exhibited emotional intensity reminiscent of Jonathan Edwards’s Great Awakening in 18th-century America, with Korean participants frequently prostrating themselves and crying out to God for mercy. Missionary Charlie Clark observed the fervent atmosphere, describing how participants appeared consumed with hatred for sin and desperate to confess and find relief from their spiritual burdens. Despite his theological conservatism and initial skepticism about supernatural phenomena, Clark eventually became convinced of genuine spiritual manifestations, including what he believed to be demonic exorcism.

Confession became the central element, with believers considering no transgression too significant or insignificant to acknowledge. A pervasive sense of fear gripped the church community, as members remained in states of distress and sorrow until they could publicly confess and repent before God.

Kil Son-ju’s Leadership

Kil Son-ju emerged as the movement’s central figure, taking charge of preaching services and promoting early morning prayer gatherings. His influence extended to the subsequent Million Souls Movement (1909-1910), where he instilled revivalist fervor throughout Korean Christianity. Kil’s impact included developing a fundamentalist Korean Christian tradition that strongly defended biblical inerrancy against liberal theological challenges.

Often called “the father of Korean Christianity,” Kil’s absence would have fundamentally altered Korean Protestantism’s trajectory. He successfully bridged Western missionary Christianity and Korean cultural understanding. Notably, his Confucian background remained integrated with his Christian faith, as he wove Korean historical elements and Chinese literary and philosophical concepts into his sermons. Korean Christians adopted the missionaries’ theologically conservative approach, emphasizing verbal biblical inspiration. Through Kil’s and the missionaries’ combined influence, theological liberalism found no foothold in Korean Christianity.

Revival Characteristics and Spread

Beginning with Horace Grant Underwood’s 1890 Bible classes, which achieved remarkable success, the revival movement gained momentum through Kil Son-ju’s commitment to conducting “twenty-two hundred preaching days” starting in 1907. The movement featured intense emotional expression and widespread sin confession. Earnest revival prayers began in 1906, with noon prayer meetings commencing in January 1907.

After several days without significant developments, Kil observed a prevailing sense of spiritual emptiness. However, on January 14, 1907, Rev. Graham Lee’s prayer encouragement triggered an immediate massive response from attendees, initiating a flood of confessions covering every imaginable transgression. Rev. W.N. Blair witnessed unprecedented scenes, describing meetings unlike anything he had previously experienced, where the most serious and shameful sins were openly declared, potentially exposing participants to severe legal consequences.

Kil Sun-ju employed dramatic preaching methods, including a memorable illustration where he bound himself with ropes and dramatically broke free to symbolize liberation from sin. As church membership surged, multiple baptismal services became necessary. March 1, 1908, marked a historic milestone when Kil Sun-ju, assisted by Samuel Moffett, conducted the first baptismal service led by a Korean pastor, baptizing 200 people at Kil’s Central Church in Pyongyang.

Institutional Development and Geographic Expansion

The revival’s success facilitated the establishment of Union Christian College (originally Soongsil Academy) in Pyongyang, which achieved university status in 1907. The institution’s primary mission involved training pastors and Christian ministry leaders. The first graduating class included two students in 1908, followed by five in 1910. Construction began with the first building in 1911 and dormitories in 1912-1913, funded by a substantial donation from Mrs. Cyrus McCormick.

The movement extended from Pyongyang throughout northwestern Korea before crossing the Yalu and Tumen Rivers into Manchuria. Korean believers carried the revival to remote areas previously unreached by Western missionaries.

백만인구령운동A Million Souls for Christ

The spiritual revivals that occurred in Wonsan in 1903 and Pyongyang in 1907 provided the foundational momentum for what would become the Million Souls Movement, which emerged between 1909 and 1910 as a deliberate effort by the Korean church to sustain and institutionalize revival energy through systematic evangelistic campaigns. While the earlier revivals in Wonsan and Pyongyang manifested as spontaneous spiritual outpourings attributed to the Holy Spirit’s work, the Million Souls Movement represented a carefully orchestrated missionary initiative that embodied both the Korean Christian community’s aspiration for continued spiritual renewal and their developing sense of collective purpose amid mounting colonial pressures.

Recent scholarship has illuminated a significant dimension of this movement that extends beyond its purely evangelistic objectives. The Million Souls Movement functioned simultaneously as an extension of revival enthusiasm and as a strategic initiative through which the nascent Korean church sought to establish its identity as a national church (민족교회) capable of serving as a spiritual stronghold against Japanese imperial encroachment. Although American Methodist and Presbyterian missionaries provided initial leadership, indigenous church leaders, particularly Kil Sun-joo, actively influenced the movement’s direction by conceptualizing large-scale evangelism as a mechanism for strengthening national moral character and sustaining hope for eventual independence.

The Million Souls Movement distinguished itself from previous revival gatherings through its emphasis on systematic organization rather than solely on repentance and communal confession. This strategic approach manifested most clearly in the implementation of Chukho (逐戶) evangelism and the Day Offering (주일헌금/하루전도) commitment, whereby believers dedicated entire days to household-to-household gospel distribution.[5] These methodological innovations represented a significant evolution from earlier revival practices and demonstrated the Korean church’s expanding organizational sophistication. Contemporary research indicates that these strategies drew inspiration from American evangelical models, particularly those associated with Moody and Finney, which Korean leaders adapted to local circumstances by integrating Western revivalist theology with distinctly Korean national consciousness.

The formal adoption of the slogan “A Million Souls for Christ” by the 1909 Seoul General Council of Evangelical Missions established an ambitious target of one million converts within a population where total Christian membership remained below 200,000. This goal functioned less as a realistic statistical objective than as a symbolic expression of the movement’s eschatological vision and social aspirations. Current scholarship emphasizes that this seemingly hyperbolic target served a crucial function as a narrative of spiritual resistance during a period when Korea was experiencing the loss of political sovereignty. The church’s discourse of “saving souls” operated on multiple levels, encompassing both spiritual salvation and the preservation of national identity.

The movement’s organizational structure, however, revealed inherent limitations within its missionary-led framework. While foreign missionaries including Gale, Underwood, Hugh Miller, and D.A. Bunker maintained administrative oversight,[9] effective local mobilization required substantial voluntary participation from lay Korean Christians, a dependency that proved increasingly challenging under intensifying Japanese surveillance. The colonial administration regarded large religious gatherings with suspicion, frequently interpreting them as potential venues for anti-Japanese activities, and consequently imposed restrictions that constrained the scope and scale of revival meetings.

Despite the movement’s failure to achieve its numerical target, its legacy proved more enduring through the establishment of sustained prayer-centered piety (금요철야기도회 등) and the development of a more systematically organized lay evangelism network that continued functioning into the 1910s and beyond. The YMCA Student Evangelical Conference held at Jingwansa Temple in June 1910 exemplifies this broader transformation, illustrating how the Korean church began cultivating collaborative relationships with other religious communities, including Buddhist monks who provided accommodation, as part of a unified response to Japanese colonial rule. This interfaith cooperation demonstrated that Korean Christianity was developing into a distinctive evangelical movement that successfully integrated revival spirituality with practical social adaptation strategies and forms of resistance.

Key Theological Issues

The 1901 decision by the Presbyterian Church Council to separate church and state arose from circumstances surrounding missionary work and Korean political developments. Missionaries had established connections with King Gojong and the royal family to support their evangelical activities. The church’s involvement with the Independence Association initially appeared compatible with missionary objectives, but complications emerged when the Association conflicted with the Korean Empire government.

Following the Independence Association’s dissolution, Christians experienced persecution. Missionary responses varied, with Methodist missionary Underwood expressing concern about church politicization while Presbyterian missionary Appenzeller showed displeasure with the government’s dissolution of the Independence Association. Underwood believed that participants who formed the People’s Joint Assembly after the dissolution could not be controlled and that even the Emperor would be unable to disperse them. These differing views between Appenzeller and Underwood contributed to the denominational disagreements that led to the Presbyterian Church’s 1901 separation declaration.

The distinction between denominational positions was less significant than it initially appeared. The Methodist church dissolved its Youth Society due to young Christians’ political activities, indicating that concerns about political involvement extended beyond denominational lines. Christians faced suspicion from multiple sources during this period, being viewed both as supporters of progressive movements and as opponents of the Independence Association, which led to assassination attempts and government pressure.

This situation created concern among missionaries. R.E. Speer, responsible for overseas missions for the Northern Presbyterian Church, reported that the reactionary political climate posed risks if Christianity became associated with liberal political movements. He stated that such associations would create problems with administrators and recommended avoiding political involvement that might cause offense.

Missionaries worried about the Korean church being perceived as a political entity, viewing this as detrimental to their work. Most missionaries held theological positions emphasizing religious purity and favoring separation between religion and politics. They noted that many people attended church for what they considered inappropriate reasons.

Missionary records indicate that some Koreans approached Christianity seeking material benefits or power, while others were attracted because Christian nations appeared powerful and civilized. C.E. Sharp observed that individuals who joined the church for political reasons often left when spiritual matters were emphasized. Underwood similarly worried about people with questionable motivations joining the church.

International relations between the United States and Japan influenced missionary non-interference policies. The United States had shown concern for Korea during the Sino-Japanese War, but after Japan’s victory, Americans sought to avoid damaging relations with Japan. With the United States dealing with issues in Hawaii and Cuba that created conflict with Spain, concern about Japan’s expansion led to efforts to avoid confrontation.

The U.S. government instructed diplomats and missionaries in Korea to avoid political intervention. Previous missionary involvement in the Gapsin Coup had drawn Japanese protests, reinforcing American non-interference policies. The official position required missionaries to focus on religious duties and avoid political matters. Mission boards generally followed this policy, with leaders including F.F. Ellinwood, R.E. Speer, A.J. Brown, and C.B. McAfee holding colonial perspectives in their missionary work. Brown played a significant role in the 1901 separation declaration.

The hierarchical structure connecting the U.S. government, mission boards, and missionaries ensured policy compliance. Mission boards operated with government support and maintained authority over appointments, assignments, dismissals, and funding. While missionaries occasionally protested board decisions, the institutional hierarchy remained strong.

The deteriorating relationship with the Korean Empire government following Independence Association activities led missionaries to view church politicization as threatening their mission. The 1901 Presbyterian General Assembly decision began the Korean church’s approach of avoiding political involvement. The resolutions stated that the church and its members were unrelated to political events and should be excluded from future political activities while maintaining relations with the United States and Japan.

These resolutions aimed to protect the church from political involvement and prevent damage to U.S.-Japan relations. The decision established the Korean church’s non-political direction, with resolutions communicated to members through a letter written by Presbyterian missionaries. The General Assembly meeting included twenty-nine missionaries from Presbyterian churches in the United States, Canada, and Australia. Fourteen Koreans participated, including Seo Gyeongjo, Bang Gichang, and Kim Jongsup, but lacked decision-making authority.

The General Assembly operated with separate English and Korean sessions. Missionaries organized and led the English session, which addressed theological issues and administrative matters until the Korean Church Assembly formed in 1907. The Korean session functioned as a discussion forum without legislative authority. The 1901 resolutions were primarily determined by missionaries, with Korean participation remaining limited.
