Korea: Agency (Pre-1900)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. 서상륜 Seo Sang-ryun (Suh Sang-yun) 徐相崙

1848 ~ 1926

Seo Sang-ryun (1848–1926) was born into a wealthy family of the Daegu Seo clan and received his early education in Chinese classics (漢學) at a local seodang (書堂). After being orphaned at age 14 due to a waterborne disease, he and his younger brother entered the ginseng trade in Manchuria as a means of economic survival.1

During his trading activities, Seo contracted typhoid fever in Yingkou, where he received medical treatment from Scottish missionaries John Ross and John MacIntyre. Through this encounter, he was introduced to Christianity and subsequently converted, receiving baptism from Ross.2 Drawing upon his literacy in both Chinese and Hangul, Seo collaborated with Ross, MacIntyre, and other Koreans in translating the New Testament into colloquial Korean. The translation project produced the Gospel of Luke in 1882, the remaining three Gospels in 1884, and the complete New Testament in 1887. The translators chose vernacular Hangul over literary Chinese to ensure accessibility for broader Korean audiences, particularly women and non-elite populations.3

In 1882, Seo received appointment as the first Korean colporteur (매서인/권서인) by the British Bible Society. He conducted clandestine distribution of Korean New Testaments, utilizing his established ginseng trade routes to transport materials through Uiju into Seoul, with initial distribution beginning around Namdaemun Market. His evangelistic activities resulted in several conversions. Due to Ross's inability to enter Korea, Underwood performed baptisms for 13 of Seo's converts in Seoul in 1885.4

Following his imprisonment in 1883 for smuggling Christian literature, Seo escaped to Songcheon in Hwanghae Province. There, he and his brother initiated prayer meetings beginning May 16, and their congregation expanded to approximately 18 believers in Sorae. This group is commonly recognized as Korea's first indigenous Protestant church.5 This development occurred prior to the establishment of formal Western missionary presence, indicating that Korean-initiated evangelization and Hangul-based biblical distribution preceded organized Western missionary activities.

  1. 이수정 Lee Su Jung (Lee Soo Jeong) 李樹廷

1842 ~ 1886

Lee Su-Jung emerged as a pivotal figure in Korea's Protestant transformation during the late nineteenth century. Originally from the elite Confucian Yangban class, Lee recognized that Christianity—along with the Western science, technology, and governance associated with it—was essential for modernizing and strengthening Joseon Korea.6 His conversion began during his travels to Japan in 1882 as part of Park Yeong-hyo's diplomatic delegation, where he encountered Protestant teachings through Tsuda Sen and received baptism from missionary G. W. Knox at a Tokyo church on April 29, 1883.7

Lee's scholarly background in Chinese classics proved instrumental in his subsequent biblical translation work. As an accomplished sinologist with early exposure to Christian texts, he collaborated with Henry Loomis of the American Bible Society in Japan to translate the New Testament into a colloquial Chinese-Korean hybrid. Between 1883 and 1884, he completed and distributed the Hyun-To-Han-Han Sin-Yak Sung-Gyung (懸吐漢韓新約聖書), which comprised the four Gospels and Acts. His translation efforts continued with the Gospel of Mark, of which 6,000 copies were printed in Yokohama in 1885.8 This distribution strategy deliberately targeted southern Koreans and Sino-educated elites, distinguishing his approach from other contemporary versions such as those supported by British missionaries Ross and McIntyre.9

While serving as a Korean language instructor at Tokyo's Foreign Language School, Lee extended his influence through pastoral ministry to Korean students. His regular worship services gradually developed into Japan's first Korean Protestant church by 1884, with graduates from this congregation later assuming influential positions within Korean government and society.10

Lee's concern about growing Japanese influence prompted him to advocate for direct American missionary engagement in Korea. His appeals to American churches proved effective, inspiring the U.S. Presbyterian Foreign Mission Board to take action. When Horace Underwood arrived in Korea in April 1885, he carried Lee's Gospel of Mark translation, having learned Korean from Lee during his time in Tokyo.^6 This connection between Lee's preparatory work and subsequent American Protestant missions has earned him recognition as a "Korean Macedonian"—the crucial link that facilitated Western Protestant engagement in Korea.

  1. 서재필 Seo Jae-pil (Philip Jaisohn) 徐載弼

1864 ~ 1951

In the turbulent 1890s, Seo Jae-pil—also known by his American name Philip Jaisohn—emerged as a pivotal figure in Korea's quest for modernization, national sovereignty, and civil rights. After the failure of the 1884 Gapsin Coup, a short-lived attempt to reform Joseon Korea through elite-led modernization, Seo sought asylum in the United States. There he became the first Korean to obtain U.S. citizenship in 1890, converting to Christianity through the local YMCA and receiving a medical degree in 1892 with the support of Christian philanthropist William Hollenback. During this period of exile, Seo fundamentally reimagined his reform strategy, coming to believe that sustainable progress in Korea required not only institutional change but also the education and active participation of the broader populace.

The opportunity to implement these ideas came in 1895 when Seo returned to Korea at the invitation of the Korean government, which was reeling from internal instability and external pressure following Japan's victory over China in the First Sino-Japanese War. The Qing dynasty had ceded its power over Korea, and new foreign threats—particularly from Russia—emerged. Amidst this crisis, Seo launched two major initiatives that would define his reform agenda: The Independent newspaper and the Independence Club.

Founded in April 1896, The Independent (Doknip Sinmun, 獨立新聞) represented Korea's first modern newspaper published in both Hangul and English. Unlike previous publications written primarily in Chinese characters (Hanja), The Independent used only Hangul, thereby making information accessible to ordinary people regardless of their education or social status.11 The paper aimed to spread Enlightenment ideals, critique government corruption, and advocate for civil rights, national sovereignty, and modernization. Foreign observers noted the newspaper's growing influence in shaping public opinion and exposing administrative wrongdoing. The editorial policy emphasized that a literate population was essential to fostering civic consciousness and national pride.12

Building on the momentum generated by The Independent, Seo established the Independence Club (Doknip Hyeophoe, 獨立協會) in July 1896. While Seo did not openly affiliate himself with the Club's leadership, he served as its guiding force. The organization sought to promote civil rights, Western technology, constitutional government, and Korean sovereignty. Its most democratic innovation was the Manmin Gongdonghoe (萬民共同會), or All People's Congress—public forums where people of all social backgrounds could gather to debate national policies and express their views freely.⁶ This participatory political culture was unprecedented in Joseon and directly embodied Christian and liberal ideas of human equality and social justice.

Seo's reform vision extended beyond publications and organizations to include symbolic nation-building projects. He spearheaded the construction of the Independence Gate (Doknipmun, 獨立門), built on the former site of Yeongeunmun—a gate associated with Chinese suzerainty—and the transformation of the Mohwagwan into the Independence Hall (Doknipgwan, 獨立館). Both projects were funded by public donations and represented Korea's aspiration for self-determination.13

Despite these achievements, conservative officials and foreign powers viewed Seo and his allies as threats to the established order. In 1898, under mounting political pressure, Seo was expelled from Korea and the Independence Club was forcibly disbanded. Nevertheless, the ideas he introduced—mass enlightenment, civil equality grounded in Christian humanism, and participatory nationhood—would leave a lasting impact on Korea's independence movement. Seo's advocacy inspired the ideological foundations of future resistance efforts, including the March 1st Movement of 1919 and the emergence of democratic republicanism in modern Korea.14

  1. John Ross

1842 ~ 1915

John Ross, a Scottish Presbyterian missionary based in Manchuria, played a foundational role in Korean Protestantism through Bible translation, border evangelism, and theological training. His earlier achievements in the 1870s had a lasting influence on Korean Christianity in the 1890s.

One of Ross's most enduring contributions was the Korean Bible translation, completed in collaboration with J. McIntyre in 1877. This was the first Korean New Testament, and it became the principal scripture used by Korean Christians and missionaries throughout the 1890s, facilitating both evangelism and literacy in hangul among early converts.15 This translation laid the textual foundation for church growth and remains recognized as a landmark in Protestant mission history.

Ross simultaneously focused his missionary efforts on Koreans living near the Yalu River, the border between Manchuria and Korea. Through his evangelistic work among Korean merchants and travelers in this region, he led to the conversion of Soh Sang Ryun, who later became the first Korean Protestant pastor. Upon returning to his homeland, Soh established a Christian community in Sorai village, which grew to become one of the earliest indigenous centers of Protestant Christianity in Korea. The Sorai Church, formed as a result of this indirect missionary work, was already active during the first Protestant missionary period (1885–1890) and continued to thrive during the expansion period of the 1890s.

Although Ross did not reside in Korea, his influence was deeply embedded in its early Christian infrastructure. His Korean Bible translation became the theological and liturgical standard for early believers, while his indirect mentorship of Soh Sang Ryun provided a critical indigenous leadership model. The combination of accessible scripture and indigenous agency, initiated by Ross in the previous decade, became instrumental to the rapid growth of Korean Protestantism in the 1890s.

Concurrently, Ross remained active in theological education during this decade. In 1890, he founded a four-year theological seminary in Shenyang, which evolved into what is now the Northeast Theological Seminary in China.16 He continued training theology students through the winter of 1898 and early 1899, solidifying his dual legacy in Korea and China through the development of well-educated, locally trained preachers.

During this same period in 1890, Ross expressed his growing conviction that Korea had unique potential as a Christian nation. In a mission report, he wrote that Korea might be the first Asian country to fully accept the Gospel, reflecting his optimistic theological vision at the start of the decade.

  1. Horace Newton Allen

1858 ~ 1932

In the 1890s, Horace Newton Allen’s activities in Korea were marked by a transition from pioneering medical missionary work to influential diplomatic service, while his impact continued to shape the modernization of Korean society and the environment for Protestant missions.

After his arrival in 1884 and the founding of Jejungwon (濟衆院), Korea’s first modern hospital, Allen’s direct involvement in medical work diminished by the late 1880s as he assumed diplomatic roles, notably as assistant and later chief at the U.S. legation in Seoul. Nevertheless, the institutions and reforms he initiated continued to bear fruit throughout the 1890s. Allen’s diary and contemporary accounts reveal that his early medical mission—establishing Jejungwon and its medical school—provided a crucial bridge for Western medicine and education in Korea and offered a legitimate platform for other missionaries to enter and operate in the country at a time when direct evangelism was restricted by law17.

During the 1890s, Allen’s diplomatic position enabled him to act as a protector and advocate for foreign missionaries. He often intervened with Korean authorities to ensure the safety of missionaries and the continuation of their work, especially during periods of political instability and anti-foreign sentiment. His influence at court, fostered through his earlier medical service to key figures like Min Young-ik and King Gojong, allowed him to mediate on behalf of the missionary community and to encourage a more tolerant stance toward Christian activity.

His diary confirms that by 1897, Allen was addressing matters such as the designation of foreign concessions and missionary protection. He mediated conflicts with the Russian envoy Speyer and defended missionaries when tensions arose. Moreover, Allen’s facilitation of the Gyeongbu Railway concession in 1898 demonstrated his strategic pivot toward promoting Korea’s industrial modernization through diplomacy, even as some fellow missionaries questioned whether his growing political ties compromised his missionary calling.

Allen’s approach to mission work, which emphasized indirect evangelism through medicine and education rather than overt proselytizing, remained a source of tension with other missionaries who favored more direct religious outreach. Figures like James S. Gale criticized Allen for neglecting evangelism, arguing that his entanglement with court politics undermined the spiritual mission. Gale even argued in 1909 that Allen’s name should be “stricken off the roll of missionaries” altogether. Nevertheless, Allen’s strategy proved pragmatic in the Korean context, where the government and much of society remained deeply suspicious of foreign religious influence.

The medical and educational institutions he helped establish—especially Jejungwon and its medical school—became training grounds for Korean professionals and centers for the dissemination of new ideas, contributing to the modernization of Korean society. These hospitals and schools provided spaces where Western notions of equality, self-support, and national identity could take root. Many graduates, shaped by Protestant ethics and Western learning, went on to play influential roles in reform movements such as the Independence Club (founded in 1896), which championed political modernization and resistance to foreign domination18.

Thus, Allen’s legacy in the 1890s was not only in the spread of Western medicine but also in fostering the conditions for the emergence of a modern, self-aware Korean nation. Despite challenges—including administrative obstacles, financial difficulties, and internal disputes within the missionary community—Allen’s contributions during the 1890s were foundational. His dual role as a medical pioneer and diplomat helped secure a relatively safe and supportive environment for the growth of Protestant missions and the modernization of Korea, impacts that would resonate well into the twentieth century.

  1. Henry Gerhard Appenzeller

1858 ~ 1902

In the 1890s, Henry Gerhard Appenzeller (1858–1902) emerged as a pivotal figure in establishing Methodist foundations and modern educational frameworks in Korea. His multifaceted activities during this transformative decade produced lasting institutional and cultural impacts on Korean society.

Appenzeller's most significant educational contribution was the establishment of Pai Chai Hakdang (培材學堂) in Seoul, which represented Korea's first modernized educational institution. After receiving official recognition and naming by King Gojong in 1886, the school had evolved by the 1890s into a primary center for Western learning and leadership development. The institution's curriculum incorporated English language instruction alongside other contemporary subjects, while simultaneously serving as a platform for Christian evangelization among Korean youth. Although the school's physical legacy has been largely diminished over time, with most original structures having been demolished, the East Hall constructed in 1916 remains standing as a protected cultural monument. The campus grounds continue to preserve ancient trees that have acquired symbolic significance representing the institution's extensive historical continuity.

Appenzeller's religious endeavors proved equally transformative through his founding of Jeongdong First Methodist Church. Following the first official Korean worship service in 1887, the congregation experienced substantial growth, with weekly attendance exceeding 200 by 1892. The decision to construct a new church building in 1894 led Appenzeller to undertake fundraising expeditions to the United States, where he secured the majority of necessary resources. The completed structure, finished in 1897 and designed in Georgia Gothic architectural style, became a central hub for Korean Methodist activities and currently holds recognition as a national cultural heritage site, marking the early establishment of Protestant Christianity in Korea19.

Beyond his Jeongdong activities, Appenzeller pursued broader missionary expansion throughout Seoul. In 1890, he established Central Methodist Church in the Jongno district while simultaneously founding Jongno Bookstore, subsequently renamed Daedong Seosi. This bookstore functioned as a critical distribution center for both Christian and secular literature, effectively connecting literacy promotion with Western knowledge dissemination and Christian instruction to the broader Korean population.

Appenzeller's publishing initiatives represented another dimension of his comprehensive approach to cultural transformation. His 1889 establishment of the Tri-lingual Press in Pai Chai Hakdang's basement facility enabled multilingual publication in Korean, Chinese, and English. This press subsequently developed into the Methodist Publishing House, which became instrumental in distributing Christian literature and general educational materials throughout Korea. These publishing efforts supported literacy advancement and Protestant ideological propagation within a cultural context dominated by established Confucian and Buddhist traditions20.

The institutional framework Appenzeller created facilitated Korea's broader modernization process. His educational and religious establishments became focal points for introducing novel concepts including self-governance, democratic principles, and social responsibility, which subsequently influenced the developing Korean nationalist movement during the late Joseon period. His collaborative relationships with fellow missionaries, particularly Horace G. Underwood in Bible translation and Christian organizational development, established patterns of interdenominational cooperation that would characterize Korean Protestant development for subsequent decades.

The enduring significance of Appenzeller's 1890s contributions remains evident in the continued operation and cultural importance of the institutions he founded. These schools, churches, and publishing enterprises persist as significant cultural and historical landmarks in contemporary Korea, preserving his integrated vision for educational advancement, religious development, and national modernization.

  1. Horace Grant Underwood

1859 ~ 1916

Underwood was a pioneering American Presbyterian missionary whose work in Korea during the 1890s produced lasting effects on Korean religion, education, and society. His multifaceted approach to missionary work established foundations that would influence Korean development well into the twentieth century.

In evangelism and church building, Underwood pursued an aggressive strategy despite significant obstacles. Legal restrictions on Christian proselytization and cautious counsel from American diplomats did not deter his active evangelization and baptism of Korean converts. His wife, Lillias Horton Underwood, characterized his unwavering persistence as that of an "English bulldog," a testament to his determined approach to missionary work. Underwood conducted extensive itinerant missions throughout northern Korea, locating Koreans who had been introduced to the Gospel through early colporteurs and personally baptizing new believers. His most significant institutional achievement during this period was the establishment of Saemunan Presbyterian Church (새문안교회), which became the first organized Protestant church in Korea and served as the foundation for Korean Presbyterian traditions.

Parallel to his evangelistic efforts, Underwood recognized the critical importance of Bible translation and literary work for establishing an indigenous Korean church. Working alongside Henry G. Appenzeller, he contributed to translating the Gospel of Mark into Korean and participated in standardizing translation efforts through the Permanent Bible Committee, which he helped organize in the early 1890s. His scholarly contributions extended beyond religious texts to include influential linguistic resources such as An Introduction to the Korean Spoken Language and A Concise Dictionary of the Korean Language. These publications served dual purposes: they assisted other missionaries in learning Korean while simultaneously promoting the wider use of Hangul, thereby advancing literacy during a period when Korea's ruling elites preferred classical Chinese.21

Underwood's educational and medical mission work reflected equally transformative vision. He and his colleagues advocated for universal education, including education for women—a concept that challenged traditional late Choson Korean social structures. This educational emphasis established the groundwork for institutions that would eventually develop into Yonsei University and its associated medical services. Through schools such as Kyungshin School, they implemented Western curricula alongside Christian instruction, producing a generation of Koreans who would subsequently lead modernization and nationalist movements.

A significant strategic development during this decade was Underwood's adoption of the Nevius Method, which emphasized self-support, self-government, and self-propagation within Korean churches. This approach cultivated local leadership, encouraged financial independence, and promoted lay participation, ensuring that churches functioned as authentic indigenous faith communities rather than colonial extensions of foreign missions22. The method proved instrumental in developing a resilient Korean Protestant movement that enabled local converts to assume ownership of their faith and establish dynamic congregations.

The impact of Underwood's work in the 1890s extended considerably beyond religious spheres. His activities contributed indirectly to the emergence of modern Korean civil society through the dissemination of Western concepts of individual rights and education, the promotion of literacy via Hangul, and the creation of environments where ideas about social equality and national identity could develop. Many early leaders of Korean independence and nationalist movements received their education in mission schools or maintained connections to church networks established during Underwood's tenure. Through these channels, his missionary work helped cultivate a generation equipped to advocate for Korean sovereignty and modernization during the challenging decades that followed.

  1. Mary F. Scranton

12/09/1832 ~ 10/08/1909

Mrs. Mary F. Scranton emerged as a transformative figure in Korean women's education and social reform during the late 19th century. When she arrived in Korea in 1885 at the age of 52, she became the first female missionary dispatched by the Methodist Episcopal Church's Woman's Foreign Missionary Society to the Korean peninsula. Her maturity and profound Christian conviction positioned her well to navigate the complexities of engaging with Korean women within a deeply patriarchal Confucian social structure that systematically excluded women from educational opportunities.

Scranton's most enduring contribution materialized in 1886 with the establishment of Ewha Hakdang, Korea's first modern educational institution dedicated to girls' education. This institution would eventually develop into Ewha Womans University. Rather than pursuing a program of wholesale westernization, Scranton's educational vision centered on helping Korean women become "better Koreans" by fostering cultural pride and self-respect alongside Christian education and practical skills. The curriculum she developed during the 1890s encompassed literacy instruction, biblical studies, and practical subjects designed to enable women's fuller participation in society while challenging the restrictive Confucian norms that relegated them to subordinate positions.

The philosophical foundation of Scranton's work integrated education, social reform, and evangelical mission. She understood Christian teachings as a means of restoring dignity and rights to women who had experienced systematic oppression under patriarchal structures. Her approach extended well beyond formal classroom instruction to include home visits and community engagement with marginalized women, providing both spiritual guidance and practical assistance in what represented a deeply incarnational missionary methodology.

Scranton's commitment to comprehensive social reform led her to establish Korea's first women's hospital, the Boguyeogwan . This medical facility addressed the healthcare needs of women who otherwise faced significant barriers to medical care due to rigid gender segregation practices. The hospital exemplified her holistic approach to social transformation, attending simultaneously to physical and spiritual dimensions of human welfare23.

Among her most innovative contributions was the development of a cadre of Korean women serving as Bible women (전도부인). These indigenous female evangelists possessed the cultural knowledge and social access necessary to reach other Korean women effectively. In 1893, Scranton deployed the first Korean Bible woman to Incheon, initiating a movement that substantially expanded Christian evangelism among Korean women while simultaneously promoting literacy and Christian values throughout the broader population.

Scranton also recognized the critical connection between literacy and religious education in addressing the widespread illiteracy among Korean women. She undertook the translation and publication of Christian educational materials in Hangul, making these resources accessible to women who had been excluded from formal education. Her 1890 adaptation and publication of a Korean-language catechism in the Seoul dialect gained widespread adoption in schools and churches, supporting her broader campaign to reach poor and marginalized women through literacy and evangelism.

The social impact of Scranton's work proved far-reaching and enduring. Her educational and healthcare initiatives catalyzed the modernization process for Korean women by providing unprecedented access to formal education and medical care, cultivating female leadership, and advancing concepts of gender equality grounded in Christian principles. Ewha Hakdang became an incubator for female leaders who would influence Korean society and religious institutions across subsequent generations. Her approach of respecting Korean cultural traditions while introducing Christian values facilitated the acceptance and lasting influence of her reform efforts24.

Over the course of her 25-year tenure in Korea, Scranton established institutional foundations that fundamentally altered the landscape of women's education, healthcare, and Christian evangelism. The Ewha Hakdang school, the Boguyeogwan hospital, and the Bible woman movement collectively represented a comprehensive transformation in opportunities available to Korean women. Her incarnational approach, characterized by her willingness to share in the hardships of the poor while advocating for their dignity and rights, opened new pathways for women's empowerment within a society long governed by patriarchal Confucian principles.

  1. Rosetta Sherwood Hall

1865 ~ 1951

In the 1890s, Rosetta Sherwood Hall (1865–1951) emerged as a transformative figure in Korean healthcare and education, establishing foundations that would reshape women's medical care and special education for people with disabilities. Her decade of work represents a significant period of institutional development and social change in late Joseon society.

Hall arrived in Korea in 1890 as a medical missionary representing the Woman's Foreign Missionary Society of the Methodist Episcopal Church, entering a context where Confucian social structures severely limited women's and children's access to Western medical care and educational opportunities. She began her work at Boguyeogwan, Korea's first women's hospital, collaborating with Mary Scranton and other female missionaries while treating Korean women and mentoring emerging Korean women leaders, including Esther Pak (박에스더, Kim Jeom-dong), who would become Korea's first female physician.

Hall's approach to healthcare was both practical and ideologically driven. She viewed the establishment of hospitals specifically serving women and children as addressing a critical gap in medical services for populations that had been systematically excluded from care. Her belief that serving "the most ignored and poorly treated" embodied Christian ideals guided her institutional development throughout the final years of the Choson Dynasty and into the Japanese colonial period.

Beyond direct medical practice, Hall actively supported the professional development of Korean women in medicine. She facilitated Esther Pak's medical education in the United States and supported her return to Korea as the nation's first Western-trained female physician, marking a significant advancement in women's professional participation.

Hall's educational initiatives extended to special education, beginning in 1894 when she started teaching braille to visually impaired girls, initially working with O Bong-rae, whose mother was among her early Korean Christian converts. This work evolved into the establishment of Korea's first school for the blind, located adjacent to the Gwanghye Women's Hospital in Pyongyang, which Hall founded in 1898. Her educational programs later expanded to include deaf education, establishing the foundation for Korea's modern special education system.

Hall's adaptation of the New York Point braille system to Korean represented a significant advancement in accessibility, enabling blind students to read and write in their native language and promoting greater social inclusion and independence for the visually impaired.

The medical institutions Hall established—including the Baldwin Clinic in Seoul (which became the precursor to Ewha Womans University Hospital), Gihol Hospital in Pyongyang, and the Gwanghye Women's Hospital—served as important centers for medical care and professional training. The Gyeongseong Women's Medical Training Institute, which she helped establish, later developed into Korea University's College of Medicine.

Hall's work earned her recognition within Korean society, including the title "Mother of Pyongyang" (평양의 오마니), and some contemporaries compared her social impact to that of Abraham Lincoln, viewing her as having advanced Korean women's social position through her integration of Christian service, education, and healthcare25.

Hall's activities during the 1890s established essential infrastructure for women's medical care in Korea, created pathways for Korean women to enter healthcare professions, and developed special education programs that continue to be recognized as foundational to Korea's educational system for people with disabilities. Her work demonstrates how individual commitment to institutional development can produce lasting social change.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

  1. 엡윗청년회 Epworth League

The Epworth League (엡웟청년회, 懿法靑年會) emerged as Korea's first organized Christian youth movement during the late 1890s. The organization traced its origins to the American Epworth League, established in the United States in 1889 and named after Epworth, England—the birthplace of John and Charles Wesley. The Korean branch was formally instituted in May 1897 through a resolution at the 13th Korea Mission Conference of the Methodist Episcopal Church26.This development represented a significant milestone in Korean Protestantism. The League introduced and popularized the term "youth" (靑年) within a Christian framework, preceding the YMCA's adoption of this terminology. Through this linguistic innovation, the organization contributed to the emergence of modern concepts regarding youth self-awareness and social responsibility.

The leadership structure incorporated both foreign missionaries and Korean Christian pioneers. Among the key organizers were Bishop I.W. Joyce, G.H. Jones, W.A. Noble, and J.O. Paine, working alongside Korean leaders such as Noh Byeong-seon (노병선) of Pai Chai School, Kim Gi-beom (김기범) of Jemulpo Church, and Lee Eun-seung (이은승) of Sangdong Church. The organization also included Yeomere (여메례), later known as Hwang Mere, who became the first Korean woman appointed to the Epworth League central committee from 1898 onward. She subsequently established and led the Joyce Youth Association, recognized as Korea's first Christian women's organization27.The rapid institutional expansion of the League became evident through the establishment of local branches in key mission centers within months of its founding. These included Jemulpo Church in Incheon (Ninde Chapter), Sangdong Church in Seoul (Mallalieu Chapter), Namsanhyeon Church in Pyongyang (Goodsell Chapter), and Jeongdong First Methodist Church in Seoul (Warren and Joyce Chapters for men and women respectively). By December 1897, at least five branches with over 150 members were operational, receiving support from local pastors, missionaries, and influential Korean Christians who were concurrently engaged in broader modernization and independence movements.

The scope of the Epworth League's influence extended considerably beyond traditional church activities. Members conducted public debates addressing social and religious issues, participated in Hangul literacy campaigns through "Everyday Schools" (매일학교), and created an environment that fostered the development of young leaders who subsequently assumed prominent roles in Korea's nationalist and independence movements. The Sangdong Church's League branch proved particularly significant in this regard, serving as a formative environment for future leaders such as Ahn Changho (안창호) and Syngman Rhee (이승만), both of whom would later contribute substantially to Korea's struggle for sovereignty.

The women's division, particularly the Joyce Youth Association, demonstrated pioneering efforts in women's empowerment. Yeomere and her associates established Korea's first women-led Christian association, providing young women with opportunities to develop leadership capabilities, literacy skills, and organizational experience. This foundation directly contributed to the creation of educational institutions such as Jinmyeong Girls' School, which Yeomere helped establish in response to the growing demand for modern women's education.

The Epworth League in 1890s Korea thus functioned as more than a conventional youth fellowship organization. It represented an early manifestation of the convergence between Christianity, social reform, and national self-determination during a crucial period in the formation of modern Korean society.

  1. 독립협회 The Independence Club/독립신문 The Independence

In the turbulent decade of the 1890s, the Independence Club (독립협회, 獨立協會) emerged as a significant force for Korea's social and political transformation. While not explicitly founded as a Christian institution, the organization's vision, membership, and activities were substantially influenced by Protestant ideals that its leading figures had encountered during their studies and exile abroad. Seo Jae-pil, known as Philip Jaisohn, later reflected on how his Christian faith shaped his worldview: "This religious influence gave me great strength throughout my life, and I swore to follow Jesus Christ, who preached the gospel of faith and love."28 Similarly, Yun Chi-ho described his mission as "preaching the gospel and giving education to my people," an aspiration that permeated the Club's activities.29

This shared Christian conviction united a generation of educated Koreans who viewed national reform and Christian ethics as complementary rather than separate endeavors. The Club's symbolic gesture of demolishing the Yeongeunmun Gate—a longstanding symbol of Korea's tributary relationship with the Qing empire—and constructing the Independence Gate (Dongnimmun, 獨立門) in its place demonstrated this integration of spiritual and political aspirations. During the dedication ceremony in November 1896, American missionary Appenzeller offered a prayer in Korean for divine blessing and protection over Korea's sovereignty, illustrating how religious and nationalist sentiments had become intertwined.

The Independence Club's reform agenda was articulated primarily through its newspaper, The Independent (독립신문), which advanced ideas that challenged traditional Confucian social hierarchies. The paper declared that "every person has rights given by God that cannot be taken away by anyone," a position that represented a radical departure from established social norms (The Independent, Jan 26, 1897).30 This egalitarian perspective extended to gender relations, with editorials asserting that "God created men and women with the same faculties and rights" (The Independent, Jan 4, 1898).31 Such positions, while controversial among conservatives, resonated with those seeking social reform.

These ideological commitments translated into practical campaigns against what the Club viewed as harmful traditional practices, including superstition, child marriage, and other customs deemed incompatible with modern civilization. The organization frequently praised missionary-established hospitals and schools as exemplars of Christian charity and social service, presenting Christianity as a modernizing force capable of advancing Korean society.

The Club's most ambitious political initiative was its sponsorship of the Joint Conference of Government Officials and the People (관민공동회), an unprecedented gathering that brought together state officials and citizens to discuss national reforms. The resulting Six Articles addressed fundamental governance issues: protecting Korea's sovereignty, ensuring transparency in foreign relations, conducting public trials for major crimes, establishing ministerial oversight of royal appointments, implementing public oversight of taxation and finances, and enforcing laws impartially. Historian Kang Jong Kwon argues that these principles reflected Protestant ethical influences and the Club's commitment to establishing modern civil society institutions.

Rather than viewing religious faith and political engagement as distinct spheres, Club members and their supporters understood Christian belief as inherently connected to patriotic duty. Churches organized prayer meetings focused on national independence, congregations sang hymns celebrating both divine and national themes, and believers were encouraged to pray continuously for the nation's freedom. A contemporary editorial in Daehan Maeil Sinbo captured this synthesis: "The root of the independence of Korea is that every believer prays to God not to lose the independence of the country and encourages fellow citizens to do the same."32

Despite its reformist agenda, the Club faced significant opposition from conservative officials who viewed its activities as threatening to established order. In 1898, royal edicts mandated the arrest of its leaders and the organization's dissolution. Nevertheless, the Club's brief existence had lasting impact. Its ideas, disseminated through The Independent, continued to influence Christian intellectuals and political reformers long after the organization's demise. Scholar Oh Kook Young observes that "The entire ideological background of The Independent is derived from Christian thought... The editorials and articles of The Independent consistently advocate the same principles as the early Protestant missionary work. Therefore, it is clear that there is a deep connection between Protestantism and The Independent."33

The Independence Club's model of Christian-informed civic engagement would later influence subsequent reform movements, including the March 1st Movement and the broader development of Korean democratic thought. What began as a group of reform-minded intellectuals motivated by Christian principles evolved into an organization that demonstrated how religious conviction, political activism, and social reform could be understood as mutually reinforcing elements of national modernization.

  1. 황성기독교청년회 Hwangseong Christian Youth Association

During the final decade of the nineteenth century, Korean society confronted the simultaneous challenges of dismantling feudal structures while defending against foreign imperial intrusion. Within this context, Christianity gained acceptance not simply as a religious alternative but as an embodiment of Western civilization and modernization. Missionaries actively pursued medical and educational initiatives, functioning as agents of enlightenment and social transformation. Reform-oriented intellectuals developed strong connections with the early Christian community, and Christian leaders assumed central roles in nationalist organizations such as the Independence Club and the People's Joint Association.

The compulsory dissolution of the Independence Club in 1898 and the reinforcement of the church's non-political position inevitably curtailed the direct political engagement of Christian leaders. Nevertheless, young individuals who had participated actively in the Independence Club and associated movements pursued alternative organizational structures to advance the nationalist agenda. In 1899, this emerging cohort—primarily comprising sons of elite families and students from modern educational institutions including Paichai School, foreign language schools, private academies, and government schools—advocated for establishing a YMCA, officially designated as the Hwangseong Christian Youth Association. In contrast to existing churches, which concentrated largely on the urban poor and lower social classes, the YMCA was conceived to include youth from varied social circumstances.

This proposal soon achieved realization through the establishment of the first student YMCA at Paichai School in 1901, creating the foundation for the official inauguration of the Hwangseong Christian Youth Association in 1903. While foreign missionaries initially supervised its formation, the organization's fundamental character and orientation strongly embodied the independent aspirations of Korean youth. The YMCA operated beyond the confines of a purely religious institution, developing into an educational and social center where young people acquired modern civic awareness and renewed national identity.

During this developmental phase, the YMCA's activities encompassed education, enlightenment, social networking, and nationalist participation. Through its close association with modern schools such as Paichai, the YMCA provided youth with practical knowledge and civic understanding while organizing lectures, debates, and discussion forums that united young people across social divisions. These efforts became recognized as components of the "National Salvation through Education" movement, significantly contributing to the development of national consciousness and self-strengthening. As former Independence Club leaders and various groups including the Sangdong faction became involved, the Hwangseong YMCA transformed into an active center for nationalist activities. Beginning in 1904, numerous Independence Club figures joined collectively, converting the YMCA into a practical stronghold for the national cause. The Sangdong faction's participation in 1907 enabled its evolution into a comprehensive, cross-class popular organization.34

Through this process, the Hwangseong Christian Youth Association addressed the organizational vacuum created by the Independence Club's dissolution and established a model for modern youth movements that synthesized education, enlightenment, and religious commitment. Its development represented a significant milestone in Korea's modern nationalist movement, creating an enduring organizational framework that connected faith with civic engagement.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Social Reform Movement

In late 19th-century Korea, the arrival of Protestant missionaries occurred during a period of profound social upheaval, widespread poverty, and growing disillusionment with entrenched customs and corrupt governance. For these early missionaries and the first generation of Korean Christian leaders, evangelism and moral and social reform proved inseparable endeavors. Operating against a backdrop of famine, infectious disease, and foreign encroachment, they initiated what became known as the "eradication of social evils" — a comprehensive movement addressing what they identified as the fundamental causes of individual suffering and national decline.

The campaign first targeted drinking and smoking, practices that were both widespread and deeply embedded in Korean social fabric. Drawing inspiration from the global temperance movement, particularly the Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU) in the United States, missionaries viewed alcohol as a direct catalyst for poverty, domestic violence, and moral deterioration. Church regulations incorporated explicit prohibitions on drinking and smoking, while pastors proclaimed that abstinence served as both evidence of genuine conversion and an expression of patriotic duty. By the late 1890s, publications such as The Christian Advocate regularly featured editorials denouncing alcohol as a "thief of the people's wealth" and condemning smoking as a habit that enslaved youth while depleting household resources35.

Closely related to this temperance effort was the campaign against opium, which emerged as another central pillar of the reform movement. During the late 19th century, opium began infiltrating Korean port cities through Chinese merchant networks, a development that both missionaries and Korean church leaders condemned as an insidious social poison. Presbyterian missionary Moffett documented instances of opium dens and addiction in his early reports, while churches implemented strict disciplinary measures against users. The movement strategically linked opium eradication with anti-colonial sentiment, emphasizing the connection between the drug trade and exploitative foreign interests.

The third component of the campaign focused on eliminating what reformers termed superstition. Early converts and foreign missionaries regarded shamanism, talisman use, and spirit exorcisms not as benign cultural practices but as impediments to both spiritual and national renewal. During periods of famine and disease outbreaks in the 1890s, Korean villagers traditionally sought protection from shamans against what they believed were spirit-induced illnesses. Missionaries responded by introducing Western medicine, establishing clinics, and promoting what they considered a rational, "modern" worldview. Converts frequently participated in public ceremonies burning fetishes and household idols, events that Christian newspapers celebrated as symbolic of societal transformation36.

The fourth strand of the reform effort addressed early marriage and concubinage practices. In a society where girls commonly married before reaching puberty — sometimes as young as eight or ten years old — and elite men routinely maintained multiple wives or concubines, missionaries identified systematic gender oppression. They condemned these customs both on biblical grounds and as social evils that undermined family stability and women's dignity. Churches denied membership to men who kept concubines, advocated for marriage based on adult consent, and promoted the ideal of monogamous, egalitarian Christian households.

This marriage reform initiative connected to a broader effort advancing women's rights through education and active church participation. Such emphasis represented a radical departure from Confucian culture, which had historically excluded women from formal education. Missionaries established girls' schools, trained female Bible instructors, and taught that women possessed souls equal to men before God. Although still influenced by Western paternalistic attitudes, this message provided Korean women with an unprecedented platform for challenging patriarchal structures.

A fundamental conviction undergirded all these reform efforts: the belief, rooted in American evangelical revivalism, that personal and social transformation were inherently interconnected. Korean Christians learned to view their homes, bodies, and moral decisions as constituting a "small church of God," while local congregations evolved into centers promoting literacy, hygiene, and modern citizenship alongside spiritual renewal. The "social evils" campaign thus reflected global Christian social reform trends while developing distinctly Korean characteristics shaped by colonial pressures and local resistance dynamics37.

This movement generated considerable controversy, sometimes conflicting with indigenous customs and occasionally being perceived as an assault on Korean cultural identity. Nevertheless, as contemporary scholarship demonstrates, substantial evidence confirms that the early Protestant church's commitment to temperance, opium prohibition, anti-superstition efforts, marriage reform, and women's rights extended beyond mere rhetoric to become institutionalized through church regulations, sermons, educational institutions, and publications38.

The "eradication of social evils" movement ultimately represents one of the most compelling examples of how early Korean Christianity functioned simultaneously as an agent of spiritual revival and a catalyst for comprehensive social transformation. While its legacy remains complex — interwoven with modernization processes, nationalist movements, and colonial entanglements — its historical significance as a defining characteristic of early Protestant activism is thoroughly documented.

  1. Educational Mission

Protestant missionaries from the United States and Europe arrived with a vision that extended well beyond religious conversion. These missionaries recognized education as their primary vehicle for introducing modernity and Christian ethics into Korean society.

The establishment of Pai Chai Hakdang by Henry Gerhard Appenzeller in 1885 represented one of the most significant early educational initiatives. The school's curriculum encompassed English, Chinese characters, Hangul, arithmetic, science, and political economy—subjects that exposed students to Western concepts of democracy, equality, and civic responsibility. Although many students initially enrolled primarily to acquire English language skills for professional advancement, Pai Chai evolved into something far more consequential: a center for social awareness and reformist thought. The institution simultaneously served as a training ground for interpreters and eventually became a focal point for emerging youth movements39.

The founding of Ewha Hakdang by Mary F. Scranton in 1886 marked an equally transformative moment for Korean women's education. As Korea's first modern school for girls, Ewha provided instruction in Hangul, English, mathematics, music, and biblical studies. The institution produced pioneering figures such as Esther Pak, who became Korea's first female physician and embodied the broader elevation of women's social status and the emergence of women's rights consciousness40.

Horace Grant Underwood's Kyungshin School, commonly referred to as the "Underwood Hakdang," extended educational opportunities to orphans and children from economically disadvantaged backgrounds. The school combined practical training with religious instruction, preparing future church leaders while serving the marginalized. For these missionaries, educational institutions functioned as more than venues for conventional learning—they were transformative environments where students encountered new social values, concepts of equality, and developing notions of national identity41.

Central to the missionaries' educational strategy was their deliberate promotion of Hangul. Recognizing the script's structural simplicity and accessibility, missionaries translated biblical texts and educational materials into the vernacular, challenging the Confucian elite's dismissal of Hangul as inferior. This linguistic initiative increased literacy rates among ordinary citizens while contributing to the growth of linguistic nationalism. The consequence was not merely expanded literacy but also the redefinition of Hangul as a symbol of cultural autonomy and national pride.

The missionary educational enterprise particularly empowered marginalized populations, especially women and members of lower social classes. Through biblical study and Hangul literacy programs, women developed greater personal agency and gradually assumed more active roles in society. Churches and schools became institutional centers for disseminating ideas about gender equality and challenging entrenched class distinctions42.

Furthermore, missionary education established the groundwork for Korea's modern civic movements. Mission schools and their affiliated churches cultivated leaders who subsequently played crucial roles in organizations such as the Independence Club, the YMCA, and the Manmin Gongdonghoe (All-People's Association), all of which were instrumental in Korea's enlightenment movements. These institutions also initiated temperance campaigns aimed at addressing drinking, smoking, and prostitution as social problems—an agenda closely aligned with broader reform objectives43.

By 1900, the educational mission of early Protestant missionaries had emerged as a significant force in Korea's modernization process. The students they educated would later become central figures in the independence movement and various social reform initiatives. Their work facilitated the introduction of Western educational methods, promoted Hangul literacy, enhanced the social position of women and marginalized groups, and introduced democratic and capitalist concepts that fundamentally altered Korean society. As one scholar observed, "Western-style education served as a channel through which advanced Western culture was introduced to Korea and contributed greatly to its modernization… missionary schools like Pai Chai, Ewha, and Kyungshin evolved into institutions of higher learning that nurtured Korea's first generation of modern intellectuals."44

Key Theological Issues

The Term Question

The final decade of the nineteenth century witnessed the emergence of the Term Question in Korea—a theological controversy concerning the appropriate designation for the Christian God in the Korean language. This debate represented one of the most significant doctrinal disputes within the nascent Korean church. While the Korean Term Question drew from the longstanding Chinese precedent that had previously divided Catholic orders, Anglican missions, and Protestant denominations across centuries, the Korean resolution proved remarkably expeditious compared to the protracted deliberations that characterized the Chinese experience.

The Korean debate centered on three primary terminological candidates: Hananim (하나님), Cheonju (천주, 天主), and Shin (신, 神). Scottish Presbyterian missionaries, particularly John Ross, advocated for Hananim, which referenced an indigenous Korean supreme deity documented in folk traditions and the Dangun creation narrative. Proponents of this term argued that it reflected a primitive monotheistic understanding compatible with biblical revelation. Cheonju represented an adaptation of the Roman Catholic and Anglican terminology employed in China (Tianzhu), while Shin functioned as a generic designation for divinity, deriving from the Chinese Shen tradition and introduced through early Korean translations by figures such as diplomat Lee Su-jung.

These terminological choices revealed fundamental theological tensions regarding the relationship between Christian doctrine and indigenous religious concepts. Advocates of Hananim, including Ross and subsequently James Gale, maintained that ancient Korean religious practice had originally centered on the worship of a singular supreme deity. This position aligned with contemporary "degeneration theory," which posited that original monotheism had gradually deteriorated into Korean shamanistic polytheism over historical time. According to this framework, Hananim offered both linguistic accessibility and theological continuity as a conceptual bridge to Christian Trinitarian doctrine.

This perspective encountered significant opposition from other missionary voices. Horace G. Underwood and certain American Presbyterian colleagues expressed concern that Hananim's association with traditional folk beliefs posed risks of syncretistic contamination that might compromise doctrinal integrity. These missionaries favored Cheonju or alternative renderings such as Syangjyu (a transliteration of Shangdi) to establish clearer demarcation between Christian theology and indigenous religious concepts.

Despite these theological reservations, practical considerations ultimately shaped the resolution of this debate. John Ross's 1887 New Testament translation, which employed Hananim and drew influence from the Shangdi edition of the Chinese Delegates' Version, achieved widespread circulation among early Korean Christian communities. This popularity persisted despite criticism from Seoul-based missionaries who objected to what they characterized as "northwestern provincialisms" in Ross's translation45. By the late 1890s, even Underwood, who had initially preferred Cheonju and later supported Syangjyu, recognized the practical futility of opposing Hananim's established usage. Following extensive deliberations within translation committees, a consensus emerged in 1900 when British, Scottish, and American Bible societies agreed to standardize Hananim for the Korean New Testament, which received official publication in 1906.

The Korean resolution's efficiency stands in marked contrast to the prolonged difficulties experienced by Chinese Christian communities. Three factors proved decisive in the Korean context. The first involved Hananim's cultural resonance with Trinitarian concepts, which Shangdi lacked within Chinese religious imagination. The second factor concerned the numerical superiority of Protestant advocates for Hananim, who successfully connected their terminological preference to emerging Korean nationalist sentiment, positioning Hananim as a distinctively Korean theological expression. The third element involved the relatively limited Roman Catholic presence in Korea, which reduced ecumenical pressure to adopt Cheonju—a dynamic that differed significantly from China, where Catholic missions maintained substantially greater influence.

The 1906 settlement, however, did not definitively resolve questions surrounding divine nomenclature. Han Jaedong's subsequent liturgical analysis identified important limitations in the Hananim designation. While Hananim effectively conveys the semantic concept of "one God," Han argued that this numerical abstraction, despite its theological precision, potentially constrains more comprehensive, imaginative, and embodied approaches to worship. In contrast, Haneunim (하느님)—derived from haneul (하늘, meaning "heaven")—employs spatial and concrete metaphorical language that aligns more closely with biblical patterns of divine naming, such as "Most High" (El Elyon) or "God in heaven" (Psalm 11:4). According to Han's analysis, such terminology cultivates what he terms a "bow-down" posture of reverent worship that better reflects the Hebrew shachah or Greek proskyneō, understanding worship as embodied prostration46.

The early twentieth-century compromise established a denominational division that continues to influence contemporary Korean Christianity. Protestant communities adopted Hananim as a distinguishing denominational marker, while Catholic and Orthodox churches maintained usage of Haneunim. This linguistic division continues to shape liturgical practices and denominational identity, though it sometimes obscures more fundamental questions about how language influences believers' conception of the divine.

The Term Question's resolution by 1900 facilitated both the indigenization and rapid expansion of Korean Protestantism, yet it simultaneously generated enduring theological tensions. Han's critique suggests that while the choice of Hananim proved historically pragmatic, it remained theologically incomplete. His analysis proposes that the more concrete terminology of Haneunim might offer Korean Christians a more substantial and biblically coherent designation that encourages deeper and more reverent engagement with the divine reality they address in worship.

Anti-Christian Leaders

흥선대원군Heungseon Daewongun 興宣大院君

When King Gojong ascended the throne in 1863 at the age of twelve, his father Heungseon Daewongun became regent and ruled Joseon for a decade characterized by significant policy shifts regarding foreign religion and influence. Historical evidence reveals a more nuanced picture than the common perception of Daewongun as consistently anti-Christian from the beginning. During the early years of his regency, he did not immediately implement harsh measures against Catholicism. Faced with the growing threat of Russian expansion in Northeast Asia, Daewongun actually considered diplomatic engagement with France, including the potential legalization of Catholicism, as a means to balance competing foreign powers and preserve Joseon's sovereignty. This pragmatic stance reflected the precarious geopolitical environment, as Qing China's influence diminished while Western imperial powers increasingly pressured Korea's borders. The complexity of his position is further evidenced by his family connections: several relatives, including his wife and daughter, were known to have converted to or sympathized with Catholic beliefs, while some officials in his circle maintained discreet relationships with Catholic communities.

This period of cautious openness proved short-lived. By the mid-1860s, several factors compelled Daewongun to dramatically reverse his approach. The immediate Russian threat had temporarily subsided, and reports from Qing China describing mass executions of Western missionaries and local Catholics emboldened conservative Confucian factions within Joseon's court to demand decisive action. Domestically, influential officials and local elites mobilized around the rallying cry "Defend Orthodoxy, Reject Heterodoxy" (위정척사, 衛正斥邪), portraying Western religion as a fundamental threat to the Confucian social order and national stability. Under mounting pressure from these forces, Daewongun initiated the most extensive anti-Catholic campaign in Korean history: the Byeongin Persecution of 1866 (병인박해).47

The persecution commenced in early 1866 and continued intermittently until Daewongun's removal from power in 1873. Nine of the twelve French Catholic missionaries operating clandestinely in Korea were captured and executed, including Bishop Siméon-François Berneux of the Paris Foreign Missions Society. The campaign extended beyond foreign clergy to target thousands of Korean converts, who faced imprisonment or death. While earlier scholarship often cited casualties exceeding 8,000, recent research suggests the actual death toll was likely between 2,000 and 3,000, with approximately 1,400 confirmed executions and additional deaths resulting from torture or starvation during imprisonment. This systematic persecution effectively dismantled the Catholic communities that had gradually developed throughout the peninsula, particularly in the central and western provinces.48

The violent suppression soon generated international repercussions. The execution of French missionaries prompted a French naval expedition later that year, known as the French Campaign against Korea (병인양요), during which French warships attacked Ganghwa Island in retaliation and attempted to compel Joseon to open its ports to foreign trade. Although Joseon forces successfully repelled the French assault, the incident reinforced Daewongun's belief that foreign contact would inevitably lead to further danger and betrayal. This conviction solidified following subsequent confrontations with Western powers, including the Oppert Tomb Robbery Attempt (오페르트 도굴사건) in 1868 and the American punitive expedition during the Shinmiyangyo (신미양요) in 1871. To demonstrate national resolve against such threats, Daewongun ordered the erection of stone steles (척화비) throughout the kingdom, declaring Korea's absolute refusal to negotiate or trade with Western nations—a symbolic manifestation of the intensified isolationist policy that defined his legacy.49

The Byeongin Persecution represents the most comprehensive anti-Christian campaign in Korean history and must be understood within the broader context of domestic political pressures and evolving international circumstances. Rather than constituting merely an expression of religious intolerance, Daewongun's anti-Catholic policy emerged from his efforts to stabilize the regime, consolidate conservative political support, and resist foreign encroachment. Despite the near-complete destruction of organized church structures, Catholic faith persisted in remote villages and mountainous communities50. The resilience of these hidden communities became evident two decades later when the Korea–France Treaty of 1886 gradually restored official recognition and worship freedoms for Catholics, marking a significant transition after decades of persecution and diplomatic isolation.
