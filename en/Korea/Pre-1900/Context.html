Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

Governmental Regulations on Religion: In this module, you will first describe the overall level of restrictions a government placed on religion in general during the selected decade. Then you will answer some specific questions about how government imposed various kinds of restrictions and/or regulations.

: Overall, how restrictive were the regulations on religion or some aspects of religion and how suppressive of the state over religion? Was there a state religion de jure or de facto? Were there privileged religions during this decade? (500-1000 words)

Severe persecutions took place in 1801, 1839, and 1846. Daewongun’s policy of exclusion, about eight thousand Catholics, including five French missionaries, were executed in 1866. This is the last and most severe persecution against Catholicism. What prompted the latest persecution was the external pressures on Korea at the time. With the collapse of the central power in China and Japan’s opening to the West in the 1850s, the Western influence in Joseon was imminent and unavoidable. In fact, between 1866 and 1871 Joseon faced hostile contacts from three military powers: the Russian Empire, France, and the United States. In the decade following King Go-jong’s ascension, the Catholic Church was, at last, free from persecution. While the anti-foreign/Catholicism sentiment was still strong, the court’s realization of the need to form an alliance with western power precluded further persecution of Catholicism. As a consequence, Joseon opened her ports to western missionaries and the first Presbyterian missionary Horace Allen arrived at Busan in 1884. They were not allowed to stay or buy houses outside Seoul, but they were not rejected by the government.

Early protestant missionaries reported that Koreans as a people without a religion (Underwood, 1908, see the Box). This impression was due to the fact that when the missionaries arrived in Korea, all ancient faiths were in a state of collapse and there was no distinct and controlling religion (Paik, 1980). In fact, three traditional faiths had claimed the Korean people: Buddhism, Confucianism, and Shamanism. Especially, Confucianism was the dominant ideology throughout the 500-year history of Joseon dynasty. It became part of Korean culture from then on.

Were there any significant changes in the education system during this decade that concerns religion? (e.g., Introduction of secular ideas in formal curriculum)

: Only upper class, Yangban, learned Confucian classics (漢學) in the traditional education. The missionaries brought new form of education to Joseon. The missionary school focused on training young men in three areas: mental, moral, and spiritual. The first students of the mission schools were mostly from lower class, Cheonmin 賤民and Sangmin 常民. The primacy of Bai-chai (培材) college and other mission schools was the teaching of Christ. Bunker explained it this way:

The brief outline does not take into consideration the constant personal influence of the Christian teacher upon the scholar, nor the wholesome uplift given to a crude boy by breathing the atmosphere of a Christian institution. Nor in this connection should mention of the nature of our textbooks be omitted. Our books are edited on strictly religious principles. They contain prayers, and many helpful hints as to God, His goodness, power and love as to Christ and his power to save all who call upon Him. There are many thoughts along these and similar lines which tend to turn the minds of the students into proper channels. One of our boys closed a letter to the writer with a benediction quoted from St. Paul’s writings. Not a bad beginning for a Bai-Chai boy. Certainly, a god ending. (1896, p. 361. See the box)

Jaisohn and other progressive Christian leaders served as teachers in Bai-chai high school and college and many of those students got involved in Hyeopsunghoe (協成會) and the Independence Club. These associations fought official corruption, inefficiency, and timidity toward foreign aggressiveness.

Women played a vital role in the education of Korean girls. Methodist Mary Scranton founded her school in June 1886 in Seoul with a single pupil, a child found lying against the city wall with her mother who was suffering from typhus (Clark, 2003). Scranton’s school became known as Ewha which was the first educational institution for women. Because of Ewha and other schools, a Korea woman’s life experienced a revolution. No longer was she reduced to the duties of reverence to ancestors and hospitality to guests. For once, the monopoly on knowledge did not belong solely to men for knowledge became just as important to them as to men for the full enjoyment and development of life.

From then on, the country never completely recovered and followed isolationism. During the nineteenth century, the political system became corrupt as practically to preclude securing justice. During this period, the door of Korea was forced open, and Daewon gun implemented an intensive close-door policy as his son, Go-jong, came to throne. The political power game between Daewon gun and his daughter-in-law, Queen Min, reshaped relationships with foreign countries during the late nineteenth century. Daewon gun maintained diplomatic relations with only Qing Dynasty and ostracized Western powers and Japan (1852-1876). This political stance resulted in General Sherman Incident with the U.S. (1866), the French campaign against Korea (丙寅洋擾) of 1866, rejection to Japanese’ sovereign’s message (1868), Korea Expedition with the U.S. (1871) and the following persecutions for Christians, mostly Catholics. He built the anti-appeasement steles (斥和碑) to remember his principles to denounce the western and Japanese power. However, his reform failed to get rid of corrupted officials and construct a strong country. Queen Min incarcerated him in a small palace. Go-jong and Queen Min signed treaties open the door to Japan in 1876 and the U.S. in 1882. Her administration committee positive relationships with Japan and western countries to hold the Russia’s expansion at the beginning. However, she lost the balance of her political directions among foreign powers and internal social and political issues. Compared to Daewon gun, she overly dependent on Qing dynasty and Japan to suppress Imo Gullan (壬午軍亂) and Donghak peasant revolution (東學農民革命). This intervention resulted in First Sino-Japanese War (1894).

The onset of the Sino-Japanese war on the first of August 1894 devasted the Korean land and plunged the Korean people into a welter of terror until it reached the Shimonoseki Treaty on April 17 in 1895. Chinese military was routed by Japan and lost its imperial power in Korea. In the midst of the war, Japan pressed the Korean government to change structurally by recalling the scattered progressives. The reputed political traitors of the Gap-sin coup in 1884 were able to return to Korea around this time. Philip Jaisohn and Chi-ho Yun (who will be introduced in the next decade section) came back to Korea. Japan subsequently installed a task-oriented supra-government reform organ, the Deliberative Council (Kun’guk Kimuch’o, 軍國機務處), largely manned by those progressives that seemingly implemented, what the progressives had originally sought. The reinstated Korean progressives and the reform, a measure welcomed by the progressives, were in fact a long-term instrument to break up the Korean leadership. Japan supported Buddhism, Shintoism, and other forms of Japanese religions in Seoul to compete against Christianity to expand Japanese power in the East Asia (Weems, 1962).

The Baby Riots of 1888 took place in the summer of 1888 in Joseon Korea. It was reported by Dr. L. Horton Underwood, one of the first female medical doctors, as well as female missionaries in Korea, and the wife of Rev. Horace G. Underwood, the first American Presbyterian missionary to the country. The uproar against Western medicine, hospitals and foreign schools-and foreigners in general-came just a few years after the first American Protestant missionaries started their work in Joseon.

“Some person or persons, with malicious intent, started a rumor which spread like wildfire that foreigners were paying wicked Koreans to steal native children in order to cut their hearts and eyes out to be used for medicine….. Babies, it was said, had been eaten at the German, English, and Americal legation, and the hospital, of course was considered by all the headquarters of this bloodthirsty work, for there, where medicine was manufactured and diseases threated, the babies must certainly be butchered.” (Underwood, 1904, see the Box).

For some weeks, the excitement among the people became so great that the lives and property of foreigners, especially missionaries, were thought to be in peril (Scranton et al., 1888). Finally, the rumor provoked a riot in which several Koreans died. Rumors of the barbarous crimes of missionaries and foreigners fueled a furious mob that assaulted a Korean office on street and gathered to attack a foreign school for women founded by an American Methodist missionary, M.F. Scranton, who recalled that, ‘One day a crowd gathered outside our gate and accused our servants of having stolen children and sold them to me for money, and threatened to kill them’ (Scranton et al., 1888). In view of these persistent attempts to attack the compound, missionaries had to be ready to leave at a moment’s notice. At the peak of the riot, Scranton also had to close the women’s school temporarily. Eventually, this incident brought about the diplomatic and military intervention of the USA, France and Russia, because foreigners and missionaries in general were accused of evil behaviors.

1894 Pyongyang Persecution

An instance of persecution in Pyongyang in 1894, shortly before the Donghak Rebellion, provides a good case in point of anti-foreign and anti-Christian atmosphere. At that time, Pyongyang was not a treaty city and foreign residence was not permitted there, much less the Christian religion. Yet, both the Presbyterian and Methodist missionaries had visited this city and made several converts there. Their justification for their activities was that the Japanese were already there and that Americans should deserve the same rights of residence as citizens of a most-favored-nation. Moreover, they purchased property in the name of local converts and made it into a clandestine church. However, the illegal Christian intrusion was soon detected by local residents and reported to the local authorities. The governor of Pyongyang quickly arrested and jailed the native Christians who had helped the foreigners, while the missionaries were saved from the same fate due to their extraterritorial status. Missionaries responded to local Christian requests “to secure protection for them,” by sending an urgent telegraph to the American and British legations in Seoul to press the Korean Foreign Ministry to release the Christians in Pyongyang. The Korean government responded quickly, but the governor, who possessed considerable power as a close relative of the current Queen, resisted the release order from the central government and continued torturing the Christians in prison. To make matters worse, the missionary residence in the city was sieged by a local anti-Christian mob. The house was stoned and the wall was torn down, while native water carriers were blocked from supplying water to the foreigners. In the vent, the governor surrendered to the central government and the Korean Christians were released, but the Pyongyang Persecution in 1894 provides further proof that there was no official or legal sanction for Christian mission work in Korea.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

Please summarize the overall level of social hostilities toward Christianity during this decade.

Despite the real sense that Joseon government was turning a blind eye toward missionary work, its optimism was nevertheless unfounded and did not reflect the reality of the foreign mission in Korea. The extension of Protestant mission work without official government sanctioning caused as many political complications with Korean government and society as the Catholics. The Protestant followed the Catholic’s lead to justify its illegal mission work under the unequal treaty and there was no difference between the two missions in terms of their neglect of Korean sovereignty and local jurisdiction.

In fact, anti-missionary opposition was conspicuous from the very beginning of the Protestant missions. When the Presbyterian mission began a hospital and an orphanage in Seoul, a vicious rumor soon spread among the local populace that the missionaries were sending them Koreans to America as Alaves and fattening them up for the table (Underwood, 1886). In 1887, street shops in Seoul closed in unison-a traditional means of expressing popular dissatisfaction-and Seoul merchants demanded the removal of all foreigners from the city center to suburban Yongsan, outside the city gate.

안식

Relationship between Donghak and Christianity (Catholicism, Protestantism) cannot be simply viewed as a relationship of rejection. Donghak, which emerged with the motto “Cheokwaeyang” 斥倭洋 (Rejecting Western and Japanese Influence) advocated by Choi Je-u who began the propagation in 1861, marked a significant milestone in Korean modern national history around the 1890s. Donghak incorporated Confucianism, Buddhism, Taoism, and folk beliefs as its core, and emerged as a national religion for a short period, rapidly spreading among the people even while facing suppression of Catholicism’s missionary activities.

Due to Choi Je-u’s use of the term “Cheonju” 天主 (Heavenly Lord), Donghak was initially misunderstood as a branch of Western learning and faced persecution.

Relationship with Catholicism: Catholicism was referred to as “Seohak” (Western Learning). Despite being criticized as a religion of foreign powers and facing persecution due to ancestral rites issues and the concept of “Mubumugun” 無父無君 (No Father, No Lord), the number of believers increased due to the involvement of the common people. However, several severe persecutions exacerbated social turmoil. Hence, Donghak intentionally emphasized its distinction from Seohak and Sahak 邪學 (evil learning).

“It was called Donghak, 東學 meaning Eastern Learning of Dongguk 東國 (East Country). And Seohak, Western Learning, is yin 陰, while Donghak is yang 陽, aiming to suppress yin with yang. Westerners lack order in speech, coherence in writing, and are solely interested in personal gain through evil tricks, lacking the 氣化 transformation in their bodies and the teachings of the heavens in their learning; they have only form but no essence. By not revering the heavens, Western learning is close to emptiness, and the learning has departed from the teachings of the heavens.” (Donggyeong Daejeon Nonhak Mun, p. 264)

Choi Je-u expressed caution towards the Catholic attitude towards ancestral rites. However, he himself faced criticism for adhering to Western learning. The initial stance of Catholics towards Donghak seems to have been marked by resentment by early French missionaries, likely due to the harsh persecution Donghak forces inflicted upon Catholic believers during the Donghak Revolution of 1894 in Jeonju and Gongju (Yoon Chi-ho’s Diary). However, an indicator of a possibly favorable relationship is the case of An Jung-geun, a Catholic who resonated with the rejection of foreign influence and joined Donghak followers in attacking a prison together in Northwestern areas.

Relationship with Protestantism: Initially, it was a relationship of rejection, especially with significant church damages in the southern Honam region. However, in the northwest, a common goal of anti-Japanese struggle was established. While Catholicism held an overall conflictual relationship, Protestantism formed different relationships in the southern and northwestern regions respectively. Particularly, McKenzie, a Canadian Presbyterian missionary in the northwest, engaged in missionary work while living among Koreans in traditional small, thatched homes and caring for the poor. Despite the Donghak forces’ combat in this region, records show that Donghak military was impressed by him and received baptism.

An example of rejection and protest occurred in the “Bokhap Sangso” 伏閤上疏 petition at Gwanghwamun in March 1893. It was a protest against the Joseon government’s recognition of Catholicism and Protestantism while refusing to acknowledge Donghak. Christian rejection notices were posted on American consulate buildings and missionary homes. The missionaries were criticized for acts like conducting unauthorized schools, collecting donations, and warned to return to their own country, with the accusation of pursuing self-interest. Joseon’s protection shielded the missionaries from harm, but efforts to understand each other were present in areas like Soraesa Church in the northern region where McKenzie and Reverend Seo Gyeong-jo were active. The church also offered protection to Donghak forces (January 1895). This cooperative effort extended with Son Byeong-hee forming the Progressive Society and Christian Seo Sang-ryun becoming its leader, eventually leading to the March 1 Movement.

Lastly, the exchange of faith practices between Protestantism and Donghak is explained through the concepts of temperament and morning prayer (Hwang, 2014). Donghak followers adhere to five principles: spells 주문(呪文), clear water 청수(淸水), Holy Lord’s day 시일(侍日), rice offering 성미(誠米), and prayer 기도(祈禱). Clear water refers to the blood spilled when Choi Je-u died, and it symbolizes washing the body. Holy Lord’s day signifies Sunday, which in Protestantism is considered a holy day. Rice offering involves collecting a spoonful of rice from each person’s meal, saved up and offered to the church at the end of each month. Morning prayer refers to prayers done upon waking up in the morning.

1894 Pyongyang Persecution

An instance of persecution in Pyongyang in 1894, shortly before the Donghak Rebellion, provides a good case in point of anti-foreign and anti-Christian atmosphere. At that time, Pyongyang was not a treaty city and foreign residence was not permitted there, much less the Christian religion. Yet, both the Presbyterian and Methodist missionaries had visited this city and made several converts there. Their justification for their activities was that the Japanese were already there and that Americans should deserve the same rights of residence as citizens of a most-favored-nation. Moreover, they purchased property in the name of local converts and made it into a clandestine church. However, the illegal Christian intrusion was soon detected by local residents and reported to the local authorities. The governor of Pyongyang quickly arrested and jailed the native Christians who had helped the foreigners, while the missionaries were saved from the same fate due to their extraterritorial status. Missionaries responded to local Christian requests “to secure protection for them,” by sending an urgent telegraph to the American and British legations in Seoul to press the Korean Foreign Ministry to release the Christians in Pyongyang. The Korean government responded quickly, but the governor, who possessed considerable power as a close relative of the current Queen, resisted the release order from the central government and continued torturing the Christians in prison. To make matters worse, the missionary residence in the city was sieged by a local anti-Christian mob. The house was stoned and the wall was torn down, while native water carriers were blocked from supplying water to the foreigners. In the vent, the governor surrendered to the central government and the Korean Christians were released, but the Pyongyang Persecution in 1894 provides further proof that there was no official or legal sanction for Christian mission work in Korea.

기독교 (천주교, 개신교)와 관계는 단순한 배척관계로 볼 수 없다. 1861년부터 포교를 시작한 최제우에 의해 ‘관제창생’ 廣濟蒼生, ‘보국안민’ 輔國安民과 함께 서양과 일본을 배척하자는 ‘척왜양’ 斥倭洋을 모토로 일어난 동학은 1890년대를 전후로 한국 근대 민족사의 한 획을 그었다. 유교와 불교, 도교, 민간 신앙이 주를 이루고, 카톡릭이 탄압 속에서 선교활동을 벌이는 와중에 민족종교로 등장해 짧은 시간 동안 민중에게 급속히 퍼져나갔다. 최제우가 ‘천주’ 天主 용어를 쓴 것 때문에 초기에는 서학의 아류로 오해받아 박해를 받기도 하였다.

카톨릭과의 관계: 카톨릭은 서학 (western learning)이라 불리었다. 서양세력이라는 것과 조상제사문제로 무부무군 無父無君의 종교로 비난 받으면서 박해를 겪으면서도 교인 수는 민중계층의 입료로 인해 증가했다. 그라나 여러번의 심각한 박해는 사회 혼란을 가중시키는 결과를 낳았다. 따라서, 동학은 서학에 반대여야하며, 사학邪學과는 다르다는 것을 의도적으로 밝히려했다.

“이것을 동학(東學) 이라고 불렀다. 동국東國의 학문이라는 뜻이다. 그리고 서학西 學 은 음陰이요, 동학은 양陽이므로 양으로써 음을 제압하려고 하였다. … 서양 사람은 말에 차례가 없고, 글에 조리가 없으며, 도무지 한울님을 위하는 속셈이 없고 오직 제 몸을 위한 술첵을 바라며, 몸에는 기화 氣化의 신비가 없고, 학學에는 한울님의 가르침이 없고 형식만 있지 실속은 없다. 이와같이 한울님을 위하지 않으므로 도道는 허무에 가깝고 학學은 한울님을 떠났다.” (동경대전 논학문, p. 264)

개신교와의 관계: 초기에는 배척관계였다. 특히 남쪽 호남지역의 교회 피해가 컸으나, 서북지역에서는 ’항일투쟁’이라는 공동목표가 설정되었다. 카톨릭과는 전체적으로 갈등관계에 있었으나, 개신교는 남부지역과 서북지역에서 다른 관계를 형성했다. 특히 서북지역에서 활동했던 Mckenzie (캐나다 장로회, 최초 한국 선교사)는 한국 전통 조그만 초가집에서 한국사람과 똑같이 생활하면서도 동네 가난한 이들을 돌보며 선교를 했다. 동학군이 이 지역에 전투를 벌였지만, Mckenzie에게 감동받았고 세례를 받았다는 기록이 있다.

Relationship between Donghak and Christianity (Catholicism, Protestantism) cannot be simply viewed as a relationship of rejection. Donghak, which emerged with the motto “Cheokwaeyang” 斥倭洋 (Rejecting Western and Japanese Influence) advocated by Choi Je-u who began the propagation in 1861, marked a significant milestone in Korean modern national history around the 1890s. Donghak incorporated Confucianism, Buddhism, Taoism, and folk beliefs as its core, and emerged as a national religion for a short period, rapidly spreading among the people even while facing suppression of Catholicism’s missionary activities.

Due to Choi Je-u’s use of the term “Cheonju” 天主 (Heavenly Lord), Donghak was initially misunderstood as a branch of Western learning and faced persecution.

Relationship with Catholicism: Catholicism was referred to as “Seohak” (Western Learning). Despite being criticized as a religion of foreign powers and facing persecution due to ancestral rites issues and the concept of “Mubumugun” 無父無君 (No Father, No Lord), the number of believers increased due to the involvement of the common people. However, several severe persecutions exacerbated social turmoil. Hence, Donghak intentionally emphasized its distinction from Seohak and Sahak 邪學 (evil learning).

“It was called Donghak, 東學 meaning Eastern Learning of Dongguk 東國 (East Country). And Seohak, Western Learning, is yin 陰, while Donghak is yang 陽, aiming to suppress yin with yang. Westerners lack order in speech, coherence in writing, and are solely interested in personal gain through evil tricks, lacking the 氣化 transformation in their bodies and the teachings of the heavens in their learning; they have only form but no essence. By not revering the heavens, Western learning is close to emptiness, and the learning has departed from the teachings of the heavens.” (Donggyeong Daejeon Nonhak Mun, p. 264)

Choi Je-u expressed caution towards the Catholic attitude towards ancestral rites. However, he himself faced criticism for adhering to Western learning. The initial stance of Catholics towards Donghak seems to have been marked by resentment by early French missionaries, likely due to the harsh persecution Donghak forces inflicted upon Catholic believers during the Donghak Revolution of 1894 in Jeonju and Gongju (Yoon Chi-ho’s Diary). However, an indicator of a possibly favorable relationship is the case of An Jung-geun, a Catholic who resonated with the rejection of foreign influence and joined Donghak followers in attacking a prison together in Northwestern areas.

Relationship with Protestantism: Initially, it was a relationship of rejection, especially with significant church damages in the southern Honam region. However, in the northwest, a common goal of anti-Japanese struggle was established. While Catholicism held an overall conflictual relationship, Protestantism formed different relationships in the southern and northwestern regions respectively. Particularly, McKenzie, a Canadian Presbyterian missionary in the northwest, engaged in missionary work while living among Koreans in traditional small, thatched homes and caring for the poor. Despite the Donghak forces’ combat in this region, records show that Donghak military was impressed by him and received baptism.

An example of rejection and protest occurred in the “Bokhap Sangso” 伏閤上疏 petition at Gwanghwamun in March 1893. It was a protest against the Joseon government’s recognition of Catholicism and Protestantism while refusing to acknowledge Donghak. Christian rejection notices were posted on American consulate buildings and missionary homes. The missionaries were criticized for acts like conducting unauthorized schools, collecting donations, and warned to return to their own country, with the accusation of pursuing self-interest. Joseon’s protection shielded the missionaries from harm, but efforts to understand each other were present in areas like Soraesa Church in the northern region where McKenzie and Reverend Seo Gyeong-jo were active. The church also offered protection to Donghak forces (January 1895). This cooperative effort extended with Son Byeong-hee forming the Progressive Society and Christian Seo Sang-ryun becoming its leader, eventually leading to the March 1 Movement.

Lastly, the exchange of faith practices between Protestantism and Donghak is explained through the concepts of temperament and morning prayer (Hwang, 2014). Donghak followers adhere to five principles: spells 주문(呪文), clear water 청수(淸水), Holy Lord’s day 시일(侍日), rice offering 성미(誠米), and prayer 기도(祈禱). Clear water refers to the blood spilled when Choi Je-u died, and it symbolizes washing the body. Holy Lord’s day signifies Sunday, which in Protestantism is considered a holy day. Rice offering involves collecting a spoonful of rice from each person’s meal, saved up and offered to the church at the end of each month. Morning prayer refers to prayers done upon waking up in the morning.
