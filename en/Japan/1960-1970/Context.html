[South Korea]

1965年9月、日本基督教団団長大村勇、韓国基督長老教会の第50回総会に出席。日本代表団受け入れをめぐって激論。

1967年8月、日本基督教団と韓国の大韓イエス長老会・基督教大韓監理会・韓国基督教長老会との協力草案成立

Japan-South Korea diplomatic normalization

In 1965, Japan and South Korea signed the Japan-South Korea Basic Treaty, legally settling their colonial relationship and normalizing diplomatic relations. On June 22 of the same year, Japan and South Korea signed the treaty, which stipulated that Japan would provide South Korea with $300 million in grants and $200 million in loans. They confirmed that all property and claims between the two countries had been “completely and finally settled.”

However, the normalization of Japan-South Korea relations was prioritized over the settlement of colonial rule, as both countries sought to strengthen the anti-communist through economic cooperation (Postwar Japan-South Korea Relations, p. 71). Therefore, the two countries set aside their differences in historical perception and aimed to achieve mutual benefits through economic cooperation, namely economic development and political stability, to strengthen the anti-communist (p. 81).

As a result, there had been criticism in South Korea that the “settling of the past” had not been adequately addressed, and there had been calls for thorough “decolonization” under the “nationalism” (81).

Within Japan, criticism arose against Japan's involvement in the “Cold War.” Looking at the current situation in Asia, South Korean troops were also deployed to South Vietnam to fight alongside U.S. forces. Therefore, it was believed that signing a formal treaty with South Korea would result in Japan effectively intervening in the Vietnam War alongside South Korea, leading to criticism within Japan. Japan was criticized for being “dragged into” the Cold War on the Korean Peninsula and for having its “peace taken away.” A common point in criticism within Japan is that little attention was paid to settling colonial rule (82).

参考文献『戦後日韓関係史』有斐閣アルマ、2017

[United States of America]
The U.S.-Japan Security Treaty, signed simultaneously with the 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty, established an unequal relationship between Japan and the United States. As a result, from February 1957, Japan began efforts to revise the treaty. Initially, the U.S. side was opposed to any revision, but after several shifts in policy, negotiations between Japan and the U.S. formally began in October 1958. By 1960, the negotiations were concluded: Japan’s request for a clearer relationship with the United Nations was acknowledged, the clause regarding internal disturbances was removed, and a treaty term was established (Miyashita, 72).

On January 19, 1960, the new U.S.-Japan Security Treaty, the Japan-U.S. Status of Forces Agreement, and five other related documents were signed in Washington.

Meanwhile, in Japan, the anti-security treaty movement gained momentum. In March 1959, the “National Council to Block the Security Treaty Revision” was established, and opposition movements expanded nationwide (Miyashita, 73).

Compared to the old treaty, the new U.S.-Japan Security Treaty undeniably established a more “equal” relationship between Japan and the United States and was far easier for Japan to accept. However, it is also true that at its core, the new treaty still maintained an asymmetrical relationship of exchange between “personnel” and “bases”—with the U.S. providing military forces and Japan providing bases—just as under the old treaty (Miyashita, 74).

As the Vietnam War intensified, anti-war sentiment in Japan grew. Around May 1965, Japan requested that the United States temporarily halt the bombing of North Vietnam. Furthermore, in July, Japan conveyed its concerns to the U.S. regarding the use of Okinawa-based B-29 bombers for maritime bombing missions over Vietnam. This stance caused growing distrust toward Japan within the U.S. government (Inoue, 124).

1951年にサンフランシスコ講和条約と同時に締結された日米安全保障条約は、米国と不平等な関係であったため、1957年2月から条約の改訂に取り組んだ。当初は米国側は否定的であり、何度かの政策変更ののち、1958年10月から日米間での交渉が開始。1960年には、交渉は終了し、日本側の希望であった国連との関係が明確かされ、内乱条項が削除され、条約期限が設定された（Miyashita, 72)

1960年1月19日、新安保条約、日米地域協定、その他交換公文など７つの文章がワシントンにおいて調印された。

一方、日本国内では、反安保運動が活発になり、1959年3月には「安保改正阻止国民会議」が結成され、反対運動は全国規模へ拡大した（Miyashita, 73)

新安保条約は旧安保条約と比べて、日本と米国がより「平等」であり、日本にとっては遥かに受け入れやすいものとなったことは、否定できない。しかし、その一方で、新安保条約はその本質において「ヒト」と「モノ」の交換、すなわち米国は兵力を提供し、日本は基地を提供するという旧安保条約時の非対象な関係を基本的に維持しているということも事実である（Miyashita, 74)

ベトナム戦争に対し日本国内で反戦運動が高まり、1965年5月ころには北爆の一時停止を日本ば米国に要請し、さらに7月には沖縄からベトナムへのB29爆撃機の渉洋爆撃に対して米国に懸念をつたえるなどした。このような日本の姿勢に米国は不信感を募らせた（Inoue, 124)
