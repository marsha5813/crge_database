Japan: Agency (1940-1950)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

List at least 3 and up to 10 leaders in this decade.

Kagawa, Toyohiko/ 賀川豊彦

Tomita, Mitsuru/ 富田満

Kozaki, Michio/小崎道雄

Summarize the key contributions/influences of each leader in the decade (500-1,000 words).

☐Kagawa, Toyohiko/ 賀川豊彦

Toyohiko Kagawa was one of the leading figures in the Christian community in the 1940s. Although he was known as a pacifist Christian, during this period, he struggled with his relationship with the state while also actively cooperating with Japanese national policy as a Japanese citizen.

When he inspected the area, Kagawa began missionary work in Manchuria around 1938. Upon his return to Japan, he proposed the establishment of Christian pioneer villages in Manchuria to the Japanese Christian Federation, and this initiative was approved as a project of the Christian Federation. As chairman of the Manchuria Christian Pioneer Village Committee, Kagawa actively promoted the recruitment for this project in his newspaper, “The Kingdom of God” (July 1940 issue) (Nakamura, 194–195).

Subsequently, the organized Manchuria Christian Settlement Corps was a national policy cooperation project aimed at “equipping those engaged in Manchuria’s settlement and construction efforts with the spirit and techniques of settlement, guided by Christian principles” (Ishihama, 148). At the training centers for settlement, the training period lasted one month, during which various lectures were held on topics such as “national principles” and “Manchurian resettlement.” Additionally, courses such as “Bible lectures” and “Christian peasantry” were conducted, with many prominent Christians, including Kagawa, participating as instructors (Ishihama, 148).

As an influential Christian, Kagawa actively recruited members through his magazine and promoted immigration to Manchuria. He stated, “The vast plains of Manchuria are waiting for the Japanese to come (omitted). We who believe in the Creator of the universe as our Heavenly Father and God, and in the peoples of all nations as children of God, can say that we possess the fundamental concept of the harmony of the five races. (Excerpt) Japanese rural youth burning with the blood of the cross gather together. Moreover, participate in the construction of a new Japan(「「満州の広野は日本人野来るのを待っている（中略）彼らは五族協和の精神をもって宇宙の創造者を天父と神事、世界各国の諸民族を神の子と信ずる我らは、五族協和の根本概念を持っているといってよい。（中略）十字架の血に燃ゆる日本の農村青年よ、集れ。そして新しい日本の建設に参加せよ」. (Nakamura, 195) He uncritically embraced the slogan of “five-race harmony” which was the founding ideology of Manchukuo. He linked it to Christian principles calling for participation in state-sponsored projects. (Nakamura, 195)

Although Kagawa demonstrated this proactive stance of cooperating with national policy, on August 25, 1940, after a worship service at Matsuzawa Church, he was arrested by the military police on charges of spreading rumors. The reason for his arrest was that his sermons at the time praised non-resistance, and there was an article titled “To Our Chinese Compatriots” in the “Kagawa Calendar” published in the United States. He was detained for approximately three weeks (Kawashima, 198).

　From June to July 1941, he visited various locations across the United States as a member of the “Christian Peace Missionary Group,” a delegation of Japanese Christians aiming to prevent the outbreak of war between Japan and the United States. He delivered lectures in various places advocating for reconciliation between the two nations (Kawashima, 199). Following this, Kagawa continued to visit the United States and participated in prayer meetings for war prevention, further solidifying his status as a central figure within the Christian communities of both countries. However, after the outbreak of the war, pressure on Kagawa intensified. He faced attacks from right-wing groups and threats of assassination and was detained by the police again in May and November 1943. The charges against him included allegations that his Christian lectures contained anti-war and socialist ideas. As a result, all of his Christian lectures were completely banned thereafter (Kawashima, 200). In such circumstances, Kagawa shifted his stance to support Japan’s war efforts, withdrew from organizations such as the “Alliance Against International Peace（国際平和反対者同盟）” and the “Japan Friendship Association(日本友和会）” and was forced to abandon his previous pacifist views, as he wrote in a letter. This demonstrates that Kagawa consciously transformed his ideology (Kawashima, 201-202). Like the military and leadership at the time, he positioned Japan’s invasion of Asia as a struggle for the liberation of Asian peoples and justified it (Kawashima, 202). In October 1944, he gave a lecture titled “The Prophecy of the Destruction of the United States” on overseas radio, condemning the barbaric acts of the United States in the name of Christ. Here, we cannot see the Kagawa, who had been critical of all wars before the war. Instead, he positioned Japan as a guardian liberating Asian peoples from Western imperialism and defended the “Greater East Asia War” as a “just war” (Kawashima, 203).

Several factors have been cited for Kagawa’s change. First, like other Christians, Kagawa had been influenced by the “imperial ideology” that had been part of compulsory education since the Meiji period. Second, Kagawa viewed Japan’s position in the Pacific War as a “weak nation” and as someone who lived in the slums and always stood on the side of the weak. He is said to have advocated for the people of Asia Japan colonized.However, this perspective lacked the view that “Japan, as the stronger power, was invading weaker Asian nations.” Third, Kagawa had family members and church associates he felt obligated to protect, which is believed to have led him to this shift in stance (Kawashim 204-208).

　In August 1945, following Japan’s defeat in World War II, Kagawa was appointed as a cabinet advisor at the request of Prime Minister Higashikuni and proposed a national movement of repentance, arguing that all Japanese people must repent.

On June 9, 1946, the “New Japan Construction Christian Movement（新日本建設キリスト教運動）” was planned by the Japan Christian Council, with Kagawa and the “Friends of Jesus Society” taking charge of the conference and fund-raising preparations (Kaino, 340-341). However, while Kagawa’s itinerant preaching tours were the only activities carried out nationwide for three consecutive years, other plans were not successfully implemented (Kaino, 346). From July 1946 to March 1949, Kagawa participated in 1,168 meetings over approximately three years, attracting an estimated audience of 730,000 people. Of these, approximately 186,000 decided to follow Christ. Kagawa held meetings at churches, theaters, community centers, schools, and factories across the country, averaging about 630 attendees per meeting, with 160 making a decision, demonstrating his popularity. (Kaino, 347) On the other hand, while Kagawa attracted many people, it was difficult to say that they had settled into the church.

Through his missionary work, Kagawa advocated the following points: (1) Rise up to rebuild Japan with a spirit of redemptive love; (2) Support a constitutional monarchy (in favor of a symbolic emperor); (3) A spiritual revolution rooted in the faith of the cross is necessary, not a materialistic revolution; (4) Promote the cooperative movement and build a society of mutual aid and cooperation; (5) Adhere to pacifism, not violent revolution; (6) Promote the world federation movement to achieve world peace; (7) and to strive for the expansion and enhancement of women’s rights (Kaino, 374).

Kagawa called for people to rise to construct a new Japan, which was Kagawa’s message within the Christian Movement for the Construction of a New Japan (Kaino, 375). Additionally, missionary stations were established following Kagawa’s missionary work, providing many people with opportunities to come into contact with Christianity (Kaino, 379).

On the other hand, Kagawa’s ideas did not deepen during the war, and he merely regained the spotlight he had enjoyed before the war. As new trends emerged, the impact of Kagawa’s arguments rapidly diminished (Kaino, 376).

Kagawa played a leading role in the postwar Christian revival, and many churches were rebuilt after the war. Additionally, his missionary work led to the establishment of missionary organizations, providing many people with opportunities to come into contact with Christianity (Kaino, 379).

☐Tomita, Mitsuru (1883.11.5 – 1961.1.15)

Theologian, pastor, and leader of the ecumenical movement.

Place of birth: Inba-muta, Higashi-Kasugai-gun, Aichi ken

Place of death: Tokyo

Education: Graduated from Kobe Theological Seminary, studied at Princeton Theological Seminary (graduation status unknown)

Born into a farming family, Tomita attended Sunday school from an early age and was baptized at Kinjo Church at the age of 16. He entered Meiji Gakuin University’s Faculty of Theology in 1904, but transferred to Kobe Seminary, which had been newly established by the Presbyterian Church in the United States. His studies at this seminary were an important period in the formation of Tomita’s theological and intellectual foundations, and it was here that his principled and conservative faith became deeply ingrained (Kaneda 32). He graduated from the school in 1909 and became a pastor at the Kobe Church of the Japan Christian Church in 1911. From 1918, he studied at Princeton Theological Seminary, and after returning to Japan, he served at several churches before becoming a pastor at the Shiba Church of the Japan Christian Church in 1920.At the time, Princeton Theological Seminary was characterized by a conservative theological orientation, and Tomita’s principled conservative theology was firmly established there (Kaneda, 32).After that, in 1929, he became the Central Committee Chairman of the Kingdom of God Movement and worked alongside Kagawa as the center of an interdenominational movement outside the church. In 1934, he became the Chairman of the General Assembly of the Japan Christian Federation, and in 1941, he was appointed as the first General Superintendent of the Japan Christian Church. After the war, he served as the President of Meiji Gakuin University, Tokyo Theological University, and Kinjo Gakuin University.

In the late 1930s, Tomita served as a representative of Japan’s Protestant churches, collaborating with the national government on its policies. In the late 1930s, Tomita, as a representative of Japan’s Protestant churches, showed a willingness to cooperate with the government’s policies.For example, in late June 1938, he visited Korea to persuade Presbyterian elders who refused to visit shrines, arguing to Chu Ki-chol(朱基徹, 1897-1944) that shrine visits were non-religious and urging them to participate. He insisted that shrines were not religious but merely ceremonial sites to be visited. However, the Presbyterian pastors still refused to visit shrines, deeming it idol worship, prompting Tomita to label their stance as a violation of the freedom of religion granted by Emperor Meiji.

On June 24, 1941, Tomita was appointed as the head of the Japan Christian Council. In January 1942, he visited the Ise Shrine along with the General Affairs Director to report on the establishment of the Japan Christian Council and pray for its development. The Ministry of Education demanded that the 11 branches of the Japan Christian Council be dissolved as a federation, so Tomita submitted a petition promising to abolish the branch system, and the Japan Christian Council was officially recognized.Regarding the Holiness Suppression Incident, the Ministry of Education notified Tomita, based on the Religious Corporations Law, of the revocation of the permits for the establishment of churches in the Sixth and Ninth Divisions and the resignation of clergy. Tomita then advised the relevant pastors to resign in order to maintain the Japan Christian Church.During the war, the Japan Christian Church, as “subjects of His Majesty the Emperor,” mobilized all its resources to donate a military aircraft named “Japan Christian Church( 日本基督教団号)” to the Greater Japanese Empire. In 1944, he was appointed as a member of the Ministry of Education’s Religious Education Policy Committee and played a prominent role as a leading figure in the Japanese religious community in implementing religious policies aligned with national policy from the perspective of the Church Protection Movement (Kaneda, 32). Additionally, during Easter in 1944, as the head of the Japan Christian Council, he sent a letter titled “Letter from the Japan Christian Council to Christians in the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere,” which was sent to Christians in the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere.the document now known as the “Letter from the United Church of Christ in Japan to Christians in the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere (「日本基督教団より大東亜共栄圏に在る基督教徒におくる書簡」).”

On August 28, 1945, immediately after the defeat in World War II, the Executive Council of the United Church of Christ in Japan held a meeting, and a circular letter was sent in the name of Tomita, urging the Emperor to reflect on and repent for the defeat.Additionally, at the first meeting of the Japan Christian Council’s General Assembly in December 1945, Tomita stated, “I do not consider myself particularly responsible for the war,” and those present did not raise any objections to this statement.Furthermore, regarding cooperation with the war effort as part of national policy during the war, he argued that “since it was a war, hoping for and intending its inevitable victory was the duty of the citizens,” and thus there was no issue or need for reflection. Such wartime leaders continued to hold similar positions after the war, and criticism of them was rare, as others hesitated to speak out against them. (Endo, 190)

At an extraordinary general meeting in June 1946, Tomita resigned as general superintendent and continued to support the denomination as a member of the General Council and the General Mission Committee. After the war, he continued to lead the ecumenical movement of the United Church of Christ in Japan and was involved in the founding of the Japan Christian Council.

☐Michio Kozaki (1888.11.16-1973.6.18)

Japanese pastor, theologian, and leader of the ecumenical movement

Place of birth: Tokyo

Place of death: Tokyo

Michio Kozaki was a pastor in the United Church of Christ in Japan and one of the outstanding leaders of the Japanese Christian church. He played a pivotal role within the church and served as a representative of the Japanese church outside its walls (Takenaka, 2).

Born in Tokyo as the eldest son of Hiromichi Kozaki, Michio received infant baptism from Niijima Jo in 1889. In 1912, he traveled to the United States, where he studied international law, sociology, and theology at Oberlin College, Columbia University, and Yale University, returning to Japan in 1922 (Takenaka, 5).Upon his return, he became a missionary at the Reinan-zaka Church founded by his father, Hiromichi and was ordained as a pastor in 1931. His decade-long education abroad significantly influenced his ecumenical character, and he participated in the International Missionary Conference held in Jerusalem in 1928 (Takenaka, 4).In April 1941, he participated in the Japan-U.S. Christian Conference held in Riverside, Southern California, as a member of the delegation to the United States. After the establishment of the United Church of Christ in Japan, he became director of the East Asia Bureau and was one of the central figures in the church.In this capacity, leaders of the Japan Christian Council, including Kozaki, engaged in activities aimed at urging Chinese Christian churches to sever ties with overseas missions and instead align with Japan (see “History of Protestant Christianity in Japan” by Akio Tsuchi, Shin-kyo Publishing, p. 360). Specific examples include the establishment of the China-Japan Christian Federation in 1942.Additionally, he engaged in exchanges with churches in “Greater East Asia” (including Southeast Asia), missionary work, Manchurian colonization, and assistance for Asian students studying in Japan.Kozaki was selected as director for these activities. The Japan Christian Council allocated approximately 114,000 yen of its 1944 operating budget of about 440,000 yen to the East Asia Bureau, illustrating the extent to which the council prioritized the bureau’s activities (Dohi, History of Protestant Christianity in Japan, p. 361).

After Japan’s defeat in World War II, in 1946, Kozaki succeeded Tomita Mitsuru as president of the Japan Christian Council. In 1948, he became the first president of the National Council of Churches in Japan (日本キリスト教協議会, NCC), which was organized as the successor organization to the Japan Christian Federation, and led the ecumenical movement. One of Kozaki’s notable characteristics was his initiative and independence. From 1946 to 1954, the Kyodan Shinpo (『(教団新報』) reported that he visited various churches in different regions, shared information about global trends, and worked hard to promote interaction between churches. The background to his activities was the support he received from Reinan-zaka Church (Takenaka, 13). Furthermore, at a meeting of religious leaders held in November 1947, when the Japanese government expressed its hope that religious leaders would contribute to the moral education of the people, Kozaki responded, “Religion should not be used for political purposes.If the government truly recognizes the importance of religion, it should be the politicians themselves who take the lead in believing in it and setting an example for the people.” (Takenaka, 15)

日本基督教団

The United Church of Christ in Japan was established in June 1941 as a Protestant church formed by the merger of approximately 30 denominations. Since the 1920s, the Japan Christian Federation has been promoting a movement to unite various denominations, but this has not come to fruition. However, due to factors such as the enactment of the Religious Corporations Law in April 1939 and the investigation of the Salvation Army in July 1940, representatives from various denominations sought independence from missionary organizations and protection from the state, leading to the formation of the Japan Christian Council.

At the founding general assembly held on June 24–25, 1941, at the Fujimi-cho Church,

Tomita Mitsuru was recommended as the first church leader, and Kozaki Michio as his deputy.

Although the denomination adopted a system of leadership, it was organized into divisions based on denominations with relatively similar ideologies, effectively consisting of 11 divisions. Each division had its budget, and although they were united, they functioned as a confederation of denominations (Kaino, 52).

The first issue of the “Church News(「教団 新報」）, published on August 15, 1941, listed the first 11 divisions and the names of the denominations affiliated with them. (Suzuki, 305-307)

First Division: Japan Christian Church

Second Division: Japan Methodist Church, Japan Methodist Church, Japan Holy Garden Church

Third Division: Japan Union Church, Japan Christian Fellowship Church, Japan Gospel Church, Japan Church, Christian Friendship Society

Fourth Division: Japan Baptist Church

However, in June 1942, a crackdown on Holiness churches occurred. Considering that maintaining the department system would be difficult under these circumstances, the United Church of Christ in Japan decided to abolish the department system at its first general assembly in November 1942 at the request of the Ministry of Education. It took a major step toward complete unification (Kaino, 53).The United Church of Christ in Japan actively cooperated with government policies during the war. After the outbreak of war, the church renamed its headquarters the Christian Patriotic Association. It reorganized its structure to streamline relations between the church, its branches, and associations, enabling it to carry out wartime activities efficiently (Dohi, 477).　For example, in the past, training sessions were held to encourage spiritual reflection and knowledge acquisition. However, these were replaced with sessions aimed at reforming attitudes deemed unworthy of “imperial citizen” and cultivating a spirit befitting “imperial citizen” (Dohi, 477-478). The first session, held in June 1942, was attended by 47 teachers selected from across the country. The Training Conferences were frequently held by various dioceses, sub-dioceses, Sunday School bureaus, Women’s Affairs bureaus, and the Women’s Moral Improvement Association. Some pastors who participated in these conferences interpreted them as indicating that “Christianity is greatly anticipated” (Dohi, 478).

In addition, it is known that churches and mission stations held ceremonies such as “remote worship for Miyagi,” “singing of the national anthem,” and “prayers for soldiers deployed in battle and soldiers who died in battle” (Dohi, 479). Furthermore, a letter written by the United Church of Christ in Japan to Christians in the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere contained content that affirmed the war as a means of saving Asia from Western invasion.

Chapter 1: The Common “Enemy” of Asia: The Christian Attack by Britain and the United States

Their racism and imperialist aggression are acts unbecoming of Christian nations, and Japan is fighting in self-defense against this injustice.

Chapter 2: Praise for the Japanese Empire and Its Culture: It is the mission of Christians to build a land of coexistence and prosperity in East Asia based on the Japanese spirit that lies beneath the large family nation with the Emperor at its head, thereby bringing God’s kingdom to earth.

Chapter 3: Praise for Japanese Christianity Rooted in the Japanese Spirit and Its Embodiment in the Church: Even Uchimura Kanzo, who challenged the Emperor’s divine right, is exploited.

Chapter Four: As witnesses to the Gospel of Christ, who realized the salvation of sin hidden in one’s own sense of superiority, let us collaborate in the construction of the “Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere.” (Dohi, 480)

It is unclear whether this letter was translated and reached Christians in Asia. However, toward the end of the war, the Japan Christian Council issued the “Guidelines for Missionary Work Under Decisive Battle Conditions” (August 1944) and, in response to the cabinet decision on the formation of the National Volunteer Corps, established the Tokyo National Volunteer Corps (June 1945).and the directive from Tomita, the general superintendent, urging all churches to hold prayer meetings for victory (June 5, 1945). These actions exhibited a sense of desperation or even fanaticism. (Dohi, 481)

The East Asia Bureau established within the Japan Christian Council mobilized pastors for pacification activities in the China region. This bureau also received substantial subsidies from the Greater East Asia Ministry. Furthermore, it sent 200 believers to the Manchurian Christian Settlement Village, where Kagawa played a central role (Nakamura, 196).

These 200 Christians were hostages offered by the Japan Christian Council to the state as proof of its cooperation with national policy and also served as evidence of the council’s collaboration with the state (Kaino, 45). Behind such activities, the council sought to defend itself by using the imperial system as a shield against the prevailing societal skepticism and distrust toward Christianity (Dohi, 479).

After World War II, the United Church of Christ in Japan did not dissolve but continued to exist as it was. This was mainly due to the efforts of those who sought to support the Japan Christian Council after the war and the influence of the missions that supported them (419). In October 1945, four American Christian leaders visited Japan, and the Japan Christian Council received assistance from overseas missions. In April 1947, eight North American missions formed the Inter Board Committee for the Christian Work in Japan (IBC), a joint committee established to support and cooperate with the Japan Christian Council. This support from the IBC was not necessarily welcomed by the Japan Christian Council, which had advocated for independence from overseas missions before the war (419). In June 1946, the Japan Christian Council declared the New Japan Construction Christian Movement, with Toyohiko Kagawa at its center, and actively engaged in missionary activities. However, as this activity demonstrates, the Japan Christian Council did not reflect on its wartime activities and instead sought to reorganize itself in response to postwar changes. As a result, the Japan Christian Council’s essence remained unchanged.

On the other hand, many denominations separated from the Japan Christian Council after the war. These include the Holy Public Church, the Japan Baptist Federation, the Japan Christian Church, the Japan Evangelical Lutheran Church, and the Society of Friends.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The movement to promote Christianity in the 1940s took place primarily after 1945.

MacArthur, who arrived in Japan as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, believed that Christianity would play an important role in the occupation policy. For this reason, despite Article 20 of the Japanese Constitution proclaiming the principles of freedom of religion and separation of religion and state, favorable treatment was extended toward Christianity. This occurred even though staff members of the Civil Information and Education Section (CIE) argued that such measures violated the principle of separation of religion and state.

Partly due to this favorable treatment by GHQ, a phenomenon often referred to as the “Christianity boom” emerged during the occupation period, backed by pro-American sentiment. Churches throughout Japan were filled with young people, Bible study sessions led by Tamaki Uemura were held within the imperial household, and Tetsu Katayama, a Christian, became Prime Minister (Kaino, p. 338).

1940年代のキリスト教を推進する運動は主に1945年以降になる。

連合国最高司令官として来日したマッカーサーは、キリスト教が占領政策において大きな役割を果たすと考えていた。そのため日本国憲法20条において「信教の自由」と「政教分離」の原則が示されているにもかかわらず、キリスト教に対しては優遇措置をとった。それはCIEのスタッフがそれは政教分離の原則に反すると主張しても、そのような措置が取られた。

このようなGHQによる優遇措置の影響もあり、占領期には親米感情を背景に「キリスト教ブーム」と呼ばれる現象も現れた。各地の教会には若者があふれ、皇室では植村環による聖書研究が行われ、クリスチャンである片山哲が総理大臣に就任した（Kaino, 338)

New Japan Construction Christian Movement

The New Japan Christian Movement was approved at the third extraordinary General Assembly of the United Church of Christ in Japan (Nihon Kirisuto Kyōdan) held on June 7–8, 1946, and was officially announced at the National Christian Conference on June 9, 1947 (Kaino, pp. 339–340).

This was a special evangelistic campaign initiated by the United Church of Christ in Japan, with its background rooted in Kagawa’s “Friends of Jesus Movement” (Kaino, p. 341).

The report of the movement submitted at the 5th General Assembly of the Church in October 1948, titled “Report on the New Japan Construction Christian Movement”, stated that the overall leader of the campaign was Chairman Michio Ozaki, and that it consisted of seven departments:

  1. Social Service Division

  2. Bible Evangelism Team

  3. Women’s Team

  4. Rural Evangelism Division

  5. Literature Evangelism Team

  6. Youth Evangelism Team

  7. Workplace Evangelism Team

This evangelistic movement was a comprehensive, large-scale campaign that included social welfare assistance, aimed at addressing the hardships and exhaustion of postwar Japanese society (Kaino, p. 341).

In Kagawa’s earlier “Kingdom of God Movement,” the slogan had called for the salvation of one million souls, but in this New Japan Construction Christian Movement, the goal was raised to three million souls. Ninety percent of the campaign’s budget was provided by Christian churches in North America (Kaino, pp. 345–346).

However, in reality, many of the plans gradually lost momentum, and only Kagawa’s itinerant evangelistic campaign, which continued uninterrupted for three years, remained active. As a result, although Kagawa’s campaign was only one part of the larger movement, it came to be remembered and perceived as the very core of the New Japan Construction Christian Movement (Kaino, p. 346).

Though Kagawa’s activities were only one element of this broader movement, between July 1946 and March 1949, he held 1,168 gatherings with a total audience of 734,654, of whom 186,508 made professions of faith. Evangelistic meetings were held in churches, theaters, public halls, school auditoriums, and factories across the country.

The average audience per gathering was 629 people, and the average number of professions of faith was 160 per event. While Kagawa’s energy was remarkable, the efforts of local churches and pastors also played a significant role (Kaino, p. 347).

In this campaign, Kagawa drew upon his earlier experiences from the Kingdom of God Movement, putting them into practice in his style of public speaking. His appeal in this movement was a call for people to rise up together for the construction of a new Japan—an appeal to those still crouched in despair amidst the confusion of the immediate postwar period, urging them to become fellow builders of a reborn Japan.

This was, indeed, the very heart of Kagawa’s message in the New Japan Construction Christian Movement (Kaino, p. 375).

Distribution of LARA Relief Supplies

(Relief supplies from Licensed Agencies for Relief in Asia, or LARA)

LARA supplies refer to the relief goods provided by Licensed Agencies for Relief in Asia (commonly known as LARA). This relief effort lasted from November 1946 to 1952, during which approximately 14 million people received supplies (Godo, p. 57).

LARA was established in April 1946 with the goal of providing relief to Asia, particularly Japan, Korea, and Okinawa, which had been devastated by World War II. It was organized under the parent body American Council of Voluntary Agencies for Foreign Service, Inc., an umbrella organization composed mainly of religious groups across the United States (Oku, pp. 175–176).

In cooperation with the Ministry of Health and Welfare, LARA supplies were distributed throughout Japan, especially in areas heavily damaged by the war. The relief included foodstuffs (such as powdered milk, flour, and canned goods), clothing, and medicines.

Since imports of goods from abroad were still restricted at the time, contributions to Japan were only possible through LARA. As a result, donations came not only from the mainland United States, but also from Canada, Hawaii, Brazil, Argentina, and other parts of South America (Iino, pp. 324–326).

“Letters of gratitude” sent by Japanese recipients often expressed appreciation toward both America and American Christians. In this way, LARA’s efforts became one of the factors that helped foster a favorable impression of Christianity among the Japanese people.

Although this was an activity undertaken by American private organizations, it can also be seen as having indirectly supported U.S. occupation policy.

ララ物資の配布/ Relief supplies from Lisensed Agencies for Relief in Asia (LARA)

ララ物資とは、Lisensed Agencies for Relief in Asia(アジア救援公認団体, 通称ララ）による救援物資のことである。この救援活動は、1946年11月から1952年まで続き約1400万人の人々が物資を受け取った（Godo, 57)。ララは、1946年４月、第二次世界大戦で困窮に陥ったアジア、特に「日本・朝鮮及び沖縄の人びとの救済事業を行なうことを目的に、全米の各種宗教団体を中心とする 海外事業運営篤志団アメリカ協議会（American Council of Voluntary Agencies for Foreign Service, Inc.）を親団体として結成された(Oku, 175-176)。

　ララ物資は厚生省と協力し、日本全国、なかでも戦争被害の多かった地域を中心に配布され、食糧（粉ミルク、小麦粉、缶詰など）、衣類、医薬品などの物資が送られた。 海外からの物資の送付が制限されていたこともあり、ララ物資を通してのみ日本への寄付が可能となった。そのためアメリカ本土だけではなく、カナダやハワイ、ブラジルやアルゼンチンなどの南米からも多くの寄付が寄せられた(Iino, 324－326)。

これらを受け取った日本人からの「感謝の手紙」には、アメリカ及びアメリカのキリスト教徒に関する感謝の言葉が寄せられている。このことからも、日本人がキリスト教に親しみを抱くきっかけの一つになったと考えられる。またこのことは、アメリカの民間団体による活動ではあるが、結果的にアメリカの占領政策を支えた活動であったとも言える。

Four Missionaries from the United States in 1945

In October 1945, at the invitation of Kotaro Miyakoda, Executive Secretary of the Nihon Kirisuto Kyōkai Kyōgikai (Japanese Christian Council), four American Christians came to Japan. The group consisted of:

•Luman Shafer, former principal of Ferris Girls’ School and chairman of the Japan Committee of the Foreign Missions Conference of North America

•Walter Van Kirk, board member of the Federal Council of Churches of Christ in America

•James Baker, chairman of the International Missionary Council

•Douglas Horton, pastor (Tsuji, p. 117).

They were appointed as delegates for this visit to Japan “in order to resume direct dialogue with Christian fellow believers in Japan, to strengthen the bonds of spiritual unity in Jesus Christ, and to discuss the common tasks of the ecumenical Church” (Ikehata, p. 120).

These four individuals were the first civilians (non-military personnel) to arrive in Japan after the war. Upon their arrival, they received special treatment from the U.S. Army in Japan for their transportation and accommodations within the country, but all travel expenses and other costs were covered by churches back in the United States (Ikehata, p. 120).

During their approximately three-week stay, they visited Tokyo, Nagoya, Kyoto, and other locations, and also flew over Hiroshima and Nagasaki on U.S. military aircraft to observe the cities from above (Ikehata, p. 121).

In addition to meeting with many Japanese Christians — including Tamon Maeda, Toyohiko Kagawa, Michio Ozaki, Michio Kawai, Tadaoki Yamamoto, Soichi Saito, Kankei Yano, Senji Tsuru, and Ken Ishihara — they also met with government officials and even Emperor Showa himself (Tsuji, p. 117; Ikehata, p. 121).

四人の宣教師/ Four missionaries from United States in 1945

1945年10月、日本基督教協議会主事の都田恒太郎の呼びかけで、4名のクリスチャンが来日した。メンバーは、元フェリス女学校 長で北米外国宣教協議会日本委員会委員長のルーマン・シェーファー （Luman Shafer）、北米教会連盟協議会理事ウォルター・ヴァン・カー ク（Walter Van Kirk）、 国際宣教協議会議長ジェームス・ ベイカー （James Baker）、牧師ダグラス・ホートン（Douglas Horton）である(Tsuji, 117)。

　　彼らは、「日本にいるキリスト教信者仲間との直 接対話再開のため、イエス・キリストにある生きた霊的団結の絆を強固にする ため、及び共通の課題である超教派の教会の形成（the common tasks of ecumenical Church）を協議するため」日本訪問団代表に任命されたのであった(Ikehata, 120) 。この4名は、軍人以外の一般民市民として初めて日本に到着した人々であった。到着後、彼らは日本駐在のアメリカ陸軍により日本国内における移動 や宿泊において特別待遇を受けたが、旅費やその他の費用は全て本国の教会の 負担によるものであった(Ikehata, 120)。

　彼らは約３週間にわたり東京や名古屋、京都、多さな感度を訪問し、占領軍機で上空から広島と長崎も訪問している(Ikehata,121) そして、多くのクリスチャン（前田多門、賀川豊彦、小崎道雄、河合道、山本忠興、斎藤惣 一、矢野貫城、都留仙次、石原謙ほか）だけではなく、政府関係者や昭和天皇などにも面会してる（Tsuji,117 and Ikehata, 121)

During their audience with Emperor Showa, the Emperor expressed gratitude for the past activities of Christian churches and also stated his hope for the prompt reconstruction of Christian schools and the advancement of their work going forward (Okazaki, pp. 54–55).

In the report submitted after their return, titled “The Return to Japan,” it was written that Japan, under occupation and aiming for democratization, presented a significant opportunity for Christian mission work. However, there was no indication of any intention to forcibly impose Christianity on Japan (Ikehata, p. 125).

The group proposed that Bibles and hymnals be sent to Japan and that experienced missionaries be dispatched to strengthen ties with Japanese Christian churches (Okazaki, p. 55). On the other hand, the United Church of Christ in Japan requested that “as far as possible, highly qualified missionaries with special skills” be sent, but also asked that the number of missionaries be limited, given the instability of Japanese church finances (Okazaki, p. 55).

From their report, it is clear that Christianity, like Shinto (which would remain simply “one of the religions in Japan” once the government ceased treating it as a state religion and stopped forcing shrine visits in all schools), would continue to exist in Japan. Moreover, the four missionaries presented themselves not as promoters of Christianity to Japan, but rather as “helpers” working alongside Japanese Christians in mission work.

They also noted that when deciding the timing and number of missionaries to be sent, it was important to widely solicit the opinions of Japanese Christians across the country. As a result, they concluded:

“What the Japanese churches desire from us is our sincere goodwill, prayers, financial assistance, and at first a small number of experienced missionaries; later, if requested by the Japanese churches, the number could be increased.” (Ikehata, p. 125)

Based on their report, American Christian churches sought to understand the situation in Japan and to carefully consider the appropriate methods for mission work and activities there.

　昭和天皇との謁見では、昭和天皇からこれまでのキリスト教会の活動に対する感謝が述べられると共に、今後のキリスト教系学校の速やかな再建と事業の進展を期待する旨が述べられた（Okazaki ,54-55)

　　帰国後に提出された報告書である”The Return to Japan”には、占領下で民主化を目指す日本は、キリスト教宣教において大きな機会であるとは記されているが、そこには、キリスト教を無理やりに日本に 押し付けようとするような気配は感じられない(Ikehata,125) 。かれらは、聖書や讃美歌集を日本に送り、日本のキリスト教会との連携を強化するために、経験豊富な宣教師を日本に派遣することが望ましいと提案してた（Okazaki, 55) 一方で、日本基督教団は、「なるべく優秀で特技を持つ」宣教師の派遣を希望しているが、日本の教科の女性が安定していないことからも、宣教師の数を制限して欲しいとも書かれている（Okazaki, 55) 。

　4人の報告書からは、キリスト教も神道（政府が国教 扱いをやめ、全ての学校での神社参拝の強要をやめれば）「日本における一つの宗教」として存続するだろうとしている。さらに、彼らがキリスト教を推進するのではなく、あくまでも「援助者」として、日本のキリスト教者と共に宣教を行おうとする姿勢を示している。そして、宣教師を送る時期や人数についても、各地で幅広く日本人キリス ト教者に意見を求め、様々な意見からの結論として、「日本の教会が我々に求 めているのは、我らの心からなる思いと祈りと金銭的な援助、そして少数の経 験豊かな宣教師をまず送り、後に日本の教会からの要請があれば数を増やすことだ。」と最後に述べている (Ikehata,125 ) 。

　 彼らの報告をもとに、米国のキリスト教会は、日本の状況を理解し、日本での宣教や活動の方法を検討した。

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

The Establishment of Theological Societies

On July 31, 1940, an incident occurred in which four officers of the Salvation Army were arrested and detained on suspicion of espionage. Even prior to this, there had been ongoing attacks on the Salvation Army in newspapers and other media (Kaino, 51). Under the influence of such domestic backlash, the Salvation Army in Japan severed its ties with the International Headquarters in London, reorganized itself as The Salvation Army Japan, and chose to join the United Church of Christ in Japan (Nihon Kirisuto Kyōdan) as Section 11 (Kaino, 52).

However, in June 1942, a wave of repression struck the Holiness churches: 97 Holiness pastors were arrested in a coordinated sweep on charges of violating Article 7 of the Peace Preservation Law. Further repression followed, with a total of 134 arrests targeting Section 6 (formerly the Japan Holy Church), Section 9 (formerly the Kiyome Church), and the Oriental Missionary Society Holiness Church—making it the largest crackdown on Christian churches during the wartime period (Kawaguchi, 70).

Believing that it had become impossible to maintain the sectional system under such repression, the leaders of each section, responding to requests from the Ministry of Education, decided to abolish the system and unify under the single structure of the United Church of Christ in Japan (Kaino, 53).

The repression of the Holiness churches was rooted in their eschatology: after a time of tribulation, Christ would return to earth, establish a millennial kingdom (Kingdom of God) by assuming sovereignty over all nations, and abolish the rule of the emperor. This was seen as a denial of the national polity (kokutai) and thus a violation of the Peace Preservation Law (Kawaguchi, 86).

Moreover, the Kiyome Church taught that the Japanese people had a mission to assist in the construction of this millennial kingdom, understood as a Jewish kingdom, and interpreted the ongoing war as part of God’s providential plan to bring about this kingdom. This was perceived as a distortion and denial of the war’s official objectives and thus intolerable from a public security standpoint. In short, the central reason for the repression was that the denial of imperial rule constituted a rejection of the national polity. The special higher police (Tokkō) also saw beneath this a “literal interpretation” of the Bible, and viewed the eschatological worldview it inspired as inherently subversive to the kokutai. The Kiyome Church’s claims about the “mission of the Japanese people” were likewise condemned as promoting a self-serving interpretation of the war (Kawaguchi, 88).

　1940年7月31日、救世軍幹部４名がスパイ容疑で逮捕拘束されるという事件が起こる。それ以前から、新聞などで、救世軍に対するバッシングが続いていた（Kaino, 51) このような日本国内でのバッシングの影響もあり、日本救世軍はロンドンの万国本部との関係を断ち、日本救世軍として組織を改変し、日本基督教団の第11部として加入する道を選択した（Kainom 52)。　しかし、1942年6月にホーリネス系教会への弾圧がおこり、ホーリネス系教師97名が治安維持法第7条違反の容疑で、一斉検挙された。その後の弾圧もあり、日本基督教団の第6部（旧日本聖教会）、第9部（旧きよめ教会）、東洋宣教会きよめ教会に対する検挙は合計134名となり、戦時下のキリスト教会に対する弾圧として最大規模のものとなった（Kawaguchi, 70) この弾圧により部制の維持が不可能であると考えた、各部の指導者たちは、文部省当局の要請のように部制を廃止し、日本基督教団として一つにまとまる道を選んだ（Kaino, 53)

ホーリネスが弾圧された背景には、彼らの終末観、すなわち患難時代ののちにキリストの地上再臨が あり、キリストが世界各国の統治権を摂取し、統治者となる千年王国（神の国）が建設されること、この千年王国の建設において、天皇統治が廃止されることが国体を否定するものと明確に示される。これが治安維持法における国体の否定と考えられためであった（Kawaguchi, 86)

またきよめ教会は、日本民族には「千年王国即ち猶 太王国建設を援助」する使命があるとし、この戦争を千年王国を建設するための神の経綸となしていることが、「聖戦の目的を歪曲否定」と理解された。その主張のもとに活動することが、治安保持上放任できないとされ るのである。 つまり、天皇統治の廃止という国体の否定が弾圧に至る最も重 要な点であったが、特高はその根底には、聖書の「現実的解釈」があると捉えた。そこから組み立てられた終末観が国体の否定をはらむものとされ、 きよめ教会の「日本民族の使命」も、戦争の独善的な意味づけをするもの として糾弾された(Kawaguchi, 88)。
