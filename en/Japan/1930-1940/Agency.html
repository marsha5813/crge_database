  1. Summarize the key contributions/influences of each leader in the decade (500-1,000 words). ☐

（1）Kagawa, Toyohiko/ 賀川豊彦

賀川豊彦（1888.7.10ー1960．4.23）社会事業家、伝道者。

One of Kagawa Toyohiko’s most representative activities of the 1930s was his leadership in developing the Kingdom of God Movement (kami no kuni undō) on a national scale. In July 1925, during a retreat of the Friends of Jesus Society (Iesu no tomokai), Kagawa proclaimed that Christianity was about building the Kingdom of God, and that Japanese Christians should follow the path of Jesus for the sake of rebuilding the nation (Dohi, 413). Inspired by this call, the Kingdom of God Movement was launched as a one-million soul-saving campaign, resonating with Christian leaders both within Japan and abroad.

In the background of this movement was the 1928 International Missionary Council (IMC) meeting in Jerusalem, where participants stressed the need to proclaim Christ’s message across all aspects of human life in response to rising secularism. Motivated by reports from those who attended the IMC, the National Christian Council of Japan initiated the Kingdom of God Movement, which was implemented nationwide over five years, until 1933. The movement expanded evangelistic efforts into previously unreached areas, including rural villages, fishing communities, and industrial zones (Takahashi, 339). It was underpinned by the belief that addressing “social problems” was the true path to reform. In this way, Kagawa’s activism reflected clear continuity with both Meiji-era socialism and Christian socialism (Yasuda, 96).

Kagawa played a central role in the Kingdom of God Movement, conducting lecture tours across Japan. His gatherings drew large audiences of seekers and new converts, many of whom resolved to be baptized. In April 1939, the movement received further momentum when John R. Mott, president of the IMC, endorsed it at a special council organized by the National Christian Council (Dohi, 413). The remarkable success of the Kingdom of God Movement owed much to Kagawa’s popularity, oratory, the dedication of like-minded speakers, and the systematic cooperation of local churches throughout the country (Dohi, 414). However, while the movement produced large numbers of seekers and new converts, it also faced the problem that many of these new adherents did not remain actively involved in the church.

In addition, Kagawa participated actively in the work of the National Christian Council, promoting the establishment and operation of Farmers’ Gospel Schools, which were linked to the council’s Rural Evangelism efforts. He also contributed to social relief: in 1934, assisting victims of famine in Tōhoku, and in 1938, helping with flood relief in Kobe. His engagement in these social missions was deeply connected to the vision and activism he developed during the Kingdom of God Movement of the 1930s (Dohi, 413).

　1930年代の賀川の代表的な活動の一つが「神の国運動」を全国で展開したことである。

1925年7月にイエスの友会修養会で、賀川はキリスト教は神の国建設であり、日本再建のため、イエスの歩んだ道を進もうと訴えた（Dohi,413)　この呼びかけをもとに神の国運動が、100万人救霊運動として実施され、国内外のキリスト教指導者の共鳴を呼んだ。

その背景には、1928年にエルサレムで開催された国際宣教協議会（IMC)で、世俗主義に対し、人間のあらゆる生活におけるキリストの宣教が訴えられた。この協議会に参加した人々の報告に刺激を受けた日本基督教連盟は、1933年まで5年にわたり全国で実施された神の国運動は、これまで伝道の対象とされてこなかった農村や漁村、工業地帯にも拡大していった。その背景には、「社会問題」の解決こそが、真に目指すべき改革であると考えられた（Takahashi, 339) このような、賀川の行動の背景には明治社会主義、キリスト教社会主義との連続性が見られる（Yasuda, 96）

　神の国運動において中心的な役割を果たした賀川は、全国で講演会を開催した。

全国で開催された賀川の集会には多くの出席者、求道者、受洗決心者が参加した。そして、1939年4月にIMC会長のJohn R. Mottを迎えて開催された連盟主催の特別協議会で神の国運動が提案され、会の賛同を得た（Dohi, 413)　神の国運動は、賀川の知名度、雄弁、彼に共鳴した講師たちの努力、各地の諸教会の組織的協力にって驚くべき成果をあげた（Dohi, 414)しかし、求道者や受洗者が多い一方で、それが定着しないとう問題も発生した。

　また、日本基督教連盟の活動にも積極的に参加し、連盟が実施した農村伝道と連携した農民福音学校の設立と運営を推進した。また1934年には、東北凶作被害者救援に尽力し、1938年には神戸水害被災者救援に協力した。このような活動に携わる契機となったのが、1930年代の神の国運動からであった（Dohi, 413)

（2）Yuasa, Hachiro/湯浅八郎

Hachiro Yuasa was an educator who served as president of Doshisha University and International Christian University and was a leader in Christian schools. Yuasa was born into a Christian family in Akasaka, Tokyo. Later, he entered Doshisha High School in 1902 and was baptized at Doshisha Church (Takeda, "Hachiro Yuasa in the 20th Century (I)," 46). After graduating from Doshisha High School in 1908, he worked as a laborer on a pioneer farm in California for three years while learning English. Then he entered the University of Kansas (then Kansas State Agricultural College), followed by graduate studies in entomology at Illinois State University. After receiving a Ph.D. degree in 1920, Yuasa became an entomology technician at the Illinois State Natural History Bureau (Takeda, "Hachiro Yuasa's Twentieth Century (II)," 3). Then, he returned to Japan and became a professor in the Faculty of Agriculture at Kyoto University. In 1935, when Yuasa was 45 years old, he became president of Doshisha University.

However, when Yuasa became president in the 1930s, Japan was on the verge of war. Doshisha University was a target of intense criticism and slander from right-wing groups in what became known as the "Doshisha Incident" (Tsuji, 2020, 111). In June 1935, shortly after Yuasa assumed the post of president, an incident called the "Kamidana(Shinto altar) Incident(神棚事件)" occurred. This was an incident in which officers assigned to Doshisha protested when the university attempted to remove a Kamidana that a student had taken the liberty of installing in the martial arts hall. A further incident occurred in January 1936 when the university refused to publish a paper on Kokutai Mecho in the Doshisha University Journal. The refusal to publish the article led the author to denounce Doshisha University and some of its faculty members as Marxists, and slander was spread as "a den of leftist professors" (Tsuji, 111-112). Furthermore, there was slander inside and outside the school over Yuasa's misreading of the Imperial Rescript on Education and a movement by officers assigned to the school demanding that Yuasa step down in February 1937. In July 1937, known as the "chapel siege incident," occurred. It was a strike and examination boycott movement. There were seven requests from the students, including one for the abolition of Christian ceremonies (prayer and singing) . After this negotiation, the Pre-College resolved to abolish hymns and prayers at the ceremonies of the four major festivals (shidaisetsu, 四大節).

Under this social situation, the "Doshisha Party platform(同志社綱領)" was criticized by the officers assigned to it and had to be changed. The "Doshisha Party platform" has been the guiding principle of the Doshisha since its founding. However, Article 3, "Christianity is the basis of moral education," was criticized by school officers. Therefore, in order to overcome this crisis, Yuasa believed that it was necessary to reiterate Doshisha's educational policy, and he issued a new academic charter (Tsuji, 124). The new basic policy for education was drafted by Yuasa alone, and the text emphasized the Imperial Rescript on Education and the Emperor's patriotism (Tsuji, 125). Although Yuasa had once respected liberal education, it is believed that he insisted on patriotic expressions and education to keep Doshisha alive as a school.This new educational program was written by newspapers as "abandoning Christian education" and continued to be criticized by the officers assigned to it (Takeda, 11). The school could not maintain the same level of education as the previous school. Schools that no longer had officers would lose various advantages, making it difficult to attract students, and there was a fear that the schools would be closed. Therefore, Yuasa resigned as president on December 31, 1937. In his third year in office, his former liberalism weakened, and he began to speak out about nationalism and militarism (Tsuji, 129). Although Yuasa resigned as president, he can be seen as one of the leading Christian leaders of the era in his efforts to protect Doshisha as a Christian school in these difficult times.

After resigning as president of Doshisha, Yuasa represented Japanese Christians at national and international events, participating in the Madras World Mission Congress in India in 1938 and traveling to the United States to spread the message of peace. In 1939, he was the only Japanese to attend the Christian Peace Conference in Geneva. After the outbreak of war between Japan and the U.S., he stayed in the U.S. He worked with missionaries returning from Japan to end the war, returning to Japan in October 1946 and again becoming president of Doshisha University in April 1947 to help rebuild Doshisha. On the other hand, he was elected president of International Christian University, a Christian general university conceived after the war. He became the first president upon its establishment in 1953 and devoted himself to developing International Christian University from then until 1961.

◎National Christian Church Council(日本基督教連盟)

This organization was a federation of Christian organizations in Japan that interacted and cooperated, conducted business, expressed views on religious, moral, and social issues from a Christian standpoint, and addressed issues related to common Christian interests on behalf of the various organizations, and communicates and interacts with Christian book groups overseas. (Dohi, History of Japanese Protestant Christianity, 241-242)

However, like the Church Alliance, it had no authority to deal with church beliefs or politics, and its resolutions were not mandatory. Nevertheless, as the League associated and acted with international Christian organizations and campaigned by expressing Christian views on various national and global issues, people gradually came to think of the League as the representative organization of Christianity and the League leaders as the governing body of the various organizations. When fascist control came to Christianity in the 1930s, the League was urged to play the role just described: as of 1926, 13 denominations, 8 organizations, and 23 missions had joined the League, and a national Christianization movement was launched in October 1924. This was decided at the second general meeting of the League in October 1924, and 800 lectures and itinerant missions were held in some 200 cities and towns across the country, with approximately 100,000 participants. One interesting aspect of this strengthening campaign was the cooperation of local government agencies. They cooperated with Christian activities by providing venues and attending meetings. It was because in 1924, government officials, troubled by the unsettled situation of the time, invited representatives of Buddhism, Shinto and Christians to discuss the revitalization of the national spirit and the guidance of thought. The cooperation of these local authorities with the Christian community is a concrete manifestation of this. This indicates that the Christian community in Japan was already in step with the government before the 1930s. (Dohi, History of Japanese Protestant Christianity, 242-243)

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

【神の国運動】

　Two of the most prominent Christian movements of the 1930s were the Kingdom of God Movement and Rural Evangelism. The Kingdom of God Movement was a nationwide evangelistic campaign carried out over roughly five years beginning in 1930.

Behind this movement was the nationwide ecumenical campaign initiated by the National Christian Council of Japan, based on reports from the World Missionary Conference held in Jerusalem in 1928. After the visit to Japan of J.R. Mott, the president of the World Missionary Conference, and influenced by Toyohiko Kagawa’s experience in cooperative evangelism, it was decided that the campaign should be more systematically organized. This led to the planning of the Kingdom of God Movement.

At the 7th General Assembly of the National Christian Council of Japan in November 1929, a declaration ceremony for the Kingdom of God Movement was held, and the campaign was launched under the motto: “Pray, Give, Work.” The movement came to be regarded as the largest organized evangelistic effort in Japanese church history, with evangelistic meetings, spiritual training retreats, and other gatherings held across the country, mobilizing roughly one million people and bringing in many new converts.

For the campaign, the nation was divided into 90 regions, which also included Taiwan, Korea, and the Kwantung Leased Territory. Each of these 90 regions established a local Kingdom of God Movement Committee, with six sections: General Affairs, Evangelism, Education, Publicity, Social Affairs, and Rural Affairs. This structure reflects the scale and systematic nature of the movement, which is considered the largest organized evangelistic effort in Japan’s history (Kurokawa, 22).

Within the Kingdom of God Movement, Farmers’ Gospel Schools were especially active. These were informal, small-group classes held during the agricultural off-season to provide Christian humanistic education, practical knowledge about rural society and its economy, and training in new agricultural techniques. Through these schools, new models of agricultural management and Christian-inspired village-building were envisioned (Dohi, 342).

1930年代の代表的なキリスト教の推進運動として、神の国運動と農村伝道が挙げられる。神の国運動とは、1930年から約５年間に渡って全国的に実施された伝道運動である。

この運動の背景には、1928年にエルサレムで開催された世界宣教大会での報告を元に、日本基督教連盟主催の全国基督教協議会によって実施された全国共同運動があった。その後、世界宣教協議会会長のJ.R.モットの来日や賀川豊彦の共同伝道の経験を踏まえ、より組織化して運動を展開するべきだとして「神の国運動」が計画された。

1929年11月の日本基督教連盟第7回総会において、「神の国運動」宣言式が行われ、「祈れよ、捧げよ、働けよ」をモットーに運動が展開された。この運動は、日本の教会史上最大の組織的伝道と言われ、全国各地で伝道集会や修養会、その他の集会が開催され約100万人を動員し、多くの改心者を集めた。この運動では、全国90の地区に分けられ、さらに台湾、朝鮮、関東州も含まれている。この90の地区には、神の国運動地方委員会が設けられ、統括部、伝道部、教育部、宣伝部、社会部、農村部の6部門が設けられた。このことからも、神の国運動が、日本史上最大の組織的伝道であった。（Kurokawa, 22）

　　神の国運動では、農民福音学校が盛んに開かれた。これは農業の閑散期に私塾的な方法でキリスト教による人間教育や農村社会やその経済の知識を教え新しい技術指導を行うということであった。これにより新しい農業経営とキリスト教精神に基づく村作りが考案された（Dohi, 342)

1932年11月の3年で神の国運動は、一度終了した。その完了報告書によると、全国で93の地方委員会が組織され、941の諸教会が協力し、全部で1,953名の講師によって4,102回の集会に約86万人の聴衆が集まり、求道または受洗決心者は4万5千人にのぼったといわれている（Dohi, 342) なかでも賀川は全国を訪問し、彼の集会には1000-2000人が集まり、その10%前後がクリスチャンとなった。（Dohi, 342)

One of the key characteristics of the Kingdom of God Movement was that Protestant evangelism expanded into previously unreached areas, including rural villages, fishing communities, and industrial districts (Takahashi, Nihon Purotesutanto-shi Nyūmon, Nihon Purotesutanto-shi no Sho Mondai, 339). Another distinctive feature was the establishment of schools for training new leaders (Ebisawa, 322–323). In addition, nationwide distribution of evangelistic literature, such as the weekly newspaper Kingdom of God News (Kami no Kuni Shimbun), was also an important factor contributing to the movement’s success.

In November 1932, the movement’s activities were extended for another two years. However, after the Manchurian Incident, the Kingdom of God Movement began cooperating with Japan’s wartime mobilization efforts, aligning with nationalistic programs such as the National Spiritual Mobilization (Kokumin Seishin Sakujo) and National Reconstruction Movement (Kokumin Kōsei Undō) (Kurokawa, 25). In April 1933, the National Spiritual Mobilization Movement and the National Reconstruction Movement were launched, with the first training ceremony for the Reconstruction Movement held on April 17. By July, the Kingdom of God Movement was actively cooperating with these government-sponsored programs. However, under the growing pressures of Japan’s emerging wartime regime, intensified by events such as the Manchurian Incident and the League of Blood Incident (Ketsumeidan Jiken), the movement ultimately came to an end.

　神の国運動の特徴としては、これまでプロテスタント伝道がこれまで未開拓であった農村や漁村、工業地帯に拡大した点が挙げられる。(Takahashi, 日本プロテスタント史入門、『日本プロテスタント史の諸問題』339頁）他にも指導者養成のための学校を開設したことなども特徴的であった(Ebisawa, 322-323)。さらに週刊『神の国新聞』などの文書伝道が全国的に展開された点も効果をあげる一つの要因となった。

　1932年11月に、もう2年の延長が決められた。しかし、満州事件後、神の国運動は日本政府の戦時政策の一部である国民精神作與・国民更生運動と協力するようになった（Kurokawa, 25）1933年4月に精神作與運動と国民更生運動が開始され、4月17日には国民更生運動の修養式が開催された。7月には、神の国運動と協力した国民更生運動の実施された。しかし、満州事変や血盟団事件など非常事態体制の圧力の元、活動は終了した。

Key Theological Issues

From the 1930s onward, the influence of the military increased. The activities of the Christian churches became more restricted than before, and from the early 1930s, dialectical theology, especially Barthian theology, was introduced to Japan and significantly impacted the Christian churches. This was an important event, as was New Theology, which had a major impact in the 1880s (Dohi, 1980, 384).

  • Barthian theology/ Dialectical theology

In Japan, the works of Karl Barth and Emil Brunner began to be read around 1925.Initially, they were introduced in magazines and newspapers, but they became a kind of fad, and translations began to be published by research institutes around 1930 (Sato, 79).

Following World War I, a theological movement emerged in Switzerland and Germany that criticized 19th-century liberal theology. It emphasized the limits of human knowledge and existence and emphasized divine revelation. Dialectical theology asserts that grace is revealed only through crisis and negation. When dialectical theology was first introduced, many of Brunner's works were translated and introduced.

Yoshitaka Kumano, Hideharu Kuwata, Enkichi Suga, and Tokutaro Takakura were among the first to read these works of dialectical theology (Sato, 79).

However, in 1932, Barth's “Church Dogmatics” was published and introduced to Japan. As the characteristics of Barthian theology became clearer, Japanese theologians began to lean more toward Barth than Brunner (Sato, 79). In particular, Japanese theologians sided with Barth during the 1934 natural theology debate between Barth and Brunner.

The acceptance of Barthian theology in Japan was centered around Keiji Ashida at Doshisha University in Kyoto, who gathered people to translate Barth's “Epistle to the Romans” and graduate students at Kyoto University. Meanwhile, in Tokyo, a group centered around Tokutaro Takakura, who founded the Shinanomachi Church, became the core (Sato, 80).

At that time, Japanese Christian intellectuals learned the depth of Christian thought from dialectical theology, which had a significant impact (Sato, 82). Since the Meiji period, they had learned Christianity from American missionaries and found it appealing, but they did not have a strong awareness that Christianity was a profound religion (Sato, 82). However, dialectical theology, which directly criticized German idealism, was introduced, and Japanese Christians at the time found it very appealing (Sato, 82). Barth's theology was so appealing because of its radical nature (Radikalität). In a society where Christian norms were common sense, Barth's theology seemed too radical. However, in Japan, where Christianity had not taken root in society, it was accepted as an attractive ideology (Sato, 83). During this period, Japanese Christian churches and theological seminaries strongly emphasized the character of “Japanese Christianity” in response to the national policy of the time. At that time, Barthian theology was the theological mainstream and was not unrelated to “Japanese Christianity.”

Additionally, the churches and denominations consolidated under the Japan Christian Council after 1940 did not resist the war effort but adopted a cooperative stance. This revealed tendencies to distort Barthian theology to conform to the era's demands or to use Barthian theology as a basis for evading the issues of the time (Terasono, 162).

  • Japanese Christianity

Furthermore, in the 1930s, attempts emerged to understand Christian doctrine in relation to the Japanese tradition (Dohi, Dictionary, 1063). There was a growing interest among Christians in such themes as "Japanese Christianity" and the "Japanization of Christianity" (Suzuki, History of Japanese Christianity, 281). For example, Tsunekichi Watase(渡瀬恒吉), a pastor of the Congregational church and a promoter of this denomination to Korean mission, published "Proposal of Japanese Theology(『日本進学の提唱』)" (Hozanasha) in 1934. In this book, Watase viewed God the Father and Ame-no-Minakanushi(天之御中主神), the Holy Spirit and the Musubu-no-Kami(産霊神)t, and Christ and Amaterasu Ōmikam(天照大神) as similar entities in Christianity, describing the God and Amaterasu Ōmikam in particular as almost identical, just with different names (Suzuki, 282).

What was the significance of identifying the historical gods of Japan's founding with the Christian God? These "Japanese Christianity" and "Christian Japanization" theorists also had something in common in their view of war. They recognized that the war against China was not based on territorial ambition but rather a war of "self-defense" and actively supported it (Suzuki, 283). This Japanese Christianity became the dominant ideology of Japanese Christianity as the war expanded. It began with full support for the "loyalty and patriotism(忠君愛国)" spirit of the Imperial Rescript(教育勅語) on Education, and the "Japanization" of Christianity proceeded (Suzuki, 283).

One significant example was Genkichi Imaizumi's "Mikuni Movement(みくに運動). Mikuni is written as "皇国" and means "imperial nation". Imaizumi, a pastor of Naka-Shibuya Church, published the magazine "Mikuni(『みくに』)" in 1935, advocating "imperialized Christianity. In this magazine, he argued that (1) the Imperial Way(皇道) is the Kingdom of God(神の国), and he hoped to complete the "Great Mikuni(大みくに）" by proclaiming the Imperial Way both inside and abroad in Japan, (2) he hoped to convey an imperialized Christianity to the world, and (3) in this time of emergency, people should live with the spirit of Mikuni, regardless of class or religion (Suzuki, 283). In other words, this was an ideology that equated the "Mikuni(皇国)," which is Japan ruled by the "unbroken imperial line（万世一系)" emperor of all generations, with the "kingdom of God" as referred to in Christianity. The " Mikuni " magazine featured such works as "Shinko Shoshoki" rather than the Bible and seemed more of a Shintoist than a Christian magazine. (Suzki, 284) They took a cooperative stance toward the war effort during World War II.

  • Catholic

In October 1936, the Catholic Church revised the Catechism("Kōkyo Kyōri," 公教要理) and the Prayer Book. In the 1860s, there were two editions of the Catechisms, one in Nagasaki and the other in Yokohama. The difference was that the one in Yokohama used many Chinese words, while the one in Nagasaki used many Latin words. Later, in 1896, Japan's first unified Catechism was published and was used until 1925. The 1936 revision was the first since 1925 and was the first "Catechism" of the Japanese bishops.

  • Publication

No new translations of the Bible or other works were seen between 1930 and 1940. However, the hymn book was revised and published as a revised edition of "Hymnbook" on December 10, 1931. This was led by Bessho Umenosuke(別所梅之助), a faculty member of Aoyama Gakuin, and others who had previously translated the Bible and selected and edited hymn book. The revised edition of "Hymnbook" was used for about 20 years until it was revised in 1954.

The influence of Barth's theology on Japan can also be seen in the publication of Hidenobu Kuwata's Dialectical Theology in March 1933 and Karl Barth's The Epistle to the Romans in November 1933, translated by Hitoo Marukawa.

From the mid-1930s onward, publications on Christianity more tied to the Japanese state were published, and texts critical of Japan were removed or banned.

The Doshisha Kamidana Incident

An incident that symbolized military interference in Christian schools during Japan’s Fifteen-Year War.

In June 1935, at Doshisha Higher Commercial School, then located in Iwakura, a portrait of the school’s founder Niijima Jō was displayed in the martial arts hall. A group of students took the portrait down and instead nailed up a shinsatsu (sacred talisman) of a martial deity from Miyake Hachiman Shrine in its place. The school authorities reprimanded the students, who admitted fault and restored Niijima’s portrait to its original position.

However, the military officer assigned to the school at the time strongly intervened, asserting that the refusal of the Doshisha authorities to install a Shinto altar (kamidana) in the martial arts hall was contrary to the fundamental Japanese spirit, which was based on reverence for the gods and veneration of ancestors. The officer further threatened that unless the school conducted a proper self-examination and complied, the military would withdraw its assigned officer after discussion with superiors.

At the time, the withdrawal of military officers meant that students would lose their draft deferment privileges and eligibility for officer candidacy—a serious consequence. The Doshisha Board of Trustees and President Yuasa Hachirō struggled to find a response, repeatedly appealing to military authorities for understanding of the school’s founding Christian principles, but to no avail.

Ultimately, under intense pressure and public attention from the press, the Board of Trustees, in its meeting on June 23, decided to comply with the military’s demand and approved the installation of a Shinto altar (kamidana) in the martial arts hall.

Thereafter, Doshisha allowed increasing encroachment by the military and right-wing forces, and President Yuasa was eventually forced to step down.

(Takamichi Kō, “The Doshisha Kamidana Incident,” Encyclopedia of Japanese Christian History, p. 931)

同志社神棚事件

15年戦争下における軍部のキリスト教学校への介入を象徴する事件。1935年6月、当時、岩倉に置かれていた同志社高商では武道場に校祖新島襄の肖像が掲げられていたが、学生の一部がこれを引き下ろし、代わりに三宅八幡に祀られていた武神の神符を釘で撃ち止めた。学校当局はこの学生の行為を叱責し、学生も非を認めて新島の肖像をもとに戻した。しかし、当時の配属将校は、武道場に神棚を掲げさせぬ同志社当局は、敬神祟祖を根本義とする日本の根本精神に反するとして強固に介入。猛省なくば協議の上配属将校の引き上げもやむなしと迫った。当時、配属将校の引揚げは学生の徴兵猶予の特典の剥奪と、幹部候補資格の喪失を意味下のものであって、同志社理事会および総長湯浅八郎箱の対策に苦慮、再三師団当局に建学の精神への理解を求めたが得られず、ついに6月23日の理事会はジャーナリズムの注目の下、軍部の意向を受け入れ、「神棚」を武道場に掲げることを決定した。その後、同志社は軍部右翼の蹂躙を許し、湯浅は退陣に巻き込まれた（高道基「同志社神棚事件」『日本キリスト教歴史大事典』、931）

Assigned Military Officers (配属将校)

Under the “Regulations for the Assignment of Active-Duty Army Officers to Schools” issued in April 1925, active-duty army officers were assigned to secondary and higher schools to provide military training to male students. While the assignment of officers to private schools and university faculties was to be based on application, schools actively applied in order to secure conscription exemptions and other privileges.

The practice of assigning military officers gradually evolved beyond military training to promote the cultivation of loyalty to the kokutai (national polity), the militarization of students as reserve forces, and opened the way for military interference in the education system.

In September 1932, during the Sophia University Incident, the assigned officer Kitahara Kazumi led students to visit Yasukuni Shrine; when some students refused to participate, he questioned President H. Hoffmann, who replied that as the shrine was of another religion, they could not attend. Kitahara reported this to the Army Ministry, which in turn notified the Ministry of Education that it would withdraw assigned officers from Sophia University. Since this would result in the loss of various privileges for the university, the Catholic Church ultimately permitted student participation in shrine visits.

Cases where assigned officers actively worked to suppress Christian-based education include the Doshisha Kamidana Incident (June 1935) and the movement to oust Doshisha President Yuasa Hachirō (1937).

(Doi Akio, “Assigned Military Officers,” p. 1097)

ー配属将校

1925年4月、「陸軍現役将校学校配属令」によって、中等以上の学校の男子生徒・学生に軍事教練を施すために配属された現役将校。私学・大学学部は申請によって配属することになっていたが、徴兵令上の特典そのほかのために、こそって申請した。将校配属は次第に国体観念の養成、生徒・学生の予備軍化に繋がっていったのみならず、軍部が学校教育に介入する道を切り開いていくことになった。1932年9月、上智大学事件においての配属将校北原一視は学生に靖国神社を参拝させたが、参加しないものがいたため、学長　ホフマン．Hに問いただしたところ、ホフマンは神社は他宗教であるから、その参拝はできないと答えた。木原はこのことを陸軍省に伝え、陸軍省は上智大学から配属将校を引き上げることを文部省に通告。これにより大学がさまざまな特典を失うことは明らかであったため、結局カトリック教会は上智大学の神社参拝を認めた。配属将校がキリスト教主義教育を排除する役目を果たした例は同志社神棚事件（1935年6月）、同志社総長湯浅八郎排斥運動（1937）にもみられる。（土肥昭夫「配属将校」, 1097)

The Sophia University Incident

An incident in which students at Sophia University refused to participate in a visit to Yasukuni Shrine.

On May 5, 1932, the university’s assigned military officer, Colonel Kitahara Kazumi, led about 60 second-year students to Yasukuni Shrine, where 2 or 3 students refused to participate in the shrine visit. The Army Ministry reported to the Ministry of Education that Sophia University was a school harmful to the state.

In response, the Apostolic Delegate of the Holy See in Japan and J.A. Chabon, representing the Tokyo Archdiocese, informed the Ministry of Education that “the shrine visit was not religious in nature but intended to foster patriotic spirit.”

Nevertheless, in December, the Army Ministry carried out the withdrawal of assigned military officers from both Sophia University and Gyosei Middle School. Major newspapers such as the Asahi, Yomiuri, and Nichinichi covered the story prominently.

This meant that the universities would lose privileges such as having their graduates qualify for officer candidacy. President H. Hoffmann, Dean Eita Takei of the preparatory division, and Secretary Yachita Dobashi worked strenuously to resolve the situation.

Finally, on December 21, 1933, Colonel Haruo Koide was appointed as the new assigned officer, bringing an end to the incident after more than a year of intense public attention as a social controversy.

(Satoru Obara, “The Sophia University Incident,” p. 674)

上智大学事件
上智大学学生が靖国神社参拝を拒否した事件。1932年5月5日、上智大学配属将校機はたら一視陸軍大佐が良か2年生60名を引率して靖国神社に赴いた所２，３名の学生が参拝しなかったことをたんに発する。陸軍省は、国家にとって有害な大学と文部省に報告したという。駐日ローマ恐慌使節とシャボンJ.A.東京大市況は文部省に対して「神社参拝は宗教的なものではなく、愛国心の寛容を目的とする」との回答があった。しかし、陸軍省は１２月、上智大学および暁星中学校から配属将校の引揚げを断行し、朝日、読売、日日等の新聞もこれを大きく取り上げた。これは卒業生が幹部候補生となる特典を失うことを意味した・ホフマンH学長、栄田猛猪予科長、土橋八千太幹事らが事態解決のために尽力した結果、１９３３年12月２１日配属将校として小出治雄大差が任命され、１年あまりの間、社会問題として世間の注目を浴びたこの事件はなんとか解決された。（尾原悟「上智大学事件」674）
