Japan: Context (1930-1940)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

A.

One of the most representative regulations concerning religion during this period was the enactment of the Religious Organizations Law (Shūkyō Dantai Hō). This law was promulgated on April 8, 1939, and came into effect on April 1, 1940. Enacted after the National Spiritual Mobilization Law, its purpose was to bring religious organizations under state control and integrate them into the total war mobilization system.

この時代の宗教に関する規制として代表的なものに宗教団体法の成立が挙げられる。この法律は、1939年4月8日に公布、1940年4月1日に施行された。この法律は、国民精神総動員法が発せられたのちに成立し、宗教団体の国家による統制と総動員体制のなかに組み込むことを目的とした。

Attempts to enact a basic law concerning religion had been made repeatedly since the Meiji era (in 1899, 1927, 1929, and 1935), but all failed to pass (Uemura, Kindle 44). In the early Shōwa period, religious affairs were handled by the Ministry of Education’s Bureau of Religion, while shrine affairs were managed separately by the Ministry of Home Affairs’ Bureau of Shrines. Since the Meiji era, it had been the government’s policy to treat shrines managed by the state not as religion but as a form of state administration distinct from religious governance (Uemura, Kindle 44). However, from the 1930s onward—amid events like the Manchurian Incident and the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War—Japanese society increasingly moved toward nationalism and a wartime regime (Hara, 19). As a result, the Religious Organizations Law, which had previously failed repeatedly, was enacted with little resistance.

宗教に関する基本法の制定は、明治以来、何度も(1899, 1927,1929,1935) 計画されては不成立に終わってた.（Uemura, Kindle 44)昭和初期には宗教行政は文部省宗教局、神社行政は内務省神社局と別々に行われていた。国家が管理する神社は宗教ではなく、神社行政は宗教行政ではないというのが明治以来の政府の方針であった（Uemura, Kindle 44) このような歴史がありがなら、1930年以降、満州事変や日中戦争の勃発など、日本は国家主義、戦時体制制へと社会が進行して行った（Hara, 19)。そのため、これまで何度も審議未了で成立に至らなかった宗教に関する法案である宗教団体法は簡単に成立した。

The Religious Organizations Law was a concise piece of legislation consisting of 37 articles, but its enforcement regulations contained 75 articles, and the detailed local implementation rules established by prefectures added another 22 articles. These included extensive paperwork, such as application and reporting forms submitted to the prefectural governors (Kaino, 28).

　宗教団体法は全体で37条からなる簡潔な法律であるが、「宗教団体法施行規則」は75条、さらに都道府県によって決められた「同施行細則」は22条となり、都道府県知事への申請書や報告書の膨大な書式が添付されているものであった。（戒能、かいのう28)

Under the law, the establishment of a religious organization required the permission of the Minister of Education (Article 3). Moreover, religious activities were to be monitored to ensure they did not disturb public peace or violate the duties of subjects of the Emperor (Hara, 20). Consequently, if a religious group’s activities were judged to contradict the moral or political order of the imperial system, they could be strictly suppressed.

この法律により、教団の設立右派文部大臣の許可が必要となり（第３条）、その宗教行為も安寧秩序を妨げないこと、臣民としての義務に背くものは取り締まるとの説明がなされてたい（Hara, 20)。そのため、宗教団体の活動が天皇制の秩序や道徳に反すると判断された場合には、厳しい取り締まりが可能となった。

The law clearly distinguished between religious organizations and religious associations. Religious organizations recognized under the law were granted legal corporate status and certain privileges, while groups not recognized as such were treated as quasi-religious bodies without privileges and remained under government supervision. Recognized religious organizations received tax exemptions (e.g., from income tax) and protections such as immunity from asset seizure. Although the qualifications and personnel decisions of religious teachers were left to the autonomy of each organization, the law also imposed obligations for reporting and required government approval for various matters, giving the state significant means of intervention (Hara, 23).

　この法律は、宗教団体と宗教結社を明確に区別し、宗教団体には法人としての一定の特権が与えられた。宗教団体とみなされない宗教結社は、類似宗教団体とされ、特権はなくただ統制の対象となった。地方長官の監察下におかれた。法人として認可された宗教団体には、所得税を課さないなどの優遇措置や、さらに不動産などへの差し押さえが禁止された。宗教教師の資格や人事は各宗教団体の自治に委ねられているが、政府への報告の義務や認可申請など、政府が介入する余地が多々見られる（Hara, 23)。

In June 1940, following the enactment of the law, the Ministry of Education’s Bureau of Religion presented the conditions and policies for approval of religious organizations at a meeting with religious leaders. The requirements included: (1) at least 50 churches and 5,000 members; (2) financial independence from foreign missions; (3) establishment of political autonomy; (4) severing ties with foreign missionaries; (5) removal of resurrection doctrines; (6) revision of doctrines to align with imperial ideology (Kōdō shisō); and (7) transferring property titles from foreign to Japanese missionaries. This made it implicitly understood that ties with foreigners, especially missionaries, should be severed and that Japanese nationals should lead religious organizations (Miyoshi, Christianity Under Wartime, 63).

宗教法案施行後の1940年6月、文部省宗教局は宗教界代表者の会議で教団認可の方針と条件を発表した。

(1)教会数50以上、信徒数5000人以上、（2) 財政的に外国ミッションからの独立

(3) 政治的に自治権の確立、(4)外国人宣教師との絶縁、(5)復活教義の除去 (6)皇道思想と一体になる教義の改訂、(7)外国人宣教師名義を日本人宣教師名義にすること

　などが挙げられる。このことは外国人宣教師を含む外国との繋がりの断絶や日本人が組織のトップとなることが暗黙の了解とされていた（Miyoshi, 戦時下のキリスト教、63) 。

As a result of applications submitted to the Ministry of Education, the 13 existing sects of Shinto remained unchanged, and the 13 denominations and 56 sects of Buddhism were consolidated into 28. The most mergers occurred among the Rinzai sect, followed by the Shingon sect (57). In Christianity, the Catholic Church reorganized as the Japanese Catholic Church in 1941, and the Protestants were merged into the newly formed United Church of Christ in Japan (Nihon Kirisuto Kyōdan). Although the Federation of Christian Churches in Japan (Nihon Kirisuto Kyō Rengō) led preparations for the Protestant merger, doctrinal and ritual differences among denominations complicated the process. Still, approximately 30 Protestant groups were united (Suzuki, 303). Records indicate that some welcomed this legislation as a sign that the government had “officially recognized Christianity” (59–60). Additionally, the Director of the Bureau of Religion, Nagazo Matsuo, cited six positive effects of the law (Uemura, Kindle, 54):

　文部省への認可申請の結果は、既存の神道教派13派は変更なく、既存の仏教宗派は13宗56派から28宗派となった。合同が多かったのは臨済宗系であり、次に真言宗であった（57)。キリスト教系の団体は、1941年にカトリックが日本天主公教教団、プロテスタントが日本基督教団を新設した。プロテスタントの合同に関しては、日本基督教連盟が準備をすすめ各教派によって信条や儀礼に違いがあり合同は難航したが、約30のプロテスタントの団体が合同を果たした（Suzuki,303)　キリスト教界においては、この法案により「政府がキリスト教を公認した」と認識し、歓迎する雰囲気があったという記録もある（59-60)　さらに文部省宗教局長の松尾長造は法律実施後の影響として以下の６点を挙げていた（Uemura, Kindle, 54)。

  1. Improved efficiency in religious administration

  2. Increased organizational capacity of religious groups

  3. Enhanced institutional capability through legal incorporation

  4. Revitalized activity due to clearer regulatory guidelines

  5. Healthy development of religious associations through supervision

  6. Potential for cultural and religious outreach on the Chinese mainland

  7. 宗教行政の能率増進、

  8. 宗教団体の活動能力増進

  9. 法人化による能力向上

  10. 監督規定の明示による活動の敏活化

  11. 宗教結社の監督による健全育成

  12. 中国大陸方面への宗教的な文化工作の可能性

Thus, the Religious Organizations Law simplified the previously complex management of religious groups and made it easier to mobilize them for the war effort. Interestingly, among Christian communities, there was more support than resistance to this state control, indicating the future direction of their relationship with the government and war. However, not all Christian groups joined the government-mandated consolidation. For example, some members of the Anglican Church of Japan did not join the United Church of Christ in Japan and were instead treated as religious associations (Hara, 23).

このことからも、宗教団体法はそれまで複雑に存在した宗教団体の管理と統制を簡潔にし、戦争へと動員しやすいようにした法律であった.またキリスト教の側からは、政府による統制への反発というよりは、同法案を歓迎する動きが見られる点からも、その後のキリスト教側と政府、戦争への向き合い方が伺われる法律の制定であった。しかし、全てのキリスト教の団体が宗教法案による合同に参加したわけではなく、日本聖公会の一部の人々は日本基督教団に参加せず、宗教結社として取り扱われた（Hara, 23)

B. 法律の名称

  1. Religious Organization Law/ 宗教団体法(1939)

Religious Organizations Law (excerpt)

(Law No. 77, April 8, 1939 [Showa 14])

Article 1:

In this law, the term “religious organization” refers to Shinto sects, Buddhist sects, Christian denominations, and other religious groups (hereinafter collectively referred to as “sect,” “denomination,” or “organization”), as well as temples and churches.

Article 2:

Sects, denominations, organizations, and churches may obtain corporate status.

Temples shall automatically obtain corporate status.

Article 3:

When establishing a sect, denomination, or organization, the founders must prepare regulations, constitutions, or organizational rules for the group. If the group is to obtain corporate status, this intention must be clearly stated, and approval must be obtained from the competent minister.

宗教団体法（抄）（昭和十四年四月八日法律第七十七号）

第一条　本法ニ於テ宗教団体トハ神道教派、仏教宗派及基督教其ノ他ノ宗教ノ教団（以下単ニ教派、宗派、教団ト称ス）並ニ寺院及教会ヲ謂フ

第二条　教派、宗派及教団並ニ教会ハ之ヲ法人ト為スコトヲ得

寺院ハ之ヲ法人トス

第三条　教派、宗派又ハ教団ヲ設立セントスルトキハ設立者ニ於テ教規、宗制又ハ教団規則ヲ具シ法人タラントスルモノニ在リテハ其ノ旨ヲ明ニシ主務大臣ノ認可ヲ受クルコトヲ要ス

The regulations, constitutions, or organizational rules of the religious group must include the following items:

1. Name

2. Location of the administrative office

3. Outline of doctrines

4. Matters concerning the dissemination of doctrines and the performance of rituals

5. Organization, appointment and dismissal, and duties and powers of the chief administrator, head of the organization, and other governing bodies

6. Matters concerning temples, churches, and other affiliated bodies

7. Matters concerning the qualifications, titles, appointment, dismissal, and other personnel matters of abbots, church leaders, their deputies, teachers, and monks

8. Matters concerning lay followers, believers, or adherents

9. Matters concerning property management and other financial affairs

10. Matters concerning public benefit projects

When a sect, denomination, or organization wishes to amend its regulations, constitution, or organizational rules, or when a sect, denomination, or organization without corporate status wishes to obtain such status, it must obtain approval from the competent minister.

Article 4:

Each sect or denomination shall appoint a chief administrator (kanchō);

Each religious organization shall appoint a head of the organization (kyōdan tōrisha).

教規、宗制及教団規則ニハ左ノ事項ヲ記載スベシ

　一　名称
二　事務所ノ所在地
三　教義ノ大要
四　教義ノ宣布及儀式ノ執行ニ関スル事項
五　管長、教団統理者其ノ他ノ機関ノ組織、任免及職務権限ニ関スル事項
六　寺院、教会其ノ他ノ所属団体ニ関スル事項
七　住職、教会主管者、其ノ代務者及教師ノ資格、名称及任免其ノ他ノ進退並ニ僧侶ニ関スル事項
八　檀徒、教徒又ハ信徒ニ関スル事項
九　財産管理其ノ他ノ財務ニ関スル事項
十　公益事業ニ関スル事項

教規、宗制若ハ教団規則ヲ変更セントスルトキ又ハ法人ニ非ザル教派、宗派若ハ教団ガ法人タラントスルトキハ主務大臣ノ認可ヲ受クルコトヲ要ス

第四条　教派及宗派ニハ管長ヲ、教団ニハ教団統理者ヲ置クベシ

Article 5:

Sects, denominations, or organizations may merge or dissolve with the approval of the competent minister.

A sect, denomination, or organization shall be dissolved if its establishment approval is revoked.

Article 6:

When establishing a temple or church, the founders must prepare temple regulations or church regulations and, except for the churches specified in Item 5, Paragraph 2 of Article 2, obtain prior approval from the chief administrator or head of the organization. If the church is to obtain corporate status, this intention must be clearly stated and approval must be obtained from the local governor.

The temple regulations and church regulations must include the following items:

1. Name

2. Location

3. Name of the principal image, enshrined deity, or title of the enshrined Buddha

4. Name of the affiliated sect, denomination, or organization

5. In the case of a church not belonging to a sect, denomination, or organization, in place of the above, the name of the religion, an outline of its doctrines, and matters concerning the qualifications, titles, appointment, dismissal, and other personnel matters of teachers

6. Matters concerning the dissemination of doctrines and performance of rituals

7. Matters concerning abbots, church leaders, and other governing bodies

8. Matters concerning lay followers, believers, or adherents, and their representatives

9. Matters concerning branch temples and associated institutions

10. Matters concerning property management and other financial affairs

11. Matters concerning public benefit projects

When amending temple or church regulations, or when a non-corporate church wishes to obtain corporate status, the consent of the representatives of lay followers, believers, or adherents must be obtained.

以下三項略

Social Hostility toward Christianity

Since the Manchurian Incident, religious groups, including Christianity, became targets of exclusion in an increasingly militarized Japan.

  1. 1932 SOPHIA UNIVERSITY- YASUKUNI SHRINE INCIDENT

In 1932, several Catholic students at Sophia University refused to visit Yasukuni Shrine, which honors the war dead. From 1925 onward, schools above the junior high level were assigned active duty officers to be in charge of "military training" of students. This worship(visitation) was planned for "military training (school education). For Private schools, accepting officers is voluntary, but several benefits could include a reduced period of military service and eligibility to take the cadet exam (Suzuki, 257). Initially, schools were also considered "better off without going" due to student complaints, and visits to shrines were viewed as a "religious ritual." Sophia University asked the Ministry of Education about shrine visits and "salutations," which it characterized as an expression of patriotism and loyalty, without mentioning their religious nature, and positioned them as patriotism education. (Suzki, 259) This incident was a significant turning point in Japanese Catholic thinking about shrines and "shrine worship. It shows military pressure after the Manchurian Incident.

  1. 1933 AMAMI OSHIMA CATHOLIC INCIDENT

An incident occurred in which authorities closed a Catholic girls' school and destroyed the church. The Catholics spread their mission on Amami Oshima and opened Oshima Girls High School in 1924. When the army headquarters established itself in Oshima in April 1923, the Catholic teaching power gradually weakened. The school required all students to visit the shrine, leading to the expulsion of two Christian students who refused to comply in October 1923. After the Manchurian Incident, Oshima became a military fortress, and the military began to oppose Catholicism. In 1933, the villager's meeting resolved to eliminate Catholicism and close Oshima Girls High School because of education contrary to Japan's "kokutai" (national constitution). The school was closed in 1934. Furthermore, the Christians were gathered by the wardens and told, "The mere presence of believers in this area is disrupting the traditional peace in the village. Today, in this time of emergency, you must swear to apostasy in front of the military police, or we will kill you." All the believers swore to apostasy. （Suzuki, 276). A series of incidents of destruction of churches and burning of altars followed this. The Christians lived like "hidden Christians".

  1. 1933 MINO MISSION INCIDENT (美濃ミッション)

The Mino Mission is a group of Christian churches in Gifu Prefecture established in 1918 by the American missionary Sadie Lea Weidner (1875–1939). In 1929, four elementary school students did not participate in a shrine. To research these students, their parents were members of the Mino Mission congregation. After that, students continued avoiding school on days when worship services were held. In 1933, another child from the Mino Mission refused to go on a school trip to Ise Shrine. It turned out that both mother and child were Christians and were opposed to shrine visits (Suzuki, 268). The police summoned the mother, and the local newspaper wrote about her as "unpatriotic". Several similar problems: the refusal of a kindergarten run by the Mino Mission to enroll schoolchildren,

The PTA condemned the church with a song, "Defend the Kokutai! Bury the Evil Religion!" The parents who had refused to send their children to Ise were severely pressured (Hardacre,421). The police questioned all residents of the men's dorm and women's home at the Mino Mission about their thoughts on the Ise Shrine or shrines as if they were criminals.

The Mino Mission incidents are clear cases of the operation of State Shinto in violation of religious freedom. Dissenters faced pressure, with police, moral suasion groups, religionists, and local society labeling them traitors for not worshiping at shrines.

  1. 同志社神棚事件

After 1935, Doshisha University experienced a series of incidents known as the "Doshisha Incidents," resulting in criticisms of the university and its president. For example, a "household Shinto altar (kamidana) incident(神棚事件)" occurred when a kamidana was installed in a newly constructed martial arts hall in June 1936. The previous martial arts hall had been decorated with the founder, Niijima Jo, but the students had installed a new kamidana. When the university tried to remove it, the officer assigned to Doshisha criticized them, and a controversy erupted (Doshisha Shashi Shiryo Henshūjo, 1979, 1060-1961). In response to this criticism, the board of trustees approved the installation of a Kamidana, one incident that illustrates the times and the situation in which Doshisha found itself. In addition, the Kokutai Mecho Essay was refused to be published in January 1936. Kokutai Meicho means to prove that the nation is centered on the emperor, and the university refused to publish this article because it was considered to be a paper that argued for the legitimacy of Kokutai Meicho (Doshisha Shashi Shiryo Henshūjo, 1979, 1104). This led the authors to criticize the university and some faculty members as Marxists, and slander was spread as if Doshisha University was a "den of the left" (Doshisha Shashi Shiryo Henshūjo, 1979, 1110-1111). In 1937, there was a strike between Doshisha University and the pre-college students demanding the resignation of the president, the abolition of Christian rites, and the punishment of the pre-college professors who had wrong ideas about the nation. This was influenced by a new officer working in Doshisha (Doshisha Shashi Shiryo Henshūjo, 1979, 1121-1122).As a result, a chapel siege by pre-college students occurred on July 5, 1937. This incident involved students gathering in the chapel to boycott the final examinations. Of the total of approximately 700 pre-college students, slightly less than half entered the chapel and boycotted the examinations (Doshisha Shashi Shiryo Henshūjo, 1979, 1122-1123). Some of the student's motives for holding up in the chapel included curiosity and sympathy for the National Defense Research Association, but some were also found to be fear of being beaten (DDoshisha Shashi Shiryo Henshūjo, 1979, 1123) There were seven requests from the students, including one for the abolition of Christian ceremonies (prayer and singing). After this negotiation, the Pre-College resolved to abolish hymns and prayers at the ceremonies of the four major festivals (shidaisetsu, 四大節). (New Year's Prayer Service(新年拝賀式, Kigensetsu紀元節, Tenchosetsu天長節, and Meijisetsu 明治節) (Doshisha Shiryosho Shiryo Shiryosho, 1979, 1126) Thus, the government and some students were rejecting Christianity, and Doshisha University, or the Christian school at that time, was in a difficult situation.

  1. 1939 WATCH TOWER INCIDENT 灯台社

In 1926, Junzo Akashi(明石順三), working at the WatchTower headquarters in the U.S., returned to Japan. After February 1933, authorities banned a series of "Golden Age(『黄金時代』）" magazines published by WatchTower because of "articles of disrespect." The Chiba Prefectural Special High Court rounded up all the WatchTower magazines in May. Numerous articles by the company were found to be "repudiations of the national political system" and "repudiations of the national social system" and were banned (Suzuki, 279). The main reason for this was that these books stated that the emperor system, the state system, and all current organizational systems were the product of the devil and should be "destroyed." (Suzuki, 279) In June 1939, police investigated Akashi and others involved in the Watch Tower. They subjected those arrested to harsh interrogations, extracting statements without their permission. They charged individuals with "national reform," "anti-war," and "disrespect" under the Public Security Law. The Lighthouse Company received an order to dissolve in August 1940 (Suzuki, 280). The element of repression strongly marked the arrest of the Watch Tower as an example to emphasize the inviolability of the "Kokutai"（Suzuki, 281).

6. Censership

During wartime, they intensified the censorship on speech and writing based on ideology and religion.Under such circumstances, any writing with even the slightest anti-war or "pacifist" bent was subject to crackdown. For example, Yanaihara Tadao(矢内原忠雄), who was a professor at Tokyo Imperial University and also a member of the Non-church movement, published an article about “Ideal of the state”（「国家の理想」）in September 1937. In this paper, he criticized Japan‘s policy toward the Rohtang Bridge Incident. The article was removed in its entirety by censors(Akae, 138). Furthermore, Yanaihara released “People and Peace” (『民族と平和』) in December 1, 1937. This book was also banned. In addition, Masaike Jin(政池仁) who was a member of the Non-church Movement, also published “ Christian pacifism”(『基督平和論』) in December 4th, 1937. In his book, he argued that not only do they repeatedly state their anti-war and anti-military ideology, but they also describe Japan as an aggressor country and Japan as a country that has committed atrocities(Suzuki, 294 ). Many of the works and journals that were cracked down on were by authors and works related to Non-church movement members. This shows the difference between the attitude of the mainline churches and non-church movement members toward the government.
