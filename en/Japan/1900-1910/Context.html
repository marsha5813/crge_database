Japan: Context (1900-1910)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. In this period, there was no religion in Japan that was explicitly designated as the state religion. However, the Meiji government created a new "religion" called "State Shinto" in order to create a modern state centering on the emperor. The Meiji government separated the existing Shinto religion into two distinct branches: denominational Shinto, which was responsible for religion and rituals, and shrine Shinto, which served as the nation's moral code. Teaching Shinto had its own gods and was considered a "religion" as a folk belief officially recognized by the Meiji government. Shrine Shinto, on the other hand, was given a special status as an important ritual of the state, and although it was sung as not a "religion," it was combined with Emperor Shinto and reorganized as a state Shinto that honored the emperor and his family (Murakami, 57-58). The modern state was then centered on the ideology of the Emperor System, and this system was reinforced by the Imperial Japanese Constitution and the Imperial Rescript on Education. Therefore, even though "freedom of religion" was recognized in the Constitution of the Empire of Japan, it was limited to "freedom of religion" under the Emperor's rule. With the enactment of the Constitution of the Empire of Japan and administrative measures, new religions and Christianity, which had previously been subject to various restrictions, were allowed to engage in religious activities similar to those of Shintoism and Buddhism. However, in order to prevent religions (especially new religions) from spreading doctrines and ideas that were contrary to the "national spirit," restrictions were placed on them through disrespectful crimes and police punishment ordinances. The new penal code issued in April 1907 included "crimes against the imperial family" and made disrespect for the emperor's family and the Jingu shrine a crime. This was the strongest state regulation in terms of doctrine for the new religion, which held a variety of deities as objects of worship (Inoue, 476-477). Article 2 of the decree stipulated that any person who spread falsehoods or false rumors, or who practiced witchcraft or prayer on a sick person, or who gave medical treatment to a sick person, and whom the police considered to be "lewd and evil" was subject to punishment, and the expansion of new religions and other organizations was severely restricted (Inoue, 477). The Security Police Law of 1900 banned priests, ministers, monks, and other religious groups from joining political associations. This deprived professional religious people of the right to vote and prohibited them from political activity, thereby almost completely depoliticizing them, but it also created a system in which the government could make the most political use of them. Furthermore, in 1906, shrines throughout Japan were reorganized and consolidated, and in 1907 the Ministry of Home Affairs institutionalized rituals. This led to a series of cases in which rituals that had previously been formed in connection with the culture and customs of each region were modified into state Shinto rituals (Murakami, 86). This is thought to have had the significance of strengthening the influence of state Shinto. The Dajokan-tatsu No. 19, issued in August 1884, ended the traditional direct relationship between the state and religion, in which the state directly selected and appointed religious leaders, and moved Japan's relationship between politics and religion into a new phase. However, it is said that this was not an end to the government's laissez-faire attitude toward religion, but rather a reflection of the deterioration of the previous relationship between state and religion, and a measure aimed at more effectively using religion to mobilize it for national policy. This continued to constrain religion in modern Japan for the next 60 years until the implementation of the Religious Organizations Law in 1930 (Inoue, 473).

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Between 1900 and 1910, social hostilities towards Christianity decreased compared to the preceding decade. This can be attributed to the increase in young intellectuals and the effects of the Russo-Japanese War. Many university-educated young people, known as the “intellectuals(知識層)”, gathered at the Hongo Church of Ebina Danjo and the nondenominational church of Uchimura Kanzo. Students also flocked to Masahisa Uemura's church, and Sunday schools and student youth associations were more active than in the early Meiji period(Ohata, Ikado, 406). In Arimichi Ebisawa and Saburo Ouchi's History of Christianity in Japan, a graph from 1928, which shows that the "Report of the Church Survey" edited by the Japan Christian Federation, shows that the number of Christian believers had increased rapidly from 1902. It shows a rapid increase in congregations since 1902 (Ouchi, 250). Also, during the Russo-Japanese War of 1904, the Christian churches in Japan were divided into two groups: those who cooperated with the war effort and those who were anti-war. The mainstream Christian churches actively cooperated in the war effort. For example, the Japanese government requested Honda Yoichi and Ibuka Kajinosuke to travel to foreign countries and to appeal that the Russo-Japanese war was not aggression. The Young Men's Christian Association also went to the front lines with a tent to provide the soldiers with bathing, laundry, hairdressing, and preaching. This tent project by YMCA has significantly corrected the prejudice against Christianity in Japan(Ohata, Ikado, 409). In October 1909, the 50th anniversary of the start of the Protestant mission to Japan（宣教開始五十年記念回）was held in Tokyo. At this meeting, many politicians came, including Prime Minister Katsura Taro, the Governor of Tokyo, the Mayor of Tokyo, and the US Ambassador(Ohata, Ikado, 413). This shows that Christianity was no longer an object of hostility in Japan. The conclusion of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance with Britain and the Russo-Japanese War also impacted Japan's rising status in the international community. Showing an amicable attitude towards Christianity, which was mainstream in Western countries, was linked to recognizing Japan's '”freedom of religion”, which may have meant showing other countries that Japan was civilized. The Christian churches also actively supported the war effort, and from 1900 onwards, Christianity, Shintoism, and Buddhism established their position as significant religions in Japan.

Although the oppressive measures on the religious activities of Christianity during this period had reduced, a number of riots in which the people, dissatisfied with the peace treaty of the Russo-Japanese War, destroyed newspaper offices, police stations, Christian churches, etc. It is said that approximately 360 facilities were burned down in the Hibiya Burning Incident, which took place on September 5-6, 1905.In addition, it resulted in 17 deaths, 2,000 injured, and more than 2,000 detained prompting authorities to take emergency measures to control the riots.

Also this period might be best described as more of a "clash of churches and religions," a time when the Christian church was politicized to serve national interests after the Sino-Japanese War. During the Russo-Japanese War, 1904-1905, some religious groups promoted cooperation among themselves while seeking nonpartisan religious group solidarity.

In May 1904, for instance, senji shūkyōka kondankai ( the Wartime Religious Leader’s Conference) was held at Shiba park in Tokyo. It was organized by Shintoism, Buddhism, and Christianity leaders and concluded that the three religious groups would unite to serve the national policy of Japan. It can be said that the nonpartisan religious meeting was one of the attempts to mobilize religious groups for the sake of the nation-state rather than encourage religious activities (Ebisawa Arimichi, Encyclopedia of the History of Christianity in Japan, 814).

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. After the Sino-Japanese War in 1900, Japan began its path toward imperialism and won the Russo-Japanese War in 1904 as a war between imperialisms, a period in which Japan established its position as an imperial power in Asia.
    【China】In relations with Qing China, Japan went to war against the Qing under the guise of suppressing the Uihe Dan and promoted imperialism in Qing China. Japan declared war on the Qing Dynasty with an eight-nation allied force, including the United Kingdom and the United States, and occupied Beijing. In 1901, Japan signed the Beijing Protocol, ordering the payment of reparations and allowing the stationing of foreign troops, and the division of the country by the imperialist powers progressed. (Narita, 165-166) On the other hand, at the time of this incident, the Japanese government took a cautious stance, emphasizing cooperation with the Western powers, partly in reflection of the Tripartite Powers' interference (Naraoka, 57) The British were not the only ones to take a cautious attitude toward Japan. Through the Giwadan Incident, the British deepened their confidence in Japan, leading to the conclusion of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in 1902, which accelerated the imperialization of Japan.

【Korea】This period, especially the outbreak of the Russo-Japanese War, accelerated and realized the Japanese colonization of the Korean peninsula. From 1904 onward, the Japanese government signed several Japan-Korea protocols with Korea (Chosun) to allow Japanese military actions within the Korean peninsula, and concluded the first, second, and third Japan-Korea Agreements. Furthermore, Japan proceeded to make Korea a protectorate, taking away Korea's diplomatic rights, and established a supervisor-general in November 1905, with Hirobumi Ito as the first governor-general. The Russo-Japanese War also accelerated the colonization of Korea, and on August 22, 1910, the Korean Annexation Treaty was signed, colonizing Korea.

【Manchuria】In 1905, Japan inherited Russia's interests in Manchuria and promoted development and emigration through the Portsmouth Peace Treaty signed with Russia in 1905 and the Beijing Treaty signed with the Qing Dynasty. The company that played a central role in Japan's expansion into Manchuria at this time and promoted development and emigration was the South Manchuria Railway Company (Manchuria Railway) established in 1906 (Naraoka, p. 60).

【Taiwan】This was the period when the Treaty of Shimonoseki, signed in 1895, established Japanese rule over Taiwan. The Japanese government gave people two years to choose their nationality, and those who chose Chinese nationality were required to move to mainland China. Therefore, those who resided in Taiwan at this time were considered to have chosen Japanese nationality (Isao Chiba, pp. 61-62). In the early years of Japanese rule in Taiwan in 1906, a system of special government was adopted by Shinpei Goto, who served as Taiwan's governor-general. This was a colonial policy adopted from the British empire, which considered Taiwan as a colony that existed outside the Japanese mainland, and attempted to govern it in an independent and special way without applying the laws of the mainland. Therefore, the previously issued Constitution of the Empire of Japan was practically not applied in Taiwan. On the other hand, there was a series of anti-Japanese movements by the indigenous people of Taiwan, including the Beipu Incident in 1907.

⇒ Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War created a great sensation among Asian countries, and many students from Qing China and other countries subsequently came to Japan to study. (Naraoka, 184)

In 1900, Japan was under the influence of the Western powers, so Japan pursued the path of imperialism while maintaining cooperative relations with Western powers. Also, Japan was still unable to compete with the European empires, Japan chose a path to strengthen its economic and military power in cooperation with the Western authorities. ・Russia To rise as one of the imperialist powers in the world, Japan's relationship with Russia was very important to achieve its goal.Following the Sino-Japanese War, 1894-1895, Russia, France, and Germany pressured Japan to return the concession, Liaodong Peninsula to the Qing dynasty. Japan had no other choice but to comply with the demand from the Western powers. Meanwhile, Japan sought to cooperate with Russia over control of the Korean Peninsula. However, Japan and Russia were constantly on a collision course over who gets the exclusive rights in Korea and Manchuria. Due to the unforeseeable geopolitical situation, Russia continued to occupy Manchuria after the (Boxer Rebellion), which was a greater threat for Japan to secure its national interests in the Korean Peninsula. Against this backdrop, Japan concluded that forming an alliance with British would maximize Japan’s interests in the Korean Peninsula and Manchuria. After signing the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in 1902, Japan went to war with Russia in February 1904. The main battlegrounds during the Russo-Japanese war were mostly fought in the Korean Peninsula and the Liaodong Peninsula. Having the critical information from the British intelligence on the Russian military secrets, Japan decided September 1905 would be the most beneficial to end the war with Russia by signing an armistice treaty, the Portsmouth Peace Treaty with America's help in mediating the treaty. Although Japan did not gain much financial reparations from Russia after the Portsmouth Peace Treaty, Japan successfully secured its dominance in the region by obtaining exclusive rights. It included the exclusive right to intervene in the Korean peninsula, receiving the right to develop Manchuria, and the right to lease the Liaodong Peninsula and the southern part of Sakhalin from Russia indefinitely. Discontented over the result of reparations, the Japanese masses expressed their frustration in violence. The angry crowds in Hibiya Park sabotaged and burned the surrounding buildings and other facilities in Tokyo. Despite of the violent reaction from the Japanese masses, the Japanese government commemorated the Russo-Japanese War by erecting a number of war memorials and military heroes in many parts of Japan.

【England】

Japan developed its strategic relations with Britain beginning in January 1902 after signing of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance. Both Japan and Britain portrayed the expansion of Russia’s influence in Asia as a common threat for them.To check the expansion of Russia in Asia, Britain recognized Japanese exclusive interest over Korea and Manchuria. Moreover, Britain agreed to remain neutral in the course of war between Japan and Russia. As part of the outcome of the Russo-Japanese War, Britain recognized Japan's protectorate status in Korea, and Japan recognized Britain's interests in India. As a result, Japan not only built its dominance in East Asia, it also found a strategic partnership with Britain which agreed to join the forces together in the future military conflict. (Naraoka, 168)

【United States 】

The U.S. also saw Russia's expansion in East Asia as a major threat to its national interest and thus it was favorable of Japanese military intervention to stop Russia's advance. Although the U.S. declared its neutrality during the Russo-Japanese War, it actively participated in mediating between Japan and Russia in favor of Russia to retreat from East Asia(Aruga, 155). In July 1905, just before the Portsmouth Conference, the Taft-Katsura Agreement, was signed between Japan and the U.S. It acknowledged mutual interests in colonization of Asian countries. For Japan, it recognized Japan to annex Korea on the basis of the agreement and for the U.S., it cemented its colonial rule over the Philippines. Furthermore, the Root-Takahira Agreement stipulated the status quo in the Asia-Pacific region by recognizing each other's sphere of influence. This agreement allowed the U.S. to recognize Japan's interests in Manchuria and Japan assured the U.S. that it has no territorial ambitions toward the Philippines and its neighboring countries. Although Japan and the United States shared their mutual interests over colonial policies in Asia, the two powers were on a collision course over immigration policy. After the official immigration policy of the US accepted Japanese immigrants in 1885, many Japanese migrated to Hawaii and California in search of opportunities. The local residents of California saw the increased number of Asians including Japanese as a threat to their community. The so called, “Yellow Peril” undermined the social status of Japanese migrants who settled in the mainland of the U.S. after 1885. The spread of Yellow Peril translated into a diplomatic tension between Japan and the U.S. as local authorities segregated Japanese students and their schools in San Francisco. In October 1906, the Japanese government officially protested the segregation of Japanese school children in San Francisco in an attempt to alleviate the segregation targeted Japanese students in San Francisco. The tension between Japan and the U.S. was resolved after the U.S.-Japan Gentlemen's Agreement in 1908. The Agreement voluntarily banned all new migrants on the Japanese side, with the exception of a person re-entering the U.S., parents/wives/children of immigrants in the U.S., students, and merchants. It resulted in the restriction of the number of Japanese migrants in the U.S. and Hawaii after 1908.

F. Summarize cross-nation religious exchange or conflicts. ☐

Before the Japanese missionaries began their overseas religious activities outwardly, the Japanese Christianity emerged after accepting the Western missionaries in the early 20th century. In August 1901, Mormon missionaries, including Grant, came to Japan for the first time, and Mormon missionary work began in Japan.In 1905, Shigeharu Nakata and Charles Cowman of the United States jointly established the Oriental Mission Society, which aimed to evangelize Chinese layperson and other Asian countries. As for new religions,Kinko-kyo established missions and churches in Taiwan after 1902. Kinkyo-kyo expanded its activities to Manchuria later. Tenrikyo also began missionary work in Hawaii and Korea after 1908. After the First Sino-Japanese War in 1896, the Jodo Shinshu sect and the Japanese Christian Church began missionary activities in Taiwan. However, with the influence of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance concluded in 1902, Japan's religious organizations' overseas missionary efforts became even more active. Despite its early missionary work in Taiwan, religious activism was on its course, and Japanese overseas missionary work flourished after the Anglo-Japanese Alliance in 1902. Hiromichi Kozaki, one of the Japanese missionaries, left for Hawaii in July 1902. The Union Church started its missionary work in Korea, establishing a church in Gyeongseong (colonial capital, Seoul) in June 1904. In August of the same year, the Japan Christian Youth League (JCYL) began a tent mission in Manchuria. Moreover, evangelical missionaries from Japan started its religious activism in other Asian countries.
