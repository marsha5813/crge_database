  1. Michio Kozaki(小崎道雄、1888.11.16-1973.6.18)

Michio Kozaki was a pastor of the Kumiai Kyokai(Union Church) and one of the outstanding leaders of the Christian churches in Japan, who did extensive work both within the Christian churches and as a representative of the Japanese church outside the churches (Takenaka, 2). Since I wrote about his prewar and wartime activities in 1940-1950, I will focus here on his activities after the war and during the 1950s and 1960s.

　After the war, Kozaki succeeded Mitsuru Tomita as president of the United Church of Christ in Japan in 1946, a position he held until 1954. He was also elected the first chairman of the National Christian Council Japan (NCC) in 1948 and attended the first World Council of Churches (W.C.C.) in Amsterdam in 1948. At this conference, he was appointed as its central committee member, a post he held for 13 years until 1961 (Takenaka,『日本プロテスタント・キリスト教史』)

There are three representatives of postwar efforts by Kozaki.

　The first is his role as chairman of the United Church of Christ in Japan. Kozaki was elected general chairman at the third General Conference of the Japan Christian Church in June 1946, and he served as the representative of the United Church of Christ in Japan during that period of turmoil until 1954. Whenever a crisis of division in the denomination appeared, such as defections from the denomination, problems with the denomination's internal congregations, or problems with the confession of faith, he called for unity in faith and fellowship in love. However, Kozaki did not take the leadership in dealing with specific problems (Dohi, 48). Rather, he focused his efforts on the following two roles.

Second, it was an activity to demonstrate the usefulness of Christianity to the nation. For example, in developing the New Japan Construction Christian Movement(新日本建設キリスト教運動), he represented Christianity at the New Japan Construction Council held at the Prime Minister's official residence in June 1947. He also attended the World Council of Churches, which he participated in in 1948, and later interviewed the Emperor to report on the meeting. These actions were similar to those of his father, Hiromichi Kozaki, who represented Christianity in Japan and participated in prewar meetings with the prime minister and government officials, preaching the usefulness of Christianity (Dohi, 49).

　Third, he worked on partnerships and cooperation with overseas churches. After the war, when many missionaries wanted to evangelize Japan, Kozaki, as a representative of the Japanese Christian Church, stated that he welcomed the missionary activities of North American denominations as long as they were willing to work for the sake of Japan. More importantly, he represented Japan at the World Council of Churches (WCC) meeting in Amsterdam in August 1948, the beginning of the postwar ecumenical movement, and participated as a member of the Central Committee until 1961. He attended the annual meetings of the Central Committee held in various parts of the world as much as possible, and wrote to Mrs. Kozaki about his activities at this meeting. She shared his letter to the church's weekly bulletin, so church members could know what Kozaki learned and thought at the meeting (Dohi, 51).　Also, at the W.C.C. conference held in Evanston in the summer of 1954, Kozaki and others presented a report on the prohibition of the use of nuclear weapons, including atomic and hydrogen bombs, as the voice of the Christian Church in Japan, and their voice was incorporated into the conference report (Takenaka, 7).

　As for the reasons why Kozaki was aptly suited for the postwar ecumenical movement, it has been pointed out that in his experience of the United States, and he was able to interact mildly with everyone, and that the people of Reinanzaka Church supported his activities (Takenaka, 11-13).

　Such activities of Kozaki were good for the postwar Christian churches in Japan to participate in the ecumenical activities of the world Christian community. On the other hand, researcher Dohi mentioned that Kozaki's way of life did not change from prewar to postwar (Dohi, 56). His concern was always to maintain and expand the church through evangelism. Therefore, during the war, it was important to take advantage of the ruling of the emperor system, and in the same sense, after the war, it was essential to be in tune with the democratic trend. Kozaki also constantly tried to show that Christianity was a useful religion for the nation, and during the war, he saw Christianity as the moral energy for the practice of imperialism, after the war, he considered it the spiritual foundation for achieving peace and democracy (Dohi, 57). From this attitude of Kozaki, there is no reflection on Christianity's cooperation in the war or the issue of its responsibility for the war.

2. Sakae Akaiwa（赤岩栄, 1903.4.6-1966.11.28)

Akaiwa Sakae(赤岩栄,1903–1966), a pastor of the United Church of Christ Japan, who declared to become a member of the Japan communist party in 1949.

Akaiwa was a pastor of the United Church of Christ in Japan who became involved in Marxism.

　He was born in Aichi Prefecture, Akaiwa, and studied theology under Tokutaro Takakura at Tokyo Theological Seminary after moving to Tokyo. After graduation, he was assigned to the Sado Mission of the Japan Christian Church and later became pastor of Uehara Church in Tokyo at the request of Takakura. After becoming pastor of Uehara Church, Akaiwa was enthusiastic about evangelism and writing from the theological standpoints of Calvin and Luther, and from the 1930s, he was greatly influenced by Karl Barth. In April 1931, Akaiwa published “Marxism and Christianity(「マルクス主義とキリスト教」),” in which he defended Christianity by stating that Christianity, which stands on the faith of absolute revelation, transcends the unitary grasp of Marxist materialist history (Dohi, ).

After the war, he remained committed to the evangelical faith, but also to social practice, and became sympathetic to Marxism. He sought to understand humans as an individual standing before God, and man as a whole person. In other words, he saw man as being in an “I and Thou” relationship and understood him within the framework of society and the individual (Yagi, 132). Akaiwa affirmed Marxism, which grasped man as a whole person, and called it humanism (Yagi, 132) Behind his sympathy for Marxism was his “humanism". And underlying that “humanism” was the fact that his encounter with Jesus disclosed to him the essence of humanity (Yagi, 133)

Furthermore, Akaiwa believed that Christianity and communism could be compatible because they were truths in different dimensions: faith and practice. In 1949, Akaiwa declared his intention to join the Communist Party, and the United Church of Christ in Japan issued a chairman's statement that Christianity and communism were incompatible, in April 1949, a special committee was held to discuss with Akaiwa.

As a result, in July he declared that he was loyal to the Order, that he was committed to evangelism, that he would refrain from joining the Communist Party, and that his previous words and actions were regrettable. Akaiwa made a statement because he was about to join the party as a pastor, but he disagreed with the United Church of Christ in Japan's views on Christianity and communism. Some also argue that, theologically, he could not fully affirm Marxism (Yagi, 133) Therefore, he defended his views and gradually became critical of the United Church of Christ in Japan from this time on (Dohi, 449).

  1. The United Church of Christ in Japan/日本基督教団

The Japanese Christian Church conducted aggressive evangelistic activities in the 1950s.

For example, the Japanese Christian Church implemented the “Five-Year Evangelism( 五ヶ年伝道)” program. This was a new evangelistic effort following the New Japan Building Christian Movement(新日本建設キリスト教運動) that continued until 1947.

The goals of this mission movement were to strengthen and build churches, enhance individual churches, make small churches self-supporting and strong, evangelize unreached areas throughout Japan and various social strata, train evangelists, and establish new evangelistic facilities (Dohi, p. 436).

Unlike the New Japan building Christian Movement, which aimed at mass evangelism and salvation movements, these activities aimed to build churches as centers of evangelism, strengthen them, and make them places where people could fellowship (Dohi, p. 436).

To implement this movement, an evangelistic structure within The United Church of Christ in Japan　was established, and a new General Evangelism Committee(総合伝道委員会) was set up to work on the evangelism of the church. Within this committee, four specialized committees were established for rural evangelism, women's evangelism, youth evangelism, and later, workplace evangelism(職域伝道). Furthermore, at the 7th General Conference in 1952, the Domestic Mission Association (内国伝道会) was established, which aimed for self-sufficient evangelism without relying on foreign support (Dohi, p. 437). Among these, the committee for workplace evangelism(職域伝道委員会), established in November 1951, aimed to provide Bible study groups and Christian worker formation and training in the workplace for workers who had not been adequately evangelized (Dohi, 437). The guidance and cooperation of Henry Jones, who was invited as a missionary specializing in workplace evangelism, and Sumiya Mikio(隅谷三喜男), a professor at the University of Tokyo, was significant (Dohi, 437). In 1950, the missionary funds received from overseas were used for church revival, rural evangelism, and pioneer evangelism.

　In October 1954, after completing the five-year evangelization, the Mission Headquarters passed a plan for the centennial commemoration of the 100th anniversary of the mission (宣教100年記念伝道) at its 8th General Conference, which covered the period from 1955 to 1959. The basic spirit of this movement was that each church should establish a concrete plan with the voluntary cooperation of individual churches and that both clergy and laity should be aware of the importance of evangelism (Dohi, 439-440). (Dohi, 439-440) The General Evangelism Committee was to administer the project, and each specialized committee was also to implement specific plans. However, although the commemorative evangelism proceeded with various themes, it failed to realize its intended goals (Dohi, 440).

　The United Church of Christ in Japan also became actively involved in the peace movement, issuing a “Christian Peace Declaration(「キリスト教平和宣言」)” in November 1950 (Ishiki, 119). However, these activities did not include any remorse or apology for its past colonialism or its own cooperation in the war.

2. 日本キリスト教協議会（National Chrisitian Council in Japan)

The National Christian Council in Japan(日本キリスト教協議会) was established in May 1948 as a consultative body of major Protestant denominations and groups, with the goal of conducting Christian activities such as evangelism, education, and service as the joint work of the organizations. It is also responsible for liaison and cooperation with foreign Christian organizations (Dohi, p. 423).

　After the war, many churches left the United Church of Christ in Japan and rebuilt their own denominations, creating the need for a new liaison and consultative body, so the National Christian Council in Japan was established in the same year that the World Council of Churches was founded. The content of the organization was almost the same as that of the former Japan Christian Federation(日本基督教連盟), since it was organized before the war and dissolved during the war by the Japan Christian Federation, which took over such organization.When it was established in 1948, Michio Kozaki was the president and Akira Ebisawa was the general secretary.

In Japan, the council works for interdenominational fellowship and cooperates with the Catholic Church in prayer for Christian unity and social activities. In addition, in terms of social witnesses, such as the Yasukuni Shrine issue, peace and anti-nuclear issues, and discrimination issues, the church is working as a contact point for cooperation with non-Christian organizations. (Shoji, 1040). As for activities outside of Japan, they have joined the World Mission Division of the World Council of Churches (CWME) to fellowship with the world church, and have joined the Asian Christian Council to cooperate in missions with Asian countries (Shoji, 1040).

The postwar Protestant churches are characterized by the division into two groups: the mainline Protestant churches belonging to the National Christian Council in Japan” and the evangelical churches belonging to the Japan Evangelical Association (JEF, established in 1968) (Ochiai, p. 223).

One of their most active activities since the 1950s was the campaign against rearmament. Among other things, they worked on the atomic energy issue, passing resolutions calling for the cessation of atomic and hydrogen bomb testing.

ICU is an Ecumenical Christian university founded in April 1953 and is a symbol of Christian schools in the postwar period. Since the beginning of the 20th century, there has been a movement to create an Christian university through the cooperation of various denominations, but the war invalidated the plan. After the war, Christian leaders proposed this movement again, and the American Church Federation Mission, which came to Japan in October 1945, and the American Christian Education Survey Mission, which came to Japan in March 1946, promised to cooperate with it (Ebisawa).　Furthermore, the Japanese and American church federations and foreign mission councils also organized cooperation committees, and together, they set up branches nationwide and raised funds for the establishment of the organization. It has also been pointed out that the background to these activities was that the American Christian community was aware of its responsibility for the war (Dohi, 429).

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. B.

In the 1950s, Japanese society was rapidly turning right-wing with the outbreak of the Korean War, the Cold War between the U.S. and the Soviet Union, and the rapid formation of U.S. military bases. After 1945, the “Christian boom” continued until about 1952, when the U.S. occupation policy ended. However, that boom ended partly due to the reverse course from about 1948 and the end of the occupation policy.

By the 1950s, however, discomfort with Christianity had largely dissipated, and the number of mission schools increased. During this period, various missionaries and missions from abroad came to Japan to evangelize Christianity in Japan. For example, in 1950, Lawrence L. Lacour, a pastor of the United Methodist Church from the United States, came to Japan and conducted a traveling mission there. He and his wife and two women conducted musical evangelism using marimbas and other musical instruments, and in about five years, they visited 128 towns, attracting 430,000 listeners and 38,000 converts. They also conducted evangelistic activities during the summer, which resulted in the birth of 31 churches. Thirty thousand people attended the Billy Graham Evangelistic Conference held in February 1956, and 1,200 people made up their minds.

Japan Protestant Centennial(日本宣教百年記念聖書信仰運動)

In 1959, the Japan Protestant Centennial was launched to celebrate the 100th anniversary of the Protestant mission in Japan. This movement was developed by evangelical denominations with “biblical faith” at its core. In the fall of the same year, the movement held various conferences in various locations. On November 18, Pastor Edward J. Young of the Pupils Church in Toronto, Canada, and New Testament scholar Dr. Roger Nicol came to Japan to lecture at the Tokyo conference.

This movement later transitioned from a single movement to an organizational entity as the Japan Protestant Conference. The leading players during this period were Minoru Okada of the Reformed Church in Japan and Futao Tsutada of tImmanuel General Mission. The Japan Protestant Conference accepted the Bible as “the untrue word of God” and aimed to promote the understanding and practice of the Christian faith according to the Bible. This organization later served as the founding body in reorganizing the Japan Evangelical Association

Although not directly related to the Christian promotion movement, Christians were also active in the peace movement. In January 1951, the “Association of Christians for Peace(キリスト者平和の会）” was formed. This organization developed its activities from opposing rearmament and defending the Peace Constitution. Each local Christian formed Christian peace groups like the Association for Peace in various places. Therefore, in March 1952, the Council of Christian Peace Movement Organizations(基督教平和運動団体協議会) was formed to liaise and collaborate with these groups. This council held a peace conference every year, and it sought to reach out to churches and cooperate with other organizations (Dohi, 450)

Many such peace movements were carried out. In March 1954, the United States conducted a hydrogen bomb test at Bikini Atoll in the South Pacific. The Japanese fishing vessel Daigo Fukuryū Maru (Lucky Dragon No. 5) was exposed to radiation, sparking a surge in Japan’s grassroots movement to ban atomic and hydrogen weapons. This movement eventually evolved into an anti-American protest movement (Shimo, 609). The Peace Committee of the Young Women’s Christian Association of Japan (YWCA) sent a statement opposing the hydrogen bomb test to YWCAs around the world. At International Christian University (ICU), an all-campus assembly was held in June to discuss the Bikini hydrogen bomb test issue (Shimo, 609).

The United Church of Christ in Japan also showed an interest in peace issues, and in 1950, it adopted a "Statement on Peace" calling for the early resolution of various problems. However, unlike the Association of Christians for Peace, the United Church of Christ in Japan's statement was unfavorable in asserting that they had been entrusted with the "Gospel of Reconciliation" without any apology or reflection on Asian countries.

Key Theological Issues

Due to the influence of postwar changes in the Japanese language education system, there was a demand for a translation of the Bible from the conventional literary style to a colloquial style. Therefore, in 1950, the Japan Bible Society decided to revise the Bible and published it as the “Colloquial Translation of the New Testament” in 1954 (Suzuki, 341).

In 1953, another colloquial translation of the New Testament, which had been continued before the war by Toraji Tsukamoto, a non-church member, was published in “Bible Knowledge(『聖書知識』)". In addition, the Colloquial Translation of the New Testament(『口語訳新約聖書』), translated by Shuichiro Watase and Tomio Muto in 1952 was published by Kirisutokyo Newspaper(キリスト教新聞社）, and the Colloquial Translation of the New Testament, translated by the Italian Catholic Missionary Federocp Barbaro, in 1953, was published by Don Bosco (Suzuki, 341).

In 1955, a colloquial translation of the Old Testament was also published by the Japan Bible Society, and in the same year, “The Bible,” which combined the Old and New Testaments, was published. The translation as a whole was inherited from the “Taisho Revised Translation” published in 1917, with the major change being the unification of “Jehovah（エホバ）” with “Lord（主）” in the Old Testament (Suzuki, 342). Criticism of these Bibles focused on the Japan Bible Society's version of the Bible, but the translators “sought above all clarity of meaning and clarity of meaning” (Suzuki, 342)

Other publications include “Hymns” by the Hymn Committee of the Japan Christian Association in December 1954, the publication of the Pointed Oral Bible in 1956, and “Hymns” edited by the Hymn Committee of the Japan Evangelical Fellowship (日本福音連盟) in 1958.

As for the postwar theological situation, Barthian theology, which had strongly influenced Japan since the 1930s, was revived after the war and became the center of Protestant theology in Japan. In addition, the theology of Swiss theologian Emil Brunner, who stayed in Japan, American liberal theologian Reinhold Niebuhr, and German theologian Paul Johannes Tillich,　were also introduced to Japan through Kiyoko Takeda's(武田清子) research. In addition, Rudolf Karl Bultmann was introduced, and post-Bultmann Pianism as a new theology was introduced, and the theological debate between Barth theology and Bultmann was actively conducted. Furthermore, this was also the period when biblical studies were established in Japan, and biblical studies, which had been a church discipline, became a university discipline (Yagi, 127).

The following three theological discussions from this period are worth mentioning.

Three theological debates of this period include the following.

(1) Yoshizo Kitamori(北森嘉蔵) and “TheTheology of the pain of God(『神の痛みの神学』)　

　In 1946, the Lutheran theologian Kitamori Kazo published “The Theology of the Pain of God,” which was widely read by non-Christians. The “pain of God” refers to God's love for sinners who have defied God's love and become objects of God's destruction, and God's willingness to bear His wrath and love sinners even more. When the God of judgment and the God of forgiveness are the same, love and forgiveness for sinners are impossible without God's contradiction and conflict, or “pain” (Yagi, 129). This book received a great response because it was an original organizational theology written by a Japanese. However, Kitamori's theology was generally unpopular among Japanese theologians because of his criticism of Barthianism at a time when Barthian theology was dominant, because his theology was considered Patripassianism, and because he favorably evaluated Japanese cultural traditions (Yagi, 131).

Kitamori's theology attracted the attention of the non-church people and philosophers in Japan and received renewed attention when the “Passion of God” gained prominence in European theology from the 1960s onward.

(2) Sakae Akaiwa'(赤岩栄)s Relationship with the Communist Party

During this period, a controversial issue was the declaration of the pastor Sakae Akaiwa, who had been an ardent Barthian before the war, to join the Communist Party. After the war, Akaiwa, who had grown sympathetic to Marxism, made clear his intention to join the Communist Party in 1949, arguing that Christianity and communism could be compatible because they were truths of different dimensions of faith and practice. Furthermore, from around 1955, he gave up Barth's theology because of the question of how Barth, a single human being, could know so much about God. Such criticism of Akaiwa came from the United Church of Christ in Japan and Kitamori. Although Akaiwa did not join the Communist Party, he was a conscious “humanist” and his humanism was based on his “encounter with Jesus” (Yagi, 135-136).

(3) Nobuo Odagiri(小田切信男) and Jesus, the Son of God

　　In 1949, while participating in YMCA activities, Odagiri questioned the statement in the YMCA's purpose statement: “The YMCA looks to Jesus Christ as its God and Savior, based on the Bible. Odagiri, who was born in Hokkaido, attended Sapporo Independent Church from childhood and was baptized in 1927, was a devoted Christian.

Odagiri insisted that Jesus Christ was not God, but the Son of God. He claimed that the Bible does not say that Jesus is God, nor can the Trinity, which makes Jesus the second-degree God, be found in the Bible. Therefore, he argued that “based on the Bible” Jesus cannot be seen as God. This is an argument based on Biblicism but he believed that if Jesus were God, his death on the cross would be an act of God and that it was not God who died and rose from the dead, but only the Son of God (Yagi, 137). In 1955, Odagiri held a public debate with Kitamori and others in the YMCA's journal “Kaitakusha(『開拓者』),” where Kitamori argued that there was biblical evidence that Jesus Christ was God, but Odagiri did not accept his criticism and continued to flaunt his claims (Yagi, 138).

Founder and first president of the new religious movement Seichō no Ie (House of Growth)

Place of Birth: Torihara Village, Yatabe District, Hyōgo Prefecture (present-day Hyōgo Ward, Kobe City)

Place of Death: Nagasaki

Masaharu Taniguchi was born as the second son of Otokichi and Tsuru Taniguchi. At the age of four, he was adopted by his aunt and her husband (Inoue, 124). While attending Osaka Municipal Oka Middle School, he aspired to become a literary figure and also developed an interest in religion. In 1911, he entered the preparatory course in the Faculty of Letters at Waseda University and continued his literary activities. In 1912, he advanced to the Department of English Literature at Waseda University. Around this time, he came under the influence of thinkers such as Tolstoy, William James, and Bergson (Inoue, 124).

However, during a visit home, he became involved in a scandal involving a woman, and his adoptive parents cut off his financial support. As a result, Taniguchi dropped out of university in 1914. He later worked at Settsu Spinning in Osaka but became disillusioned with the work after witnessing the harsh labor conditions endured by the female factory workers under his supervision, and returned to his hometown (Inoue, 124).

Around this time, Taniguchi developed an interest in spiritualism and hypnotism. In 1918, he joined Kōdō Ōmoto (present-day Ōmoto) and moved to Ayabe in Kyoto. During this period, he also encountered Nishida Tenko of Ittōen, but eventually grew dissatisfied with both Ōmoto and Nishida’s lifestyle and withdrew from both. Meanwhile, he began assisting the Psychic Science Association of Saburō Tsuwano, an acquaintance from his time in Ōmoto (Inoue, 124).

Previously idealizing a life of poverty and simplicity, Taniguchi was overwhelmed with a sense of powerlessness when his daughter fell ill and he could not afford to consult a doctor. One night, he had a mystical experience in which he conversed with God in a dream and heard a divine voice say, “There is no such thing as matter.” He came to understand that his life had been, in fact, imprisoned by materialism (Inoue, 125). Later, while practicing spiritual training at his home in Kobe, he heard another voice: “There is no matter. There is Reality. Reality is God.” In 1929, he heard yet another divine message: “Arise now.”

　谷口は、谷口音吉とつるの次男として生まれ、4歳の時に叔母夫婦の養嫡子となった（Ionue, 124) 。大阪市立岡中学校に在籍した頃から文学者を目指し、宗教に対しても関心を抱いていた。1911年早稲田大学高等予科（文科）に入学し、文学活動を続け、1912年には早稲田大学英文科に進学した。この頃からトルストイ、ウィリアム・ジェイムス、ベルクソンなどの思想に大きな影響を受けた(Ionue, 124)。しかし、帰省中に女性との問題を起こし、養父母から仕送りを止められた谷口は、1914年に大学を中退した。その後、大阪の摂津紡績に勤務するも過酷な環境での労働を強いられる女工たちの姿を監督するうちにその仕事に嫌気がさし、故郷に帰った（Ionue, 124)

それまでは、清貧の生活を理想としていたが、自分の娘が病気になった時に医者にも見せられないという現実に無力感に苛まれた。そして、ある夜に夢の中で神と対話するという神秘体験をし、「物質はない」という神の声にそれまでの生活が実は物質に囚われた生活であったと理解した（Ionue, 125)。その後、神戸の自宅で修行をしていた際に「物質はない。実相がある。実相とは神である」という声を聞き、さらに1929年に「今起て」との声を聞いた。

On March 1, 1930, Taniguchi launched the magazine Seichō no Ie. This date is regarded as the official founding day of the Seichō no Ie religion (Inoue, 125). In 1933, he moved to Tokyo, making it the center of his activities. Around this time, he established the religion’s core method of propagation—evangelism through printed literature. He also emphasized the importance of education; in 1935, he founded the magazine Education of Life (Seimei no Kyōiku) and established the Seichō no Ie Educational Federation. The movement gradually spread across the country, and by 1941 it was recognized as a religious organization under the Religious Organizations Law.

Taniguchi advocated for the idea of the “unity of all religions” (bankyō kiitsu) and promoted a spiritually cosmic, emperor-centered view of history. His Japan-centric ideology persisted both before and after World War II, making him a highly influential ideologue of conservative, patriotic movements (Nakano, 82). During the war, he firmly believed in the imperial military’s assured victory and worked to boost wartime morale. He donated military aircraft to the army and navy and gave lectures across Manchuria and various parts of China.

As a result, in 1948, he was purged from public office by the Allied Occupation (GHQ). However, immediately after the war, he quickly formed a political organization, and in the first House of Councillors election in 1947, Seichō no Ie successfully got Torio Yano elected (Nakano, 82). In the 1950s, Taniguchi intensified his political engagement. In 1953, he founded the Seichō no Ie Election Strategy Committee, and in 1957, he formally broke away from the new religious movement sphere, making his right-wing orientation explicit.

He also became active in nationalist campaigns, such as the movement to promote the Hinomaru (the Japanese flag) and the restoration and revision of the Meiji Constitution. In 1958, he joined as a founding member of the Self-Defense National Council (renamed the Japan National Council the following year), a national movement led by the right wing of the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP). Deeply alarmed by the intensification of student movements, which he saw as paving the way toward communization in Japan, Taniguchi took a firm oppositional stance. In 1975, he relocated the headquarters of Seichō no Ie to Nagasaki.

そのため1948年にはGHQから公職追放されたが、終戦直後にいち早く政治結社を結成し、1947年の第一次参議院選挙では生長の家の矢野酉雄を当選させてた（Nakano, 82) 1950年代に入ると、谷口は政治活動をさらに活発化させ、1953年に「生長の家選挙対策委員会」を立ち上げ、1957年には新宗教を脱退し、右翼化を鮮明にした。他にも日の丸高揚運動や明治憲法復元改正運動などに力をはじめ、1958年には自民党右派が主導した国民運動団体「自衛国民会議（翌年に「日本国民会議」と改称）」に発起人として加わるなど、右寄りの国民運動に積極的に関与した(Nakano, 82)。特に学生運動の激化は、日本の共産主義化につながるものとして、危機感を募らせ、これと真っ向から対決する姿勢を見せた。1975年には本山を長崎に移した。

Tokuchika Miki (御木徳近, April 8, 1900 – February 2, 1983)

Founder of the Hito no Michi Kyōdan before World War II, and later reestablished the movement as the PL Kyōdan (Perfect Liberty Church) after the war.

Place of Birth: Matsuyama City, Ehime Prefecture

Place of Death: Unknown

  1. Tokuchika Miki （御木徳近, 1900.4.8-1983.2.2)

戦前に「ひとのみち教団」を創設し、戦後はこれをPL教団として再出発を図った。

出生地：愛媛県松山市

Tokuchika Miki was born to Tokuichi, a Buddhist priest (who later returned to lay life). After the death of his wife, Tokuichi converted to Tokumitsu-kyō, a religious movement founded by Tokumitsu Kaneda, and became a teacher of the faith. Tokuchika, following in his father’s footsteps, became an apprentice teacher in Tokumitsu-kyō as well.

He later moved between several religious organizations and, in 1928, transferred to Fusō-kyō. In 1931, the sect was renamed Fusō-kyō Hito no Michi Kyōdan (Inoue, 184). By 1935, it claimed over one million adherents. Tokuchika became the second-generation spiritual leader in 1936. However, in April 1937, the Hito no Michi Kyōdan was forcibly disbanded by government order (Inoue, 184).

After being released from detention in 1945, Tokuchika reestablished the organization under the name Perfect Liberty (PL), aiming to rebuild the movement. From 1955, he became involved in education, and that year, PL Gakuen High School was founded. In 1959, a junior high school was added, followed by a junior college in 1974.

御木徳近は、仏教の住職（その後、還俗）をしていた父徳一のもとに生まれた。父徳一はは妻を亡くし、金田徳光を教祖とする徳光教に入信し、その教師となった。息子である徳近も徳光も徳満教の見習い教師となった。

その後いつくかの宗教を行き来し、1928年に扶桑教に転属し、1931年に扶桑教ひとのみち教団と改称した。（Inoue, 184) 1935年には公称信者数が100万人を超えた。しかし、1936年に徳近が２代目教祖を継承したが、1937年4月ひとのみち教団は閉鎖命令により解散させられた。（Inoue, 184)

戦後、1945年に釈放された御木は、パーフェクトリバティ(Perfect liverty, PL)と命名し、組織の立て直しをはかる。1955年から教育に携わり、PL学園高校がこの年開校した。1959年には同中学校、1974年には短大が開校。

Due to his experience of religious suppression before the war, Tokuchika Miki likely felt a strong sense of solidarity among new religious movements when rebuilding religious activities in the postwar period (Inoue, 185). As a result, in 1951, together with Nikkyō Niwano of Risshō Kōsei-kai and others, he co-founded the Federation of New Religious Organizations of Japan (Shinshūren) and served as its first executive director. He went on to serve as chairman for 14 years.

The PL Kyōdan began expanding its base of followers overseas, particularly in Brazil, where it gained a large following. In 1960, Tokuchika toured North and South America, traveling around the world at a leisurely pace.

In November 1973, he was invited to the Vatican and held a meeting with Pope Paul VI.

One of Tokuchika’s notable characteristics was his role in dramatically transforming the traditional image of Japanese religion (Inoue, 185). Using Roman letters in the name of his religious organization and expressing its mission in English reflected the Americanization of postwar Japanese society, and while such moves may be seen as part of broader social trends, they were truly groundbreaking within the religious sphere.

戦前に弾圧された経験から、戦後の宗教活動を展開していく上で、新宗教の結束を感じたと思われる（Inoue, 185)　そのため、1951年立正佼成会の庭野日敬らと一緒に、新日本宗教団体連合会（新宗連）を結成し、その初代理事となった。以後、14年間理事長を務めた。PL教団は、国外にも信者を増やすようになり、特に何倍には多くの信者ができた。1960年には、北米・南米を順教し、世界一周を楽しむ余裕をもった。

In August 1951, Taniguchi of the Seichō no Ie religious organization was released from his public office ban, and in October, the Federation of New Religious Organizations of Japan was established—marking a period of increased activity among new religious movements (Nakano, 79–80).

On June 3, 1952, the day the San Francisco Peace Treaty came into effect, Emperor Shōwa and the Empress made their first postwar pilgrimage to Ise Grand Shrine. This was followed by visits to Meiji Shrine on July 31 and Yasukuni Shrine on October 16. In response to these developments, movements to revive Shinto gained momentum, exemplified by the Japan War-Bereaved Families Association’s resolution advocating for “state protection of Yasukuni Shrine.” Among these efforts, the Shinto establishment regards the “Movement to Restore National Foundation Day (Kigensetsu)” as the most successful example (Nakano, 80).

Shinnihon Shūkyō Dantai Rengōkai (Federation of New Religious Organizations of Japan, or Shinshūren)

  1. 新日本宗教団体連合会（新宗連） Shinnihon Shūkyō Dantai Rengōkai (Shinshūren)

The Federation of New Religious Organizations of Japan—commonly referred to as Shinshūren—was established in October 1951 by 24 new religious organizations. Founding members included Risshō Kōsei-kai, PL Kyōdan, Myōjikai, Bussho Gonenkai, World Salvation Church, Seichō no Ie, among others. The first chairman was Tokuchika Miki of Perfect Liberty, and the second was Nikkyō Niwano of Risshō Kōsei-kai.

During the war, new religious organizations either cooperated with the state or were subjected to government suppression under ultranationalism. For this reason, the postwar guarantees of “freedom of religion” and the “principle of separation of religion and state” as defined in Article 20 of the Constitution were seen as foundational not only for the emergence of new religions, but also for the establishment of Shinshūren itself (Hirohashi, 85).

Shinshūren promotes the goals of “human welfare” and “world peace.” From its inception, it engaged in the anti-nuclear movement and actively pursued peace activism, while also engaging in ongoing study of “war and peace” (Hirohashi, 86). In the 1960s, Shinshūren opposed the proposed Yasukuni Shrine State Protection Bill, on the grounds that it violated the principle of separation of religion and state (Hirohashi, 86).

The idea of stripping religious organizations of their religious character through a single law and placing them under state control was naturally seen as a return to wartime religious oppression. Thus, Shinshūren placed great emphasis on honoring and memorializing the war dead. In a chaotic postwar society where many had lost their belief systems, prioritizing such memorial practices was a natural step in addressing people’s real suffering. This focus likely contributed to the broad popular support Shinshūren received (Hirohashi, 87).

However, Shinshūren firmly upheld the principle that “memorials for the war dead should be conducted based on individual belief and faith, and not by the state or other public institutions” (Hirohashi, 87).

新日本宗教団体連合会（通称、新宗連）は、1951年10月に24つの新宗教の団体によって結成された。加盟団体は、立正佼成会、PL教団、妙治会、仏所護念会、PL教団、世界救済教、生長の家などがあり、初代理事長はパーフェクトリバティーの御木徳近、２代目には、立正佼成会の庭野日敬が就任した。

　戦時中、新宗教教団は超国家主義のもと国家に協力または取締りの対象とされた。そのため、戦後の憲法二十条に定める「信教の自由」「政教分離の原則」は新宗教成立の、ひいては新宗教連存立の基盤と捉えているのである(Hirohashi, 85)。

新宗連は、その目的に「人類の福祉」と「世界平和」を掲げている。結成直後から原水禁運動に関わり、平和運動にせ局的に取り組む一方「戦争と平和」について学習を重ねていった(Hirohashi, 86)。さらに、1960年代に「靖國神社国家護持法案」問題において、新宗連は政教分離の原則から反対の立場をとった（Hirohashi,86)

たった一つの法律によって宗教法人から宗教性を剥奪し、国管理下に置くという行為は、戦時中の宗教統制の再来と当然受け止めるられた。そのため、新宗連は、戦没者の慰霊や供養を尊重した。戦後の混乱した社会の中で、信念体系を失った人々を救済し、民衆の現実的苦悩を解決していくにあたっては戦没者の供養を重視することは当然のことであった。だからこそ、新宗連は多くの大衆の支持を得ることができたのであろう（Hirohashi, 87)　しかし、その「戦没者の供養は個人の信仰や信念に基づいて行うべきであり、国などの公の機関がするべきではない」と新宗連は政教分離の原則をしっかりと踏まえた考えを打ち出した（Hirohashi, 87)。

Amaterasu Kōtaijingū-kyō

  1. 天照皇大神宮教

During the latter half of the 1950s and the period of rapid economic growth, many new religious movements experienced rapid expansion. One of the most prominent among them was Amaterasu Kōtaijingū-kyō, often referred to as the “dancing religion.”

The founder, Sayo Kimura (1900–1967), was born into a farming family in Yamaguchi Prefecture. After graduating from elementary school, she spent her time engaged in agriculture and caring for her siblings. She later married into another farming family in Tabuse Town, which is known as the hometown of two prime ministers: Nobusuke Kishi and Eisaku Satō (Murakami, 184).

Sayo was a woman endowed with both leadership and persuasive ability, and she took the initiative in handling a variety of tasks. However, her family home caught fire, and the incident caused her deep psychological trauma. Seeking answers, Sayo consulted an Inari gyōja (a practitioner who venerates Inari) in a neighboring town. The practitioner told her that if she sincerely devoted herself to faith, the identity of the arsonist would be revealed. Motivated by this, Sayo committed herself to intense spiritual training (Murakami, 186).

1950年代半から高度経済成長の時期において新宗教の急激な成長があった。

中でも代表的なものが「踊る宗教」と呼ばれた天照皇大神宮教である。

教祖の木村サヨ（1900-1967)は山口県の農家に生まれ、小学校を卒業した後、農業と兄弟の世話をして過ごしていた。その後田布施町の農家と結婚する。田布施町は岸信介、佐藤栄作両首相の出身地として知られる（Murakami, 184)

　サヨは指導力と説得力を兼ね備えた女性であり、率先して様々な仕事をこなした。しかし、一家は火事を起こし、このことに深い心理的打撃を受けたサヨは、とりな町の稲荷行者で稲荷を奉る人に相談した。その結果、サヨが信心すれば犯人がわかるとし、小夜は修行に励んだ（Murakami, 186)

Toward the end of the Pacific War, Sayo began delivering street sermons on the corners of Tabuse Town (Murakami, 188). After Japan’s defeat, she came to recognize herself as the only begotten child of God and a savior, calling for the realization of a divine nation and launching vigorous missionary activities. Sayo denounced all established authorities—including the emperor—and shocked people who were wandering in despair amid the postwar disillusionment (Murakami, 188).

Her teachings were characterized by a strong apocalyptic worldview. One of the central practices was the Dance of Egolessness, in which believers, eyes closed, expressed their joy at being saved by God through ecstatic dancing. This ritual recalled the tradition of odori nenbutsu (dancing nembutsu), producing a sense of collective ecstasy (Murakami, 189). As her teachings became more systematized, they absorbed elements from other religions such as Seichō no Ie and Nichiren Buddhism, deepening their eclecticism. After the formation of the movement, it increasingly took on conservative political and social tendencies.

Given that Tabuse had a large number of emigrants to North and South America, Sayo expanded the movement to Hawaii in 1952. From there, she established branches centered around Japanese emigrants in places including the U.S. mainland, Taiwan, India, Thailand, the Middle East, Europe, Africa, and South America (Shimada, 67).

Although many Japanese new religions gained followers abroad after World War II, Amaterasu Kōtaijingū-kyō was among the pioneers of this international expansion (Shimada, 67).

太平洋戦争も末期に入った頃にサヨは、田布施の街角で辻説法を始めた（Murakami, 188) その後、日本が敗戦を迎えた際には、サヨは神の独り子であり、救世主であると自覚し、神国実現を呼びかけて活発な布教を始めた。サヨは天皇をはじめ、既成の全ての権威を罵倒し、敗戦直後の虚脱の中に彷徨う人々をおどろかせた（Murakami, 188)

　サヨの教義は、強烈な週末観を特徴とし、目を閉じて神に救われたよろこびを表して踊る無我の舞はとおく踊躍念仏の伝統をひく、集団的エクスタシーを現出した（Murakami, 189) さらに教義の体系化の過程で、生長の家の教義と日蓮教学をはじめたような宗教の影響を受けた集合性を深めるとともに、集団形成後は、政治、社会に対しては保守的傾向が強くなった。

　また、田布施が南北米に移民を多く出していることからも、1952年にハワイに進出し、他にもアメリカ本土や台湾、インド、タイ、中近東、ヨーロッパ、アフリカ、南米と世界をめぐり、日系人を中心に各地に支部を作った（Shimada,67)

Risshō Kōseikai

Risshō Kōseikai is a lay Buddhist organization in the Nichiren tradition, founded in 1938 as an offshoot of Reiyūkai. Myōkō Naganuma (1889–1956) served as the de facto spiritual leader, while Nikkyō Niwano (b. 1906) was the organizer.

Niwano, initially drawn into Tengu Fudō worship in search of healing for his eldest daughter, later practiced ascetic rituals and studied name divination (seimeigaku). In 1934, following the illness of his second daughter, he joined Reiyūkai (Murakami, 34). In 1938, Niwano and Naganuma left Reiyūkai in opposition to the leadership of Kimi Kotani and established the Dai Nippon Risshō Kōseikai. From 1942, Niwano served as president and Naganuma as vice-president, devoting themselves to missionary work.

Under the U.S. Occupation, the organization engaged in active propagation, and by the 1950s, it had grown into one of the leading Nichiren-based new religions in eastern Japan, second only to Reiyūkai. Risshō Kōseikai’s doctrine inherited Reiyūkai’s emphasis on a combination of Nichiren Buddhism and ancestor veneration, but it also developed a unique theological direction by emphasizing the perfection of individual character. Centered on the merits of the Lotus Sutra, its teachings explain karma and fate through a doctrine called myō–tai–shin, and incorporate diverse elements from popular religion such as esoteric syllables (kuji), the six-day calendar (rokuyō), seven deities, kigaku (a type of divination), and seimeigaku (name analysis) (Murakami, 34–35).

The group’s missionary method relied heavily on small group sessions called hōza (Dharma circles), used for persuasion, personal testimonials, and study. These proved highly effective in expanding its membership. It also promoted personal character development and the restoration of human dignity in response to the breakdown of the traditional family system. Although the group was proactive in modernizing Buddhism, it maintained an overall conservative tone.

Risshō Kōseikai’s household membership grew rapidly: from 1,277 in 1945, to 17,983 in 1948, then to 133,232 in 1952, and reaching 402,238 households by 1959 (Shimada, 136).

From the 1960s onward, the group promoted the slogan “Faith for All Citizens, Religious Cooperation” and engaged in social initiatives such as the Movement to Create a Brighter Society. Internationally, it became a major organization within the World Conference of Religions for Peace (WCRP) and emerged as a leading voice in religious peace movements and the broader world of new religions (Murakami, 35).
