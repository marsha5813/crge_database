Furuya Yasuo (1926–2018)

Furuya Yasuo was a Japanese theologian and pastor, known internationally for his role in introducing Japanese Christianity to the global stage. Although aligned with the ecumenical movement, he also showed a degree of understanding toward Evangelicals, and critically examined the nature of Christianity in Japan from a broad perspective. His writing career was wide-ranging, beginning with Christian America (1967) and extending to Is Christianity in Japan Genuine? (2011), throughout which he persistently offered sharp critiques and hopeful visions for both the Japanese church and society. His contributions can be categorized into three fields: theological journalism, the theology of religion, and Japanese theology (Aizawa, 2019).

At the heart of Furuya’s thought was the question: “Why hasn’t Christianity spread in Japan?” As key factors, he identified external obstacles, such as the imperial institution symbolized by the emperor system, and internal issues like the divisions and conflicts within the church. He argued that the unity of Protestant denominations, the Catholic Church, and the ecumenical movement was essential for increasing the Christian population in Japan (Furuya, 2014).

Furuya was drafted at the age of 18 during the war, and based on this personal experience, he explained that Japanese Christians compromised with militarism because they lacked the option of conscientious objection (CO) (Furuya 2009, pp. 182–184). He also criticized Japanese churches for losing their identity between the “Kingdom of God” and “Divine Nation Japan,” and for using Karl Barth’s theology as a “cloak” to justify wartime cooperation (Furuya 2009, p. 96). In this context, Furuya stressed the importance of both the church-based and socially engaged factions of Christianity sharing a common vision rooted in the “Kingdom of God” when addressing church–state relations (Furuya 2009, pp. 190–191).

From a similar viewpoint, he emphasized the significance of Kagawa Toyohiko as a model of evangelism that could reach the masses—especially workers (Furuya 2009, pp. 91–123).

　

  • Furuya Yasuo/ 古屋安雄（1926–2018）

古屋は、日本の神学者・牧師であり、日本のキリスト教を国際的に紹介した人物としても知られる。エキュメニカル派に属しつつも、福音派にも一定の理解を示すなど、幅広い立場から日本のキリスト教の在り方を批判的に問うた。著作活動は多岐にわたり、『キリスト教国アメリカ』（1967）に始まり、『日本のキリスト教は本物か？』（2011）に至るまで、日本の教会と社会への鋭い批判と希望を発信し続けた。古屋の業績は、神学的ジャーナリズム、宗教の神学、日本の神学という三領域に分類できる（相澤, 2019）。

　古屋の関心の中心には「なぜ日本にキリスト教は広まらないのか」という問いがあった。その要因として古屋は、外的には天皇制に象徴される皇室の存在、内的には教会の分裂・対立の問題を課題として挙げ、日本のプロテスタントの諸団体とカトリックとエキュメニカルの一致こそが、日本におけるキリスト教人口の拡大に繋がると主張した（古屋, 2014）。

　古屋は戦時中に18歳で召集された経験を持っているが、こうした自身の経験から、日本のキリスト者が軍国主義と妥協した理由を、良心的兵役拒否（CO）という選択肢がなかったためであったと説明している（古屋2009、182-184）。また、古屋は日本の教会が「神の国」と「神国日本」の間でアイデンティティを見失い、バルト神学を「隠れ蓑」にして戦争協力したことも批判している（古屋2009、96）。この文脈で古屋は、分裂・対立した教会派と社会派のそれぞれが、教会と国家の問題を考える際に、「神の国」という共通の視座を持つことの重要性を説いている（古屋2009、190-191）。また、同様の観点から、大衆（特に労働者）に届く伝道のあり方として賀川豊彦の重要性を強調した（古屋2009、91–123）。

Tuneaki Kato (1929–2024)

Tuneaki Kato was one of Japan’s most prominent theologians and preachers. Born in Harbin, China, he was baptized at the age of 13 after being deeply moved by a sermon by Tadao Yanaihara. Disillusioned by the decline of the church and preaching in postwar Japan, he later encountered the sermons of Masaichi Takemori, which rekindled his faith.

He graduated from the Department of Philosophy, Faculty of Letters at the University of Tokyo in 1953, and completed his studies at Tokyo Union Theological Seminary in 1956. He subsequently taught practical theology at the same seminary until 1986. He also served as a visiting professor at Heidelberg University and as president of the International Academy of Homiletics.

Placing great importance on “pointing to a living Christianity,” Kato founded the “Sermon Workshop” (説教塾) in 1987. Its aim was to promote the development of homiletics in Japan and to raise the quality of preaching practice through voluntary and sustained collaborative learning. According to the Sermon Workshop Booklet, its purpose was “to reform the church through preaching as a breakthrough and to overcome the stagnation of evangelism” (Kato, Sermon Workshop Booklet, p. 2). The direct inspiration for its establishment came from Kato’s participation in the 600th anniversary international homiletics symposium at Heidelberg University in 1986. His dialogue with preachers from around the world convinced him of the need for a movement to shake up rigid preaching practices, and upon returning to Japan, he also raised concerns about the “professionalization” of pastors and churches (Hirano, pp. 121–136).

From 1969 to 1997, Kato pastored Yukinoshita Church in Kamakura for 28 years. When he began, the church was in a state of division, but he preached the “essence of the gospel,” and by the time he retired, attendance at worship services had grown to about 400 people. Commenting on Kato’s role in navigating denominational conflicts as a preacher, Hirano described him as a person shaped by a “delicate spirit, a strong ego, a profound awareness of himself as a sinner, and a proud belief in being the foremost adult in Japan”—a unique blend of contrasting traits that defined Kato’s character (Hirano, p. 4).

Tuneaki Kato/ 加藤常昭 (1929–2024）

　加藤は、日本を代表する神学者・説教者である。中国のハルピンに生まれ、13歳のときに矢内原忠雄の説教に感銘を受けて受洗。戦後の教会・説教の堕落に失望する中で、竹森満佐一の説教に触れ、再び信仰に目覚めた。1953年に東京大学文学部哲学科を卒業し、1956年には東京神学大学を修了。その後、1986年まで同大学で実践神学を教え、ハイデルベルク大学の客員教授や国際説教学会会長も務めた。

　「生きたキリスト教を指し示す」ことに重きを置いた加藤は、1987年に「説教塾」を創設。日本における説教学の発展と、説教実践の質的向上を目指して、自主的かつ継続的な共同学習の場を築いた。『説教塾ブックレット』によれば、その目的は「説教を突破口に、教会を改革し、伝道の低迷を打破しよう」というものであった（Kato, 『説教塾ブックレット』, 2）。設立の直接的な契機となったのは、加藤が1986年にハイデルベルク大学創立600年記念の国際説教学シンポジウムに出席した経験である。各国から集まった説教者たちとの対話を通じて、加藤は説教の固定化を揺り動かすような運動の必要性を痛感し、帰国後には、牧師や教会の「職業化」にも問題を提起した（Hirano, 121–136）。

　　1969年から1997年までの28年間、加藤が牧会した鎌倉雪ノ下教会では、就任当初に分裂状態にあった教会に「福音の真髄」を説き、退任時には礼拝参加者が約400人にまで増加した。平野は、教団紛争の道のりを伝道者として歩んだ加藤について、「繊細な精神と強烈な自我、そして、罪人としての自覚と日本第一の大人であるという自負。相反する独特な個性が加藤を造り上げてきた」と評している（Hirano, .4）。

Nobuo Watanabe (1923.5.5–2020.3.27)

Nobuo Watanabe was a Japanese theologian and pastor, best known as the translator of John Calvin’s Institutes of the Christian Religion into Japanese.

Born into a Christian home in Osaka, Watanabe graduated from the Department of Philosophy, Faculty of Letters at Kyoto University in 1949, and later received his doctorate in 1977 for a dissertation on Calvin’s ecclesiology. Beginning his ministry in 1949, he went on to start pioneering evangelism in 1958, eventually founding the Tokyo Confession Church of the Church of Christ in Japan (Nihon Kirisuto Kyōkai).

Watanabe persistently addressed the issue of war responsibility in Evangelical settings, including the Japan Evangelical Association (JEA) and various other gatherings. His advocacy had a significant impact, leading to the organization of a major event in August 1995, commemorating 50 years after the war:

“The Confession of Guilt 50 Years After the War — A Confession from All Japanese Christians to Asia,” held at the Ochanomizu Christian Center, with Watanabe as a central initiator. The event drew many Christians from across Japan, including a large number from Evangelical churches (Nakamura, 239).

Beginning in 1993, Shinji Yūki, pastor of Ueno-machi Church of the Japan Alliance Christ Church in Yokohama, and Watanabe co-led a continuing effort to re-examine the histories of Japan and Asia from the perspective of war responsibility, and to explore new paths for mission. This initiative took the form of an annual summer seminar in Maruko Town, Nagano Prefecture, titled the Shinshū Summer Mission Lecture Series (Nakamura, 240).

Nobuo Watanabe/ 渡辺信夫 (1923.5.5-2020.3.27)

渡辺は日本の神学者、牧師であり、ジャン・カルバンの『キリスト教綱要』の翻訳者でもある。

大阪出身の渡辺は、クリスチャンホームに生まれた。1949年に京都大学文学部哲学科を卒業し、1977年に「カルバンの教会論」について執筆した論文で博士号を取得した。同時に1949年から伝道者となり、1958年には開拓伝道を始め日本キリスト教会東京告白教会を形成した。

　渡辺は日本福音同盟をはじめ福音派の諸集会で戦争責任の問題を一貫して追求し、発言してきた。これは大きな影響を与え、1995年8月に彼を中心とする呼びかけ人によって「戦後50年罪責告白集会─全日本キリスト者の告白をアジアに向けて」という集会がお茶の水クリスチャンセンターで開催された。この集会には日本全国から多くのクリスチャンが参加し、福音派に所属する人も多かった（Nakamura, 239)　さらに1993年から日本同盟基督教団横浜上野町教会牧師の結城晋次、渡辺信夫を中心に戦争責任の視点から日本やアジアの歴史を再検討し、新たな宣教の道を探る会が長野県の丸子町で毎夏、「信州夏期宣教講座」として開催された（Nakamura, 240)

Nippon Revival Association (NRA)

日本リバイバル同盟

Founded in May 1996, the Nippon Revival Association (NRA) was the first nationwide organization established with the goal of bringing together churches aligned with Pentecostalism, the Charismatic movement, the Third Wave of the Holy Spirit, and power evangelism (Nakamura, 243). Minoru Okuyama was selected as the association’s first chairperson, and the organization aimed to contribute to revival in Japan.

The NRA has been positioned as a third major force in Japanese Christianity, alongside and in contrast to both the National Christian Council in Japan (NCCJ) and the Japan Evangelical Association (JEA) (Nakamura, 243).

The Expansion of Evangelicalism

In Protestant churches, the influence of traditional denominations declined by the mid-Shōwa period, while Evangelical organizations increased in number and strength (Shimazono, 21). Globally, Evangelical movements that emphasized faithfulness to the Bible, preached the nearness of the end times, and advocated healing through the Holy Spirit were on the rise, replacing the increasingly liberalized old mainline denominations (Shimazono, 21).

In the 1990s, Evangelical groups in Japan held large-scale gatherings such as the Kōshien Revival Mission and the Billy Graham Tokyo Crusade. A notable feature of this period was the rise of discipleship-oriented churches, influenced by American small group ministries and pastoral training programs imported from South Korea (Nakamura, 235–236).

From November 5 to 7, 1993, the All-Japan Revival Mission was held at Hanshin Kōshien Stadium in Nishinomiya, Hyōgo Prefecture. This event was organized by the Japan Revival Crusade (Nakamura, 237). Aimed at the revival of the Japanese church, it featured a wide range of programs, including a mission congress, and invited speakers from both Japan and abroad. The total attendance reached 117,000 people (Nakamura, 237).

This gathering was groundbreaking in both scale and format, especially as it was not centered around a denomination, but rather organized by individuals and churches who supported its aims. Across Japan, preparatory rallies were held in advance of the main event to build momentum (Nakamura, 237). Notably, revival missions continued across Japan even after the event, including the Tokyo Revival Mission, which was held at the Nippon Budōkan in September 1998. At the center of this movement was Shinshiro Church, led by Pastor Akira Takimoto, which hosted the headquarters and launched the Japan Revival Seminary. The group also distributed magazines, books, and tapes, and became a major force within the Japanese church (Nakamura, 237).

Additionally, from January 13 to 16, 1994, the Billy Graham Tokyo Crusade was held at the Tokyo Dome. Unlike the Kōshien Revival Mission, this event was spearheaded by 120 cooperating churches in the Tokyo metropolitan area and featured the globally renowned evangelist Billy Graham as its central speaker (Nakamura, 238).

Over four days, the event drew a total of 130,000 participants. Approximately 52,000 individuals responded to Graham’s invitation to commit themselves, and around 12,000 people filled out decision cards (Nakamura, 238). The meetings were also broadcast via satellite to various venues in major cities across Japan, allowing simultaneous participation through screens—an innovative use of media that extended the reach of the revival nationwide (Nakamura, 238).

  1. The New Interconfessional Translation Bible (Shin Kyōdō Yaku Seisho, "New Joint Translation Bible")" was published, in 1987

In September 1987, after 18 years of work, the "The New Interconfessional Translation Bible" was completed and published by the Japan Bible Society, hoping to unite the Catholic Church and Protestant denominations in Japan. The creation of the New Common Translation began in 1968 and was produced by a total of 76 translators, including biblical scholars, literary scholars, and linguists (The Christ Weekly, 123)

This Bible has two distinctive features: first, it is a "common translation" of the Roman Catholic Church and Protestant churches; second, its translation philosophy. Regarding the translation itself, the "New Testament Common Translation" was completed in 1978, but this version differed from the 1978 version in terms of editing and translation principles. The 1978 version of the "Common Translation" was translated based on the translation theory known as "dynamic equivalence," which was influential worldwide at the time. This approach aimed to capture the meaning of the language accurately, employing a translation method that prioritized interpretation over literal translation. This translation was done to make it accessible to people outside the church. However, it received criticism for being too new in its notation and terminology and for deviating significantly from the original text. For example, proper names and place names were rendered too faithfully to the original language's pronunciation, such as "Lukas" instead of the established "Luka," which was unfamiliar and awkward (The Christ Weekly, 123).

Subsequently, reflecting on the idea that "the Bible should spread gradually to more people through its use in the church," the translation philosophy was revised (The Christ Weekly, 123). In the "New Common Translation," the formal correspondence method was adopted instead of the free translation method (Kotomo, 2).

As a result of this translation, all Christians and all Japanese people could possess the Bible for the first time. In 1987, the first Bible in Japan, the "Gullough Translation," was published, marking the 150th anniversary of the first Bible translation and the 100th anniversary of the publication of the "Literary Translation."

Aum Shinrikyō

(Aum Supreme Truth)

Aum Shinrikyō originated when Shoko Asahara (real name: Chizuo Matsumoto, 1955–2018) founded the Aum Shinsen no Kai (Aum Society of Hermits) on February 14, 1984, and opened a yoga dojo in Shibuya.

From its founding through its recognition as a religious corporation to its eventual collapse, Aum’s trajectory lasted approximately ten years. During this short period, the group rapidly expanded, reportedly gathering 10,000 to 14,000 lay followers in Japan and an even larger following of 30,000 in Russia. Within the group, VX gas, sarin, hallucinogens, and other chemical agents were produced. In the name of preserving the organization, Aum murdered dissenting members, anti-Aum activists, and even a lawyer’s family who had supported defectors. The group eventually transformed into a terrorist organization that carried out indiscriminate mass murder using sarin gas.

Followers justified murder through the concept of “poa”, which interpreted killing as a form of spiritual training accompanied by suffering. Even indiscriminate mass killings were understood as trials to be spiritually overcome. Asahara was indicted in connection with 17 incidents, including the sarin gas attack, and in 2006, the Supreme Court finalized his death sentence. In July 2018, Asahara and 12 former senior members were executed.

Japan’s Public Security Intelligence Agency (PSIA)—which identifies Aum members as “constituents” of the group—explains the group’s rapid expansion and transformation into a cult as follows:

“Aum Shinsen no Kai” took advantage of the occult boom of the time, promoting itself with images claiming to show feats like levitation, which were sold to magazines, thereby increasing its membership. Later, the group introduced a monastic ordination system, engaged in proselytizing, sold Asahara’s books, and opened new branches. In July 1987, the group changed its name to Aum Shinrikyō, and Asahara’s title evolved from “Asahara-sensei” or “Asahara Daishi” to simply “Guru”, reflecting the growing absolutism of his authority.

The organization continued to expand, promoting its “Japan Shambhala Project”—a utopian plan to create a “Lotus Village” where members could live according to spiritual truth. It constructed large monastic and residential complexes called “Satyans” in Shizuoka and Yamanashi Prefectures, and secured more than 30 facilities across Japan and overseas. By March 1993, its membership had reached approximately 10,000.

Scholar Shimada argues that the growth of Aum’s membership paralleled the expansion of Japan’s bubble economy. Aum’s training combined yoga and elements of Tibetan esoteric Buddhism, which attracted young people drawn to rigorous spiritual practice. Many of these followers were elite university graduates who felt alienated by the materialism of the bubble era. Aum offered them what seemed like a spiritually meaningful way of life (Shimada).

The PSIA also explains the group’s radicalization and eventual execution of mass violence. Following a crushing defeat in the February 1990 general election (all 25 Aum candidates, including Asahara, lost), the group became increasingly anti-social. Shimada notes that while Asahara’s candidacy may have been impulsive, the defeat itself did not directly cause militarization (Shimada).

However, the group soon shifted from seeking political influence to pursuing its goals through armed means, and constructed large-scale facilities to produce chemical weapons such as phosgene, VX gas, and sarin, as well as automatic rifles. They began research, development, and mass production, accelerating a campaign of militarization on a significant scale.

Thirty Years After the Dissolution: Aum Shinrikyō’s Successor Groups in 2025

As of 2025, thirty years after the dissolution of Aum Shinrikyō, three groups believed to be its successors remain active, all under surveillance by Japan’s Public Security Intelligence Agency (PSIA).

The PSIA website explains their continued monitoring as follows:

“Aum Shinrikyō continues to operate today under different names, while maintaining its dangerous organizational character. Since February 2000, the PSIA has conducted surveillance under the provisions of the Act on the Control of Organizations That Have Committed Acts of Indiscriminate Mass Murder (the Group Regulation Law), including the collection of reports on organizational activities and inspections of facilities.”

Among these successor groups, the PSIA identifies two as the main factions:

• Aleph, and

• the Yamada Faction (Yamada-ra no Shūdan).

Both are reported to show absolute devotion to Asahara (Shoko Asahara).

The third group is Hikari no Wa (“The Circle of Light”), led by Fumihiro Joyu, a former senior member of Aum and once the leader of Aleph. Although this group appears to have distanced itself from Asahara’s influence, it is said to still uphold the foundational or essential elements of Asahara’s teachings and activities.

These groups have increasingly drawn in younger generations unfamiliar with the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack, and their recruitment of new followers has become a serious social concern.

Meanwhile, numerous unresolved issues persist, including:

• compensation for victims who still suffer aftereffects from incidents such as the VX gas assassination attempt, the Matsumoto sarin attack, and the Tokyo subway sarin attack,

• claims by Asahara’s family regarding the return of his remains, and

• the support and reintegration of former members who have left the group.

To combat societal forgetfulness of the Aum incidents, the PSIA continues to update its website with:

• records of Aum’s terrorist acts, and

• monitoring reports on the successor groups.

Jehovah’s Witnesses (エホバの証人)

Jehovah’s Witnesses is a religious movement that began in the United States in the 1870s, founded by Charles Taze Russell (February 16, 1852 – October 31, 1916).

In Japan, the movement was introduced by Junzo Akashi, who opposed the war as a Christian during World War II and was imprisoned for his beliefs.

After the war, foreign missionaries arrived to lead the movement in Akashi’s place, but as of 1951, the number of believers was still only in the hundreds, mainly in urban areas. However, from the 1970s onward, the movement saw remarkable growth in Japan. Starting in 1975, Jehovah’s Witnesses expanded rapidly across the country, establishing “congregations” and “Kingdom Halls” in many regions.

Members who actively engage in evangelism are called “publishers,” and evangelism is considered a religious duty. The number of publishers in Japan rose dramatically:

• 3,693 in 1965,

• 30,294 in 1975,

• 97,822 in 1985,

• 165,823 in 1992, and

• 221,364 in 2000 (Shimazono, 281).

In 1975, the group predicted the coming of Armageddon and the end of the world, which led to a surge in new believers. Although the prophecy failed to materialize and membership declined globally, Japan was an exception, where the number of believers continued to grow.

In Japan, 60% of the “pioneers” (full-time evangelists) were housewives, and often their husbands were not members. Their focus tended to be on issues like childrearing and family, and end-times prophecy did not receive much attention (Yamaguchi). The large presence of housewives contributed to the continued expansion of Jehovah’s Witnesses in Japan after 1975, and more Kingdom Halls and congregations were established throughout the country.

On the other hand, the movement has faced legal and social controversies. In 1985, in Kawasaki, a tragic incident occurred in which the parents, who were Jehovah’s Witnesses, refused a blood transfusion for their son injured in a traffic accident, resulting in his death. The court ultimately prioritized religious freedom, and the doctor who administered the transfusion lost the case.

Jehovah’s Witnesses in Japan refuse not only blood transfusions, but also military service, martial arts, saluting the national flag, singing the national anthem, and voting in elections.

In 1990, a student refused to participate in kendo (Japanese fencing) classes based on Isaiah 2:4, which speaks of turning swords into plowshares. The student was expelled and denied entry to further education, but filed a lawsuit against the school. Although the Constitution guarantees freedom of religion, the court upheld the school’s actions, and the ruling was later affirmed by the High Court and the Supreme Court.

Happy Science (Kōfuku no Kagaku)

Happy Science is a religious organization founded by Ryuho Okawa in 1986. The group experienced rapid growth, claiming a membership of over 70,000 in the 1990s, and 1.52 million by 1991, the year it was officially recognized as a religious corporation.

Among the group’s leaders and followers are many public figures and professionals active in top sectors of society, including corporate employees, bureaucrats from the Ministry of International Trade and Industry, and economic commentators. Their focus lies in how to achieve success in society, which marks a sharp contrast with earlier new religions in Japan, where most believers came from ordinary backgrounds, and joined in response to life struggles such as poverty, illness, or family disputes.

Happy Science stood out for its flashy public activities, such as holding mass events at the Tokyo Dome, challenging the traditional image of new religions in Japan. Its rapid and conspicuous expansion as a new type of religious organization triggered public suspicion and criticism from the media, to which the organization frequently responded with lawsuits.

Happy Science teaches faith in “El Cantare” as its central doctrine. El Cantare is described as the “Supreme God of Earth” and literally means “The Light of the Earth.” According to its teachings, El Cantare has guided various civilizations and religions throughout history under different names, including:

• “The Lord God” that Jesus referred to as the “Father” in Christianity

• “Elohim”, who delivered revelations to prophets in Judaism

• “Allah”, who revealed the Qur’an to Muhammad in Islam

• “Tentei” (Heavenly Emperor), as spoken of by Confucius in China

• The eternal and original Buddha in Buddhism

• And “Ame-no-Mioya-no-Kami”, the primordial deity from pre-Kojiki Shinto in Japan

Happy Science regards all of these as manifestations of the same being, El Cantare, who is said to guide all deities on Earth and aims to bring happiness to all living beings on the planet (as stated on the official website).

A unique feature of the group is its use of “reigen” (spirit words)—a practice in which Ryuho Okawa uses his advanced spiritual abilities to summon spirits of the deceased or cosmic entities and delivers their messages verbally. This is regarded as a defining characteristic of Happy Science (official website).

Educational and Political Activities:

In 2010, Happy Science opened a fully residential middle and high school in Nasu, Tochigi Prefecture, known as the Happy Science Academy. A second school opened in Ōtsu, Shiga Prefecture, in 2013. Although the organization applied in 2014 to establish a university, it failed to obtain official accreditation, and since 2015, it has operated as a private, unaccredited institution under the name Happy Science University.

In the political sphere, the group founded the Happiness Realization Party (Kōfuku Jitsugen-tō) in 2009 to contest national elections, but has not succeeded in electing any candidates.

Murders Committed by Aum Shinrikyō

In 1994, members of Aum Shinrikyō carried out the Matsumoto sarin attack, killing eight people and causing chemical poisoning in 140 others. In 1995, they conducted another sarin gas attack on the Tokyo subway system, killing 13 people and poisoning approximately 6,000 others.

Following these acts of indiscriminate mass murder, Aum leader Shoko Asahara and senior members of the group, along with believers who participated in the crimes, were arrested in 1995, and the group’s status as a religious corporation was revoked.

The Public Security Intelligence Agency (PSIA) describes Aum’s radicalization and execution of mass violence as follows:

After the group’s crushing defeat in the February 1990 general election (in which all 25 candidates, including Asahara, lost), Aum began exhibiting increasingly anti-social tendencies.

It shifted its strategy from achieving its aims through political power to pursuing them through armed force, constructing large-scale facilities to produce chemical weapons such as phosgene, VX gas, and sarin, as well as automatic rifles. The group then commenced research, development, and production of these weapons, aggressively pursuing militarization.

Around March 1990, based on Asahara’s teachings on “world domination through armed force,” the group also began research and development of biological weapons, including botulinum toxin and anthrax, with the intent of eliminating and destroying all forces that posed an obstacle to its goals.
