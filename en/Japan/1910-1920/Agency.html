Japan: Agency (1910-1920)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Yoshino Sakuzō(吉野作造, 1878-1933)

Yoshino Sakuzō(吉野作造, 1878-1933) was a Christian politician and educator who played a prominent role in advocating for the advancement of democracy in Japan during the early 20th century. Yoshino attended Baptist missionary Anny Syrena Buzzell's bible class under the old education system in his high school. Many young people who participated in this class became the bearers of Taisho democracy (Tazawa, 30-31). He was baptized in 1898 and soon became a key person in the Christian Socialist movement. After entering Tokyo Imperial University in 1900, he attended the Hongo Church of Ebina Danjo. Hongo Church was a Congregation church in Japan headed by Ebina Danjo. Then, Yoshino was hugely influenced by Ebina.
From January 1910, Yoshino studied in Germany, England, and the United States. Yoshino used the YMCA network to visit various cities and universities during his study abroad. This suggests that the international Christian network was important for him to study foreign countries and research other countries' religions and democratic systems.（Ōta, 79）Since about 1915, Yoshino claimed a “people's democracy” (Minponshugi, 民本主義), emphasizing that the main goal of governance should be to listen to and consider the opinions of the people.. To achieve this end, he tried to promote universal suffrage, civilian oversight of the military, and the gradual establishment of a socialist state. Minponism, a combination of Christian Socialism, trade unionism, and Confucian morality, was one of the popular and influential political thoughts in the 1910s. His appointment as president of the Tokyo Imperial University YMCA in March 1917 shows that he was at the center of Christian activities at the Imperial University. In the 1920s, however, it failed to influence further due in part to changes in people's political consciousness under the influence of Marxism (Ōta, 67).

  1. Yamamuro, Gunpei/山室軍平(1872.8.20-1940.3.13)

Yamamuro Gunpei was the first Japanese Salvation Army officer.When he was young, he came to Tokyo from Okayama to study. In 1887, he attended the evangelical association "Tsukiji Evangelical Church" in Tsukiji, Tokyo, then studied at a seminary (missionary school) and was baptized there. He entered Doshisha in 1889 and got interested in social work. As a Christian, Yamamuro had been interested in social issues, poverty, and the reformed movement since his days at Doshisha. This interest in social issues was also the basis for his enlistment in the Salvation Army and his lifelong thought. (Murota, 40) He could not graduate from Doshisha but worked for social problems and joined the Salvation Army in December 1895. Yamamuro actively addressed social issues in Japan. In 1900, he advocated for the voluntary cessation of prostitution, and in 1906, he established a labor referral center to solve the economic downturn after the Russo-Japanese War.
In 1916, he initiated a tuberculosis control effort by founding a sanatorium, and in 1922, he launched a campaign to prevent child abuse. At the time, his activities paved a way to address some of the ongoing social problems such as the prohibition of prostitution, aiding farmers, rescuing and supporting victims of human trafficking after the Great Kanto Earthquake, and safeguarding human rights.Around 1918, the term "social work (Shakai undo)" began to be commonly used in place of the word "relief (Kyusai), "and this period was called the "period of the establishment of relief work" in Japan (Murota, 170). The central idea of Yamamuro was based on Christianity and the Bible, and the idea of transforming people at the bottom of society, thereby enabling further social transformation, was carried through. (Murota, 180-181) In contrast, most of the Protestant churches in Japan were not interested in alleviating the existing social problems. Therefore, Yamamuro’s social activism provided one of the important examples of what Christian social work can do for society (Akimoto, 1444-1445).

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

  1. Nihon Kirisuto Kumiai kyokai/ 日本基督教組合教会（Congligation Church)

The Nihon Kumiai Kirisuto Kyokai (Japan Congregational Church) was founded in 1886, mainly by missionaries from the American Board(Congregational). Doshisha served as a training institute for this denomination, and graduates such as Ebina Danjo, Ozaki Hiromichi, and Miyagawa Keiki later became active as Christian leaders. Their evangelism took place mainly in large cities, and until the 1940s, it was one of the three major Protestant denominations in Japan (Takahashi, 1951-1952). Korean evangelism by Kumiai kyokai had been conducted for the Japanese since before 1910. After Korea was under Japanese rule, the Kumiai kyokai established a Korean mission department and began evangelizing Koreans (Nakamura, 29). The person in charge of the Korean mission of the Kumiai kyokai was Tsunekichi Watase（渡瀬恒吉), who Danjo Ebina influenced. He had worked at the Gyeongseong School(京城学堂） of the Dainippon Overseas Educational Association, an educational institution aimed at reconciliation between Japan and Korea, since 1899. In addition, he had experience working in Korea (Nakamura, 30). In 1913, he published a pamphlet entitled “Chosen kyoka no kyumu(『朝鮮教化の急務』),” in which he justified the annexation of Korea and further argued that the Korean people needed not only political and economic support but also spiritual inspiration through religion. Watase's insistence on evangelizing Korea was integrated with the Japanese government's colonial policy (Nakamura, 32). The Kumiai kyokai established churches in Gyeongseong and Pyongyang in 1911 and subsequently showed tremendous growth.

By 1918, the Chosen Kumiai kyokai had grown rapidly to 149 member churches and 13,631 congregations. During this period, the Japanese Kumiai kyokai showed no significant growth, with the number of churches growing from 102 to 113 and the number of congregations from 16,630 to 20,427. Behind this growth in Korea, support from Japanese financial circles and intervention by the Office of the Governor-General of Korea were noted.

In 1914, the Kumiai kyokai's Korean mission fund was 38,000 yen, more than double the annual budget of the Kumiai kyokai in Japan (Nakamura, 34). Since 10,000 yen at that time would be worth about 100 million yen today, a considerable amount of money was allocated for the Korean mission. The funds were provided by Shigenobu Okuma and other government officials who lobbied Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and other financial cliques(zaibatsu), and the Office of the Governor-General of Korea also paid a large amount of money anonymously from confidential funds (Nakamura, 34). Furthermore, it is believed that the intervention of the Office of the Governor-General of Korea was behind the large number of people joining the Kumiai churches (Nakamura, 35). It is highly likely that participation in the Japanese Kumiai church, rather than Western Christianity, was being promoted in Korea. In addition, the leap of the union church in Korea was seen as a result of the connection between the Governor-General of Korea and the Kumiai church. In other words, after under the Japan of Korea in 1910, the the Governor-General of Korea, who was struggling with colonial rule, welcomed the mission of the Kumiai churches advocating assimilation policies and expected the mission of the Kumiai churches to the Korean people to play a role as a pacifying agent to facilitate colonial rule. Therefore, the Governor-General and the financial cliques provided tremendous assistance for Kumiai church (Nakamura, 35-36).

  1. Sophia University/上智大学

Sophia University is the first Catholic institution of higher education in Japan. The school was founded by the Jesuits in 1913. The Society of Foreign Missions of Paris mainly conducted Catholic missions in Japan, targeting the lower middle class. However, Catholic missions stagnated from the 1890s and reached an impasse in the early 20th century. They plan to establish Catholic higher education institutions to break through these deadlocks. Catholic leaders thought that (1) the image of the Catholic Church in Japan was not good (2) missionary methods were not adapted to the conditions of Japanese society, and (3) a lack of human and financial resources was behind the missionary slump. Therefore, they decided to make a new effort: the arrival of Jesuits in Japan and establishing an institution of higher learning by them (Miyoshi, 84-88). The papacy changed its policy on the Japanese mission, citing problems with the sole pastoral system by the Paris Foreign Mission and the lack of influence of French missionaries in the upper echelons of society. It was demonstrated through the multinationalization of missionary organizations and the establishment of higher education institutions (Miyoshi, 94). After 1904, various missionary groups came to Japan, including religious orders that focused on educational projects.

In October 1908, three Jesuits arrived in Japan, and Sophia University was established in 1913. At first, even though it was named “university,” it was legally classified as a “technical college. In 1918, the Japanese government issued a “University Ordinance,” in 1928, Sophia University was officially approved as a university (Miyoshi96). Initially, the university had three departments: Philosophy, German Literature, and Commerce. The fact that foreign languages were English and German only, with no French at all, clearly shows the intent of de-France in university education (Miyoshi96). 16 students were enrolled in the first year in 1913, 91 in 1917, and 146 in 1920, making it a Catholic higher education institution in Japan. In 1917, there were 91 students enrolled, and in 1920, there were 146 students enrolled, making it a Catholic institution of higher education in Japan (上智大学史資料集編纂委員会編, 1908, 47). Also, the establishment of the Department of Commercial Science attracted many students in response to the times' needs.

Furthermore, Sophia University, as the center of intellectual activities of the Catholic Church in Japan, established the Kirisitan Library（キリシタン文庫) in 1932 and launched the international academic journal Monumenta Nipponica (1938) to promote Japanese culture abroad. In addition, they also published the first volume of the Dictionary of Catholicism (the first volume was published in 1940). (Miyoshi, 96-97)。

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. From 1910 to 1920, Japan colonized Korea and entered World War I as a member of the Allied side. Therefore、 This period was referred to as the formation of "Christianity in the Japanese Empire (ソ・ジョンミン). Compared to the Meiji period, this period was also characterized by the emergence of Christians with individualistic and liberal tendencies. In addition, against the backdrop of a worldwide movement, some Christians became involved in cooperative activities, social issues, and labor movements in a para-denominational manner (Takahashi, 337).There were three pro-Christian movements during this period.

Meeting of the Three Regirions (Sankyo Kaido, 三教会同) The "Meeting of the Three Religions (Sankyō Kaidō, 三教会同)" was devised by Tokonami Takejirō (床次竹次郎） in February 1912 and arranged by the Home Ministry. It was an attempt by the government to become more actively involved in the "leadership" of the mainstream religious groups: Shinto, Buddhism, and Christianity (Shield, 163). On February 25, 1912, fifty-one representatives of Buddhism, thirteen representatives of Shinto, and seven representatives of Christians gathered at the Rokumeikan Building in Tokyo.

During the meeting, the attendees discussed various issues about doctrine, morality, and the campaign of national welfare. They jointly issued the following "resolution" on the 28th, the event's last day. “Demonstrating each and every one of our doctrines, we pledge to assist the fortunes of the Imperial Household (nōun 皇運) and increasingly promote the national morality (kokumin) ... We call for respect towards religion, a harmonization of politics, religion and education, and for everyone to play a part in expanding our national destiny (koku’un 国運)(Shield, 164). Mainstream Christian denominations welcomed the government's treatment of Christianity as equal to other religions and actively participated. However, Kashiwagi Gien, Uchimura Kanzo, and others expressed reluctance or active opposition to this association. ( Dohi, 589) At this time, Shinto shrines were considered non-religious, and thus, Shrine Shinto was not invited. However, besides political and social recognition, it was also significant that Christianity stood on equal footing with Buddhist and Shinto leaders. (Kiga, 244)　

（２）The National Evangelical movement or The Nationwide Co-operative Campain of Evangelism ( Zenkoku kyodo dendo, 全国協同伝道)

National cooperative evangelism was a joint effort by Japanese denominations from 1914 to 1917. The World Missionary Conference was held in Edinburgh in June 1910, with the participation of Honda Yoichi and others from Japan. This marked the beginning of the ecumenical movement, as the traditional closed evangelism system of individual churches was replaced by cooperative evangelism (Dohi, 240; Ochi, 231; Ochi, 232). In 1913, John R. Mott (1865-1955), president of the World Missionary Council, visited Japan and encouraged the ecumenical movement in Japan (Dohi, 240; Ouchi, 792). With Mott's visit to Japan, Japanese Christians organized a committee to continue the movement, with Kajinosuke Ibuka as a chairman, Masahisa Uemura representing the eastern side of Japan, and Kyoki Miyagawa representing the western side.

In this evangelistic movement, various methods were used, such as camp meetings, bicycle meetings, lectures, and conventions for girls students, office workers, educators, etc. The total number of meetings was 4,788, with 777,119 participants, 27,350 seekers, and more than 1 million tracts distributed (Ouchi, 792). Furthermore, due in part to the influence of the Taisho Democracy, the number of churches increased from 586 in 1910, including various denominations, to 1,056 in 1915 and to approximately 1,500 in 1920, tripling in 10 years, and the number of believers increased to 164,500 (Ouchi, 792). The following are three characteristics of this movement.

First, this mission was the first movement of the Christian Church in Japan to work with international organizations overseas. This helped solidify cooperation with foreign missions and shift the focus from independence during the Meiji period to domestic and international cooperation. Second, they conducted activities to use the mass media, especially newspapers. At the time, newspapers were becoming a means of information, and the church used them to evangelize. In addition, they also strengthened their ties with politicians, thus gaining greater access to the ruling class. Third, Masahisa Uemura was in charge of this evangelism. Although he had criticized cooperative evangelism by other denominations during the 20th-century Taikyo evangelism(「20世紀大挙伝道」), he played a central role at this time (Dohi, 240-241).　

These activities later expanded to Korea, Taiwan, and China. Masahisa Uemura and Suroku Ehara conducted a Korean mission for Japanese people living in Korea. At Ehara's lecture, representatives of various Korean denominations were invited to a gathering of Japanese and Korean Christians(内鮮基督教親睦会). It was said that about 300 people attended this meeting. Later, evangelistic meetings were held in Lushun, Dalian, and Shanghai, and Danjo Ebina and his wife participated in the evangelism in Taiwan (three-year continuous nationwide cooperative evangelism).

　

(4)再臨運動

On January 6, 1918, Kanzo Uchimura gave the first lecture on the “Second Coming” or “Eschatology” at the Tokyo Christian Youth Hall under the title “The Second Coming of Christ from the Standpoint of a Bible Researcher(「聖書研究者の立場より見たる基督の再来」)." (Ouchi, 533). Shigeharu Nakada(中田重治), who started the Second Coming movement together with Uchimura, was the founder of the Holiness Church, and he advocated a “Fourfold gospel” (New birth and Sanctification through faith, Divine healing through God, and the second coming of Christ). Furthermore, Kiyomatsu Kimura(木村清松), a pastor of the Japanese Congregation Church, returned from the United States and spoke about the Second Coming movement as a notable trend in American Christianity when he visited Kanzo Uchimura (Suzuki, 2017, 221). Initially, Uchimura was not interested in the Second Coming movement, but he would later develop this movement himself.

　Uchimura's Second Coming movement was influenced by the death of his daughter Lutsu in January 1912 and World War I in 1914 (Ouchi, 535 : Suzuki, 2017, 222). These events changed Uchimura's sentiments and directed him toward the Second Coming movement. The outbreak of World War I and the entry of the United States into the war made Uchimura realize that this problem could only be solved by the Second Coming of Christ (Ouchi, 535). In addition, in 1916, his American friend John M. Bell sent him an article in The Sunday School Times discussing Charles G. Trumbull's "Is the Truth of Our Lord 's a Return a Practical Matter for Today" (Suzuki, 2017, 222). Uchimura had received this newspaper for a long time but temporarily refused to get it because it emphasized the Second Coming movement too much. Therefore, this article was not special; Uchimura's change of mind led him to strengthen his resolve for the Second Coming movement ((Suzuki, 2017, 222). The flourishing of pre-millennialism in the United States during the same period also impacted Uchimura living in Japan (Yakushige, 2018, 161-162).

Uchimura vigorously launched the Second Coming movement: in January 1918, he began a series of three Bible prophetic lecture speeches(予言的研究演説会) in Tokyo with Nakata and others; from March to July, he traveled back and forth between Osaka, Kyoto, Kobe, and Tokyo, giving lectures in Hokkaido and Tohoku. He lectured in Okayama and other parts of the Chugoku region in autumn. For Uchimura, who had been called a "hermit," these activities were surprisingly active. Therefore, this movement had a significant meaning for him (Suzuki, 2017, 224). In March 1918, the Japan Christian Hope Group, which promoted the Second Coming movement, was formed in Osaka. In September, the Kashiwagi Brotherhood(柏木兄弟団) was organized by people belonging to Uchimura's Bible study group to support his movement. To accommodate a large audience, a Bible study group was held at the Tokyo Christian Youth Hall in September 1918, and over 1,200 people attended the lectures there.

However, some pastors of the Tokyo Christian Young Men's Association, which managed the Tokyo Christian Youth Hall, said their opinion that renting the hall to an atheist who denied the existing church was not right. They were forced to change the venue to a different facility (Suzuki, 2017, 225). Due to this influence, the Second Coming movement ended its activities around 1919 (Suzuki, 560).

Key Theological Issues

Kanzo Uchimura's commitment to the idea of the Second Coming is due to his realization that there is no other solution to the problems of this world than the return of Christ after experiencing the death of his daughter Lutsu in 1912 and the outbreak of World War I. Until then, Uchimura had thoroughly criticized the current state of Japanese society and the nation based on God's justice. On the other hand, he had an idealistic view of Japan, which he believed could be realized by the grace of God through the efforts of the Japanese people, even if it would take time. However, after the outbreak of World War I, when the United States, which Uchimura believed a Christian nation, entered the war, his ideology turned toward the Second Coming. Uchimura realized that the problems of this world could not be solved except by the Second Coming of Christ (Ouchi, 535) Shigeharu Nakata, a pastor of the Holiness Church, and Kiyomatsu Kimura of the Union Church, who had been exposed to the Second Coming movement in the United States, also contributed to developing their Second Coming movement.
On January 6, 1918, Uchimura lectured about “The Second Coming of Christ from the Standpoint of a Bible Researcher” at the Tokyo Christian Youth Hall with Nakada, and Kimura. This was the first time Uchimura publicly advocated “Second Coming” or “Eschatology,” and the passage, “I feel that I have left the old world and entered the new world(「余は旧き世界を去りて新しき世界に入りし感がする」),” suggests that his eyes had been opened and his mind renewed (Ouchi, 533). During the past year, Uchimura and his colleagues have traveled throughout Japan giving lectures on the second coming of Christ to an estimated audience of over 20,000 people (Mullins, 84).
On the other hand, there was also strong criticism of the Second Coming movement. In "Premillennialism in Japan (「日本におけるキリスト再臨の信仰」)," Kanzo Uchimura noted that the movement was criticized by Christian leaders, including Ebina Danjo, and the relationship between the mainstream church in Japan and the Second Coming movement (Uchimura, 1985, 276). On the other hand, Uchimura laments that although ordinary believers enthusiastically support the movement, church leaders have condemned it, deeming it "irrational, unnecessary, and dangerous."

The Second Coming movement had a significant impact on Japanese Christianity. For example, as Ebina had a speech against the Second Coming of Christ at Hongo Church in June 1918, he also wrote "Eliminating the Theory of the Second Coming of Christ(「基督再臨説を排す」)" in response to Uchimura's "Collection of Lectures on the Problem of the Second Coming of Christ(「基督再臨問題講演集」)" (Suzuki, 1988, 560)Furthermore, Uchimura's series of lectures were held at the Tokyo Christian Youth Hall. Still, questions arose about renting this place to the Nonchurch people. Therefore, they sent a notice denying the use of the hall in May 1919. Because of this issue and other factors, the Second Coming movement gradually declined.

Compared to the previous Chinese translation version of the Bible, the Japanese language and biblical studies had advanced, and the translation was recognized as an excellent Bible, faithfully translated from the original, with distinctions made between rhyming and prose. Therefore, it was widely used until 1954, when a new colloquial translation of the Bible was published (Suzuki, 218).

Uchimura Kanzo's "The Story of Denmark(『デンマルク国の話』)" in 1911 was a representative Christian publication. The general people widely read this book, as it expressed the feelings of Koreans whose territory had been taken by the annexation of Japan and Korea, superimposed on those of the Danes whose territory had also been taken, and also warned of the possibility of the destruction of nature that would accompany modernization in Japan (Suzuki, 192). As for publications, Masahisa Uemura published "Dai Nihon no Chosun" (Korea in Japan) in Fukuin Shimpo in 1910. This article was banned from sale. Also, Uemura published "Christianity in Korea" in September 1908, criticizing the government's assimilation policy and was forbidden. In January 1916, Komei, Japan's first Catholic magazine and a piety and culture magazine for general Christians, was published by Komyo-sha.

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Kōtoku Shūsui, 幸徳秋水(1871.11.5-1911.1.4)

Kōtoku Shūsui was a journalist and socialist activist who was later arrested and executed in the Taigyaku Jiken (case of high treason). Koutoku was born in Kochi Prefecture in 1871 and graduated from junior high school. Then, he moved to Osaka and studied under Nakae Chomin, a leader of the Jiyu Minken Undo (Movement for Liberty and People's Rights). After graduating from the "Kokumin Eigakkai ( National English Society) in 1893, he became a reporter for "Jiyu Shimbun(The journal of the Liberal Party)" and "Yorozu Choho (The newspapers that focus on social issues)". Around 1897, he became a member of the socialist movement and joined the Christian socialist movement. At the outbreak of the Russo-Japanese War, he advocated non-war and resigned from the Yorozu Choho with Uchimura Kanzo and others. Later, he formed the socialist association Heiminsha with Christian socialist Sakai Toshihikoi in 1903. During this period, he translated "The Communist Manifesto," written by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. They published it in their newspaper "Heimin Shinbun” but it was banned and imprisoned for eight months due to press regulations. Later, he was arrested for involvement in the Great Treason in 1910 and executed in 1911 (Ebisawa, 506). From early on, Koutoku criticized religions, including Christianity, and advocated "socialism" as an alternative to religion. Among his many criticisms, his ideological opposition to Christianity was particularly intense.（Ogawara, 139-141）After he was arrested, Koutoku wrote his "Kirisuto massatsu ron (Elimination of Christ)" in prison, and this essay was published after his death. In this essay, based on the Left Hegelian criticism, he denied Jesus as a historical being, pointing out the inconsistencies in the writings of the four Gospels, the ridiculousness of the miracle stories, and the lack of non-biblical Christological arguments（Itoya, 409）
