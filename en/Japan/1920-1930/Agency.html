日本基督教連盟(National Christian Church Council, NCCC)

The National Christian Church Council is a federation of Christian organizations in Japan, founded in November 1923 by several groups of Japanese Protestant denominations, foreign missions, and organizations. The Japan Continuing Committee, formed after the World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh in 1910, contacted the Federation of Christian Mission in Japa(日本ミッション同盟) and the Japanese Christian Association(日本基督教会同盟) and discussed the establishment of the NCCC in May 1922. After the discussion, they established a Christian organization, the NCCC, in 1923 (Dohi, 2006, 20). The establishment of the NCCC was part of the National Christian Council (NCC) movement, an organization with a solid international flavor and close ties with ecumenical organizations overseas (Dohi, 2006, 207). The NCCC was established as a federation of Christian organizations in Japan for mutual exchange, cooperation, and joint projects. In addition, they aimed to work together to address common Christian problems and to interact with Christian organizations overseas (Dohi, 1980. 241-242).

In September 1926, 13 denominations, 8 organizations, and 22 missions had joined the NCCC. The Anglican Episcopal Church in Japan(日本聖公会）, which had decided that it was possible to cooperate with other denominations without denying its own traditions, joined in November 1929.(Dohi, 1980, 242).

The number of members of the NCCCJ was 100, of which 85 were 51 Japanese and 34 foreign missionaries. Although the Japanese accounted for more than half of the members, it is evident that many missionaries participated. The breakdown was as follows: 15 from the Christian Church of Japan, 12 from the Methodist Church, 12 from the Anglican Church, 10 from the Congregation Church, 6 from the Baptist, and the remaining 15 were elected by the 85 members of the council. (Kirisuto Shinbunsha, 1960, 43) This headcount percentage is likely post-1929 since it includes the Anglican Church.

The first 20 regular council members were Kogo Uzaki (Methodist), Kameharu Ishizaka (Methodist), Samuel H Wainright (Methodist), Yoshimichi Hirata (Union Church), Hiromichi Ozaki (Union Church), Makoto Kobayashi, August Karl Reischauer (Presbyterian Missionaries of America), Daikichiro Tagawa ( (Japan Christian Church), Yugoro Chiba (Baptist), William Axling (Baptist), A.P. McKenzi (Canadian Methodist), Matanosuke Tayama, Kakujiro Ishikawa (Christian Church, Disciples)), Soichi Saito (YMCA Director), J.E. Nipp (Japan Christian Brotherhood Church), Charles Elmer Cowman (OMS), Shoichi Imamura, Ochimi Kubu Shira (Christian Women’s Association), Kikuta Matsuno (Holiness), and Castayota Inanuma (Japan Mipu Church, Methodist) were appointed. Ebisawa (Union Church) was appointed secretary. (Kirisuto Shinbunsha, 1960, 43)

The majority of Protestant denominations, groups, and mission organizations, except for evangelicals, were members of the Federation, and normally, the NCCC came to be thought of as the representative organization or contact point for the Protestant denomination in Japan (Dohi, 2006, 207). However, the federation did not have the function of dealing with church beliefs or politics, and its resolutions were not mandatory. Therefore, the NCCC was able to firmly oppose the Religious Organizations law of 1927 because the various denominations support it. However, the Religious Organizations Law of 1929 caused a conflict between the Methodist Church, which was negative about the bill, and the Japanese Christian Church, which was positive about it, and each groups to consider withdraw from the NCCC, so the NCCC could only take steps to step aside from the opposition movement. Therefore, the NCCC was not that powerful at that time（Dohi, 2006、207-208).

無教会主義の人々/ Groups of Non-chuch movements

Summarize the key contributions/influences of each organization in this decade (500-1,000 words).☐

One of the most active Christian organizations in the 1920s was the group of non-church movements. Kanzo Uchimura initiated it, and it is Japan’s own Christianity and one of the most famous Japanese Christianity (Mullins,2005, 77). Uchimura started his evangelism in 1900 when he launched the magazine “Study of the Bible(聖書之研究).” Although Uchimura emphasized a direct connection with God, he was critical of the Japanese churches for their economic and spiritual dependence on Western missionary organizations. He also criticized the existing church system as hindering the independence of believers and came to believe that it was possible to live as a Christian outside the church. Uchimura named such a position “Non-Church movements (無教会運動)” (Akae, 2013, 11). In 1901, he published the magazine “Non-Church (無教会)” and used the term “Non-Church” as “the church of the churchless. The background for the establishment of Non-Church was the experience of an independent church (独立教会) in Sapporo, where he realized that it is possible to conduct a life of faith without relying on the church system or clergy. Uchimura’s strong evangelical faith was also based on his interaction with Quakers while studying in the U.S. and his religious experience at Amherst College (Mullins, 2005, 79-81).

Although the non-church and the non-church movement took various organizational forms, it mainly revolved around Uchimura’s Bible lectures, or “meetings or study groups,” and publishing a “magazine.” Uchimura’s direct disciples paid fees to attend the study groups. At the same time, Uchimura’s activities through magazines and other publications were so active that they were also called “churches on paper,(紙上の教会)” the number of subscribers gradually increased (AKae,2013, 14). Uchimura’s writings on nondenominationalism were accepted by many people both inside and outside the church, and among those considered Uchimura’s disciples were Toraji Tsukamoto (塚本虎二), Tadao Yanaihara(矢内原忠雄), Kokichi Kurosaki (黒崎幸吉), Shigeru Nanbara (南原繁), and others who would go on to write many works in higher education and Christianity (Mullins, 77). In 1905, Uchimura organized a Kyoyu-kai (教友会) requiring 119 members (this group became a Kashiwagi Brotherhood (柏木兄弟団) in 1916). Membership was limited to individuals who had faithfully served a Christian life for at least one year (Mullins,2005, 88-89)

Uchimura’s disciples independently developed activities through meetings and magazines even before he died in 1930. (Akae, 2013, 14) The development of Non-Church movements coincided with the expansion of the publishing media in Japanese society. In 1937, at least 29 magazines were published by non-church members, and these magazines varied in circulation from a few dozen to several thousand, with readers subscribing to several magazines and attending lectures (Akae, 2013, 14-15). In the postwar 1950s, the number of unchurched Christians was estimated to be between 30,000 and 50,000 or even 100,000. Akae points out that this number is too large, as those counted “readers” as members of their congregations (Akae, 2013, 15). The bearers of Non-Church movements are basically the “readers” of the magazines. Within them, there were two types of culturalism/ liberalism: one was the academic elite (students and intellectuals) who were established based on Ichi High School and Tokyo Imperial University, and the other was the middle-class culturalism/ liberalism people who were farmers and merchants, but who sought to read the Bible lectures through books and magazines (Akae, 2013, 21).

Thus, the Non-Church movement grew while criticizing the existing church system and increased its followers through subscriptions to “magazines.” Uchimura’s strong personality attracted readers and resonated with the Non-Church Movements. Furthermore, the development of the publishing culture contributed greatly to the expansion of Non-Church Movements, allowing a diverse range of people throughout Japan to learn about the ideas of Non-Church Moments.

Although I could not see it this time, Yuichi Akae’s “‘Church on Paper’ and Japanese Modernity: Historical Sociology of Non-Church Christianity,「紙上の教会」と日本近代――無教会キリスト教の歴史社会学,” Iwanami Shoten, 2013, can be cited as a reference.

During this period, Christianity developed a certain growth and activity without being severely tested (Dohi, 1980, 228).

The first is the Taisei Movement (大成運動) of the Methodist Church in Japan. This movement was a response to the Centennial Movement conducted by the Mission bords of the Methodist Episcopal Church in the U.S. to commemorate the centennial of its establishment. In Japan, the movement was implemented over four years beginning in March 1919, with one congregation leading one new person (一人一霊), one Bible verse read each day (一日一節), and a small amount of donation each day (一日一金). To do this movement, they aim to Christian indoctrination throughout the Methodist Church in Japan (Dohi,1980, 238). During this four-year campaign, the total number of members increased by 7,700, and the number of baptized persons increased by more than 11,000, a 50% increase. In addition, educational facilities and a church building were also constructed with funds raised through this campaign and donations from the three Methodist missions. On the other hand, the huge donations from the three missions indicate the difficulty of independence from the missions the Japanese church aimed for at that time. (Dohi,1980, 238-239)

Next, the National Christian Church Council (日本基督教連盟, NCCC), formed in 1923, conducted the National Christianization Movement. From October 1924 to June 1925, the National Christianization Movement involved about 800 lectures, sermons, and pilgrimage evangelism in some 200 cities, towns, and villages throughout Japan, with about 100,000 people participating (Dohi, 1980, 242). One interesting point of this movement is that government officials supported it. For example, some local governments provide places, and their officials attend the meetings (Dohi, 1980,242). It was due to the government’s efforts to use religious power to promote goodwill or a good way of thinking in response to the social unrest following the Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923. In February 1924, a meeting of Shinto, Buddhist, and Christian representatives was also held in this connection. The cooperation of local government offices with the Christian churches was a concrete manifestation. It also promoted Inciting National Spirit (国民精神作興) and the goodwill of thought (思想善道)(Dohi, 1980,242). On the other hand, the NCCC’s attitude of cooperation with the government was also the same attitude of the Christian Society in the mobilized society that followed. On April 12, 1929, at the National Christian Council (全国基督教協議会) organized by the NCCC, they decided to start a campaign the Nationwide Evangelical Movements (全国協同伝道). Furthermore, at the National Christian Council organized by the NCCC in April 1929, Toyohiko Kagawa proposed the implementation of the “Kingdom of God Movement. Then, the Kingdom of God Movement began nationwide in 1930.

This period also saw the active involvement of Catholic and Protestant groups in leprosy. Christian churches had already begun to work for leprosy during the Meiji period, and Christian participation also increased during this period (Sugiyama, Kindle, 115). (Sugiyama, Kindle, 115) Public hospitals were established in various areas, and patients were actually admitted during this period. Christian doctors and nurses worked at these facilities. Some tried to promote Christian evangelism among the people there (Sugiyama, Kindle, 115-116). For example, Fumio Hayashi (林文雄, 1900-1947) was a physician who wanted to become a leprosy doctor to solve the problem of medicine and faith (Sugiyama, Kindle, 116). Also, Christian missionaries began to work at the sanatoriums, and some Christians thought evangelizing in sanatoriums was their mission. The sanatoriums also welcomed Christian missionaries to exhort there to keep the public morale in the sanatoriums (Sugiyama, Kindle, 116). In 1925, the Japan MTL (Mission to Lepers), a leprosy relief organization led by Toyohiko Kagawa, was formed to support sanatoriums and promote segregation, following comfort visits and missionary activities for the inmates. (Sugiyama, Kindle, 111)

During this period, Christianity found its raison d’etre in social works. They tried to resolve social issues such as relief for orphans and disabled children and support for delinquent children. Because of this work, it led to a great development of social programs by Christian organizations. Furthermore, working with lepers was perceived by Christians as an act of “love,” and they sought to work with the most oppressed people in the world to demonstrate “God’s love. However, it should not forgotten that evangelism in sanatoriums also helped to keep public morale and supported the segregation policy (Sugiyama, Kindle, 118).

出口王仁三郎（1871.8.27-1948.1.19)

Deguchi Oninzaburo was a second-generation leader of the Oomoto-kyo from the 1920s onward. He became a member of the Myōrei Church(妙霊教会, a kind of mountain worship) after mystical experiences with his family and himself (Kawamura, 2017, 138-139). After various occupations, he decided to become a religious practitioner and studied the spiritual arts, and in 1898, he met Nao Deguchi and joined the Omoto-kyo. With his experience as a religious practitioner, Onisaburo established the organizational structure of the Omoto-Kyo and founded the Kinmei Reigakkai(金明霊学会), with Nao as the head minister and himself as president. This association was characterized by its national theocratic appearance, interspersed with discourses on the state based on an imperialist view of history (Kawamura, 2017, 160).

　After gaining Nao’s trust, Ohninzaburo was chosen as the successor of the Daimonkyo in 1900, married Sumi, the fifth daughter of Nao Deguchi, and changed his name to Ohninzaburo. Meanwhile, internal conflicts erupted, and in 1908 the Konmei Reigakkai was renamed the Dai Nihon Shusaikai(大日本修斎会), with Nao as president and chancellor and Ohninzaburo as chairman. One of the characteristics of Oninzaburo’s religious thought and practical movement was that he emphasized imperialism and Japanism by juxtaposing and joining Nao’s teachings to the Imperial Law and National Shinto (Kawamura, 2017, 197-198). With the outbreak of World War I, Oomoto also sought to gain adherents through written propaganda and the use of mass media (Kawamura, 2017, 198).

In 1916, Onisaburo revised the Oomoto-kyo as the Kodo-Oomoto( 皇道大本, Imperial Way Omoto) and published the journal Shinreikai(『神霊界』); when Nao died in November 1918, Onisaburo used his previous knowledge (Kokugaku, Shinto, Confucianism, spiritualism, etc.) to interpret Nao’s writing, formulating and publishing his writings. He interpreted it with a Kokutai discourse based on national Shinto and inclined toward Japanism and kokutai ideology (Kawamura, 2017,213). He then constructed Omoto and Kokutai discourse that surpassed the Emperor Myth and National Shinto by speaking of the so-called Kodo. (Kawamura, 2017,213)

　　Meanwhile, the Taisho Restoration Movement of Omoto-kyo developed nationwide, and fervent missionary efforts were carried out nationwide (Kawamura, 2017, 229). In 1920, a training hall was constructed, enabling more large-scale lectures and practices, and a new training facility was also built in Kameoka, Kyoto (Kawamura, 2017, (233). Street missions and public lectures increased the number of those interested in and practicing Oomoto-kyo. The number of local branches, mainly in Kansai, increased from 40 in 1917 to 80 in 1919, and in the 1920s, it expanded to Hokkaido, Okinawa, and Taiwan, bringing the total to 148 (Kawamura, 2017,233). At the same time, accusations and attacks against Omoto-kyo were also prolific (Kawamura, 2017, 240). This rapid expansion resulted in government repression, and on February 12, 1921, several members, including Ohnisaburo, were arrested for disrespect and newspaper violations. And police desrpyed Deguchi Nao’s tomb. At the time, the Kodo-Oomoto was estimated to have a congregation of 10,000, a tremendous growth from the 1,000 members around 1912. Furthermore, contact with the Baha’i Faith brought internationalist overtones. The Baha’i Faith used Esperanto, which Wang Jinzaburo was eager to learn and which was also adopted by the Oomoto Church (Kawamura, 2017, 261). Furthermore, Onisaburo’s Asianism led to an alliance in 1923 with the Chinese Daoin (the five great religions) and even the World Red Manji Jikai, and later to an expedition to Manchuria 261)

The characteristics of Onisaburo’s thought were understood as a mixture of Japanism, Asianism, and internationalism booming in the 1920s. However, it is also important to note that at the root of Onisaburo’s thought was the coexistence of nationalistic Shinto thought. Imperial Japan was placed at the center of ethnocentrism and chauvinism (nationalistic) for China and Manchuria (Kawamura, 2017,274). It shows that in the 1920s, Onisaburo, although repressed, was still free to carry out the missionary work of the Oomoto religion. This organizational expansion led to another round of repression in 1935 when Ohnsaburo and his cadres were arrested on charges of disrespect and violation of the Security Law; after the second round of repression, the cult was dismantled when the government confiscated its land, but after the war, it was restarted as Aizen-en, which was later renamed the Aizen-en(愛善苑) after the war.

Shinran boom/ 親鸞ブーム

Summarize the key characteristics of each event/movement in this decade (500-1,000 words). ☐

There was no general opposition act to Christianity during the 1920s and 1930s. On the other hand, this period showed a growing interest in new religions and Buddhism. In particular, interest in Shinran(親鸞,1173-1263), the founder of Jōdo Shinshū(浄土真宗), began to grow around 1922. It was also known as the “Shinran boom(親鸞ブーム),” and the literary community led this phenomenon. During this period, many novels and plays were published on Shinran, with 16 major works with “Shinran” in the title alone(Nakajima, 2017, 148). Works featuring Shinran include Gohei Ishimaru(石丸梧平,1886-1969)’s “Ningen Shinran(『人間親鸞』)” and “Passion of Shinran(『受難の親鸞』),” both of which became famous. As a human being, Shinran was portrayed not as the founder of Jodo Shinshu or as an incarnation of God, but as a human being suffering from his own problems. (Nakajima, 2017,154) On the side of historiography, the mystical aspect was denied, and Shinran was explored as a historical fact. Some scholars began to study Shinran as a living person who did not perform miracles, and some even proposed the “theory of Shinran’s elimination(親鸞抹殺論)” that he did not exist in history(Nakajima, 2017,155). These discussions led to the acceptance of Shinran as an “ordinary human being” who was neither an incarnation of God nor a miracle worker (Nakajima, 2017,156).

In 1917, Kurata Hyakuso(倉田百三, 1891-1943) published “Priest and his Disciples(出家とその弟子),” and it was the beginning t of the Shinran boom that began in 1917. These works were written based on existing biographies and do not show Shinran as a mystic (incarnation of God, miracles, etc.). Furthermore, most works depict Shinran as a young man in his youth and a person in pain (Nakajima, 2017,157-158). The two types of Shinran depicted in this period are Shinran as a young man suffering from his afflictions and earnestly seeking salvation from them and Shinran as an older man who warmly watches over his disciples from the same perspective. In this period, the First World War had ended, and human suffering would never cease no matter how advanced civilization became. Shinran attracted attention as the embodiment of such human suffering (Nakajima, 2017,161-162). Even more noteworthy is the development of the publishing culture during this period. In this background, Shinran was mass-produced and consumed as a diverse “Own Shinran” beyond the framework of Jōdo Shinshū(Nakajima, 2017,162). During this period, Hideichi Noyori(野依秀市,1885-1968) was a powerful link between the media and Shinran. He started the Dainippon Shinshu Propaganda Association(大日本真宗宣伝協会) in 1921. In September, he launched the magazine Shinshu no Sekai (『真宗の世界』) to convey his vision of Shinran to the general public(Nakajima, 2017,162). He tried to spread Shinran andJōdo Shinshū’s thoughts, criticized corruptJōdo Shinshū priests, spread Shinran’s message to people outside the Shinshu community, and increased his visibility Nakajima, 2017,(163-164).

The background to the depiction of Shinran as a human being and his wide acceptance by the public was also the climate of the Taisho period (1912-1926). In this time, people began to think about “individual liberation(個人の解放)” and to seek “individual fulfillment(個人の充実)” in Japan. Therefore, this was also why religious literature such as Toyohiko Kagawa’s “Beyond the Battle to the Death” and Koyata Ehara’s (江原小弥太, 1882-1978) “The New Testament(「『新訳』),” both based on humanism, was widely read (Nakajima, 2017,171). Thus, people’s interest in Buddhism and new religions expanded, including the Shinran boom.
