Hong Kong: Agency (1930-1940)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

(1) Karl Ludvig Reichelt (Sharpe 1984)

Dr. Karl Ludvig Reichelt (1877–1952), a Norwegian missionary, arrived in China in 1903 under the Norwegian Missionary Society. Initially, he ministered in Hunan and later taught at the Lutheran Theological Seminary in Hubei. In 1905, during a visit to a major Buddhist monastery on Mount Weishan, he envisioned a reformation of Western missionary methods and a new mission strategy for evangelizing Buddhists. In 1921, he founded the Christian Mission to Buddhists in China (CMB) to foster dialogue between Christianity and Buddhism.

In 1922, Reichelt established the Jingfengshan Christian Monastery in Nanjing. Between 1922 and 1927, the monastery hosted 5,000 Buddhist monks, 50 of whom converted to Christianity. However, during the Northern Expedition in 1927, anti-foreign sentiment led to the destruction of Jingfengshan. Reichelt temporarily relocated to Hangzhou and established the Tianfengshan Monastery but eventually decided to leave China.

In 1929, Reichelt moved to Hong Kong, and in 1930, he purchased a site in Sha Tin, naming it Tao Fong Shan (Mountain of the Christ Wind). He invited Danish architect Johannes Prip-Møller to design the monastery buildings in the style of Chinese Buddhist temples. By 1932, a series of buildings with distinct Chinese characteristics were completed, and missionary work targeting Buddhist monks gradually began at Tao Fong Shan.

Reichelt maintained a close relationship with Bishop Ronald O. Hall of the Anglican Church in Hong Kong. Bishop Hall admired the tranquil environment of Tao Fong Shan and chose to reside in the Ling Yim Terrace on the mountain rather than the Central District bishop’s residence. In 1934, Hall presided over the dedication ceremony of the Jingzun Treasure Hall.

Tao Fong Shan placed great emphasis on literary missionary work, using publications to promote its vision. In 1934, it launched the journal Tao Fong, publishing two issues annually. The journal ceased publication during the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong but resumed in 1948. The journal primarily explored theological perspectives on the relationship between Christianity and other religions and included records of lectures aligned with the monastery’s mission.

During the Chinese Civil War, the Lutheran Theological Seminary in Hubei decided to relocate to Hong Kong. Classes resumed at Tao Fong Shan in December 1948, with Reichelt assisting in teaching. He passed away in March 1952 in Hong Kong and was buried in the Christian Monastery Cemetery at Tao Fong Shan. Reichelt’s notable works include Introduction to Religion (1931), The Transformed Abbot (1954, 1959), Truth and Tradition in Chinese Buddhism: A Study of Chinese Mahayana Buddhism (1934), Religion in Chinese Garment (1951), and Meditation and Piety in the Far East: A Religious-Psychological Study (1953).

(2) Ronald O. Hall (Paton 1985; Chan-Yeung 2015; 吴青 2017)

Ronald O. Hall (1895–1975) served as the seventh bishop of the Anglican Diocese of Victoria (1932–1966). A native of Newcastle, England, Hall graduated from Oxford University in 1920 and was ordained as a curate. He later served as pastor at St. John’s Church in Lambeth. In 1922, Hall first visited China to attend the World Christian Student Federation annual meeting in Beijing.

From 1925 to 1926, Hall worked as a secretary for the YMCA in Shanghai, where he met Gu Ziren, a leader of the Christian student movement. Gu gave Hall the Chinese name “Ho Ming Wah,” meaning “light to China,” reflecting Hall’s mission. In 1927, Hall returned to England and served as rector of St. Luke’s Church in Newcastle.

In 1932, Hall was elected Bishop of Victoria. He was consecrated at St. Paul’s Cathedral in London in October and assumed his position in Hong Kong in December. In 1933, during the 12th Synod of the Diocese of Victoria held in Guangzhou, Hall advocated for appointing a Chinese assistant bishop to promote church autonomy. He also proposed that future church buildings adopt traditional Chinese architectural styles. In 1934, at St. John’s Cathedral in Hong Kong, Hall consecrated Mok Sau Tseng as the first Chinese assistant bishop of South China. Under Hall’s leadership, two new churches were constructed in the 1930s—Holy Trinity Church and St. Mary’s Church—both designed in Chinese styles.

After World War II, Hall was instrumental in establishing several social welfare organizations, including the Hong Kong Council of Social Service, the Hong Kong Children’s Reception Centre, St. James’ Settlement, the Hong Kong Family Welfare Society, the Boy Scouts, and the Hong Kong Housing Society. He also led the Anglican Church’s efforts to address the shortage of school places by establishing 19 primary schools and 8 secondary schools between 1950 and 1966, as well as founding vocational training schools. Hall played a key role in the establishment of Chung Chi College, which later became part of the Chinese University of Hong Kong.

Hall’s tenure lasted 34 years, spanning the significant transition of 1949 when the People’s Republic of China was established. During this time, Hall reorganized the diocese, transforming the Diocese of Victoria and Canton into the Diocese of Hong Kong and Macau. His term also saw controversies, such as the ordination of the first female Anglican priest, Li Tim Oi, and his 1956 visit to China.

Hall received two honorary medals from the Nationalist Government and was appointed a Companion of the Order of St Michael and St George (CMG) by Queen Elizabeth II. In 1965, the University of Hong Kong awarded him an honorary Doctor of Divinity degree in recognition of his contributions to Hong Kong society.

Hall retired in 1966 and passed away in Oxford, England, in April 1975.

(3) Hsü Ti-shan (黃文江 2014: 247-252; 全港文化界 1941)

Hsü Ti-shan (1894–1941), a renowned writer and scholar with the pen name “Luo Hua Sheng” (Falling Peanut), was born in Tainan, Taiwan, and traced his ancestry to Jieyang County, Guangdong. His family fled Taiwan following his father Hsü Nan-ying’s failed involvement in the Taiwan Democratic State’s resistance against Japanese occupation.

After attending schools in Shantou and Guangzhou, Hsü became a teacher in Zhangzhou and Myanmar. In 1916, he was baptized at the London Missionary Society Church in Zhangzhou. He then pursued studies at Huiwen University in Beijing (the predecessor of Yenching University), where he graduated in 1920. He continued theological studies at Yenching University’s School of Religion, earning a Bachelor of Divinity degree in 1922. Subsequently, Hsü pursued advanced studies in religious history and comparative religion at Columbia University, Union Theological Seminary in New York, and Oxford University.

Returning to China in 1927, Hsü taught at Yenching University’s Faculty of Arts and the School of Religion and later at Tsinghua University, where he also lectured on anthropology. In 1930, he joined Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou as a sociology professor. After conducting research in India, he returned to Yenching University before moving to Hong Kong in 1935.

With Hu Shih’s recommendation, Hsü was appointed head of the Department of Chinese at the University of Hong Kong (HKU). During his tenure, he reformed the curriculum, moving away from the traditional focus on classical Chinese texts like the Four Books and Eight Masters of the Tang and Song Dynasties. Instead, he introduced a modern framework dividing courses into literature, history, and philosophy.

In Hong Kong, Hsü actively promoted cultural and social initiatives, serving as chairman of the Sino-British Cultural Association and holding leadership roles in various organizations. He also participated in the Union Church of Chinese Christian Churches and occasionally delivered sermons.

Hsü passed away in August 1941 in Hong Kong from heart disease. His funeral service was held in HKU’s Loke Yew Hall, led by Bishop Ronald O. Hall, Zhao Zichen, and Reverend Zhang Zhuling. Governor Geoffrey Northcote mourned Hsü’s passing, calling it a “great loss not only to HKU but also to Hong Kong’s cultural community.”

Hsü’s literary works, including The Merchant’s Wife and The Jade Pass, reflect elements of his Christian faith. He also made significant contributions to religious studies with works like A History of Taoism and A Study of Spirit-Medium Superstition.

(4) Wong Chung-hoi (邢福增 2014: 160-163)

Wong Chung-hoi (1895–1979) was a native of Taishan County, Guangdong, and a prominent pastor in the Methodist Church. He was baptized on Christmas Day in 1912 at the Methodist Church in Taishan. In 1921, Wong dedicated his life to ministry and graduated from the Union Theological Seminary in Guangzhou’s Baihedong District in 1924.

After serving in several locations in Guangdong, Wong was assigned to Hong Kong in 1933 to succeed Reverend Kwan Kang-yau as pastor of the Methodist Church on Caine Road. During his ministry, he actively supported the Five-Year Forward Movement initiated by the National Christian Council of China. He spearheaded fundraising for the construction of a new church building in Wan Chai, raising over HKD 200,000. The new church, located at the intersection of Johnston and Hennessy Roads, was completed in January 1936.

In 1937, Wong was ordained as a pastor and appointed superintendent of the Hong Kong District. During World War II, Wong was transferred to Kaiping and later served as superintendent of the Taishan-Kaiping District. Despite the Japanese invasion, Wong continued his ministry, relocating the district office to Shaoguan in northern Guangdong.

After the war, Wong returned to Guangzhou to lead church reconstruction efforts. In 1948, he was reassigned to Hong Kong as district superintendent and pastor of the Wan Chai Methodist Church, where he served faithfully until his retirement in 1965. Wong passed away in 1979 at the age of 84.

(5) Hwang Sze-Tian (黃彩蓮 2017: 103-111)

Hwang Sze-Tian (1896–1988), originally from Jinjiang, Fujian, moved to Hong Kong at age 13. Although he was born into a Christian family, he was indifferent to his faith until his 30s, when a battle with tuberculosis and liver disease led to a spiritual awakening. Hwang became an active member of the Union Church of the Church of Christ in China.

In 1929, Hwang participated in the Anti-Slavery Society and was elected its Chinese secretary. He introduced the society’s work to Xiamen, where it inspired the founding of the China Domestic Maid Rescue Society. During the Second Sino-Japanese War, Hwang joined relief efforts for refugees from southern Fujian who fled to Hong Kong. He organized prayer meetings for these refugees and established worship services in the Minnan dialect in 1938, laying the foundation for the Minnan Church in Hong Kong.

Hwang played a vital role in establishing Hong Kong’s Minnan-speaking churches and continued his ministry until his death in 1986 at the age of 91.

(6) Lau Yut-shing (李金強 2014: 36-41; 2002: 93-103)

Lau Yut-shing (1893–1960), a Baptist pastor from Dongguan, Guangdong, was converted to Christianity as a child while attending a school run by the Church of Christ in China. After completing teacher training, Lau felt called to ministry and pursued theological studies at the Baptist Seminary in Guangzhou, graduating in 1918.

Lau served in Baptist churches in Guangzhou, Taishan, Shanghai, and San Francisco before returning to China in 1932. During the Second Sino-Japanese War, Lau moved to Hong Kong in 1938 and became pastor of the Caine Road Baptist Church. He founded the Hong Kong Baptist Convention and initiated relief efforts for war refugees.

Lau also held various leadership positions, including vice president of the South China Baptist Relief Association and chairman of the Hong Kong Wartime Children’s Relief Association. He continued to serve the Baptist community until his retirement in 1958 and passed away later that year.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Hong Kong Christian Population (Xing Fuzeng 2004: 4; Hong Kong Government 1930a, 1930b, 1938a, 1938b)

According to the Hong Kong Blue Book’s Ecclesiastical Return, from 1930 to 1939, the worship attendance of Chinese churches continuously increased from 4,040 to 6,220. During the same period, the worship attendance of non-Chinese churches declined from 1,505 to 1,135. In terms of growth rate, Chinese churches (54%) far surpassed non-Chinese churches (-24.6%).

However, the Chinese churches included in the Hong Kong Blue Book were limited to mainstream denominations: Anglican Church, London Missionary Society, Congregational Church, Methodist Church, Basel Mission, and Rhenish Mission.

In 1938, the total population of Hong Kong was 1,050,256, with 23,611 non-Chinese and 1,026,645 Chinese. In other words, Chinese Christians (worship attendees) accounted for approximately 0.6% of the Chinese population in Hong Kong, which was higher than 0.38% in 1929. In 1936, the number of worship attendees recorded in the Blue Book was around 4,375, increasing to 5,035 in 1937 and further to 6,220 in 1939. Comparing 1939 with 1936, the growth rate of attendance was 42.2%.

The growth of the Chinese Christian population during this period is believed to be related to the refugees fleeing the Sino-Japanese War from South China to Hong Kong (the Chinese population increased from 966,363 in 1936 to over 1 million in 1939, an increase of 6.2%).

  1. Tao Fong Shan Monastery, 1930 (Sharpe 1984; Li Zhihao 2006)

Tao Fong Shan Monastery was established by Karl Ludvig Reichelt of the Christian Mission to Buddhists in 1922 on Jingfeng Mountain in Nanjing, aiming to evangelize to Buddhists. At the end of 1929, due to political instability in China, Reichelt decided to relocate to Hong Kong, first temporarily settling in Tai Po in the New Territories. Shortly afterward, he selected a barren mountain in Sha Tin as the site, completed the land purchase in April 1930, and named it Tao Fong Shan.

In May 1933, the “Pilgrim Hall” (now the Hall of Worship) was the first to be completed, serving as a place for worship, dining, and learning. Later, dormitories and schools were built, and the sanctuary was inaugurated in 1934. The architectural materials adopted the traditional Jiangnan style, including bright red columns, bricks, black tiles, and flying eaves. The main structure mimicked the octagonal double-eaved spire of the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests in Beijing’s Temple of Heaven. Eight ridges featured statues of monks, Taoist priests, and mythical creatures. At the center of the hall was a golden cross, with a plaque above and couplets on either side, all written in gold on a black background in regular script. The original plaque read, “In the beginning was the Word,” and the couplets read, “The divine light shines universally; the great vow guides the compassionate vessel,” aiming to guide Buddhist and Taoist seekers to follow Jesus Christ. In 1936, the Tao Fong Shan Christian Cemetery was established, and the Research Institute building (now Yun Shui Hall) was completed in 1938. Besides publishing the periodical Tao Fong, the monastery also released the “Tao Fong Series,” including Reichelt’s An Introduction to Religion (1931), Tao Fong Shan, and the edited The Work of Tao Fong Shan (1938).

  1. Chinese Methodist Church, 1936 (Huang Dicai 2015: 90)

The Chinese Methodist Church (initially named the Wesleyan Church) in Hong Kong moved multiple times in its early years, relocating between Wellington Street, High Street in Sai Ying Pun, and Wan Chai. It finally purchased a chapel on Aberdeen Street in 1906 and opened branch chapels in nearby areas. In 1916, it moved to Caine Road. The church later selected a triangular site at the junction of Hennessy Road, Johnston Road, and Fenwick Street. Construction began in November 1934. In April 1935, E. Dewstoe, president of the Canton-Hong Kong Methodist Conference, laid the foundation stone, engraved with the words, “This chapel is dedicated to the worship of God, the proclamation of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the glory of the Heavenly Father, and the salvation of humankind.” The new chapel on Hennessy Road, Wan Chai, was completed in January 1936, marking a new milestone for the Methodist Church. At the time, the church had 630 adult members and 371 child members. After the chapel’s completion, church activities expanded further, strengthening organizations such as the Bible night school, Sunday school, fellowship groups, youth department, women’s department, and choir, allowing ministries to grow.

The chapel, designed by Mehlert in a Chinese Renaissance architectural style, featured red bricks in a triangular layout, with the upper floor serving as the sanctuary. The Chinese architectural elements included a tiled roof, a tower, and a balcony, while Western features were evident in the cornices, arched windows, and red brickwork. The front bore the inscription, “Chinese Methodist Church.”

In 1994, the church was demolished and rebuilt. In 1998, the new Methodist House was completed, a 22-story building that retained elements of the original red-brick chapel. The lower floors housed the sanctuary and the Methodist Church’s General Assembly offices, while the upper floors were leased as commercial office spaces. The reconstruction was a groundbreaking collaboration between the church and the New World Development Group, pioneering a model of partnership between Christian institutions and real estate developers.

  1. Local Church, 1937 (Wu Longyun 2018: 65-67; Lee Changshou 2001: 259-274)

In the 1930s, the Local Church movement led by Watchman Nee reached Hong Kong. Before 1937, several brothers were already engaged in gathering and worship in Hong Kong. In 1937, Watchman Nee arranged for Wei Guangxi to come to Hong Kong, where he started breaking bread meetings on Nam Cheong Street, Kowloon. At the end of 1937, Watchman Nee and Franz Lün arrived in Hong Kong. Nee sent Wei to Kunming and returned to Shanghai in 1938. In 1940, Wei Guangxi returned to Hong Kong, first holding meetings at the Minsheng Academy, later moving to Yen Chow Street in Sham Shui Po and Shek Kip Mei, and finally relocating to Jordan Road in 1941. In 1938, 1939, and 1940, Chen Zexin visited Hong Kong three times to preach and later shifted his mission to Southeast Asia. In August 1949, Watchman Nee held a revival meeting in Hong Kong. In September, Chen Zexin arrived in Hong Kong and was appointed by Nee to oversee the ministry in Hong Kong and Macau. From January to March 1950, Nee held special meetings in Hong Kong, delivering the message “Turn It Over,” which was referred to as the Hong Kong “revival.” During this period, five elders were appointed to oversee the Hong Kong church: Chen Zexin, Wei Guangxi, Qu Ziyuan, Xu Junqing, and Zheng Qianyuan. Wei was also assigned to manage the Gospel Bookroom in Hong Kong. In December 1950, the meeting hall on Observatory Road, Tsim Sha Tsui, was completed and inaugurated in January 1951. By 1953, the number of attendees had exceeded 1,100.

After 1955, internal divisions arose within the church. Wei Guangxi followed Witness Lee, while Chen Zexin considered Lee’s teachings to be heretical. In 1970, when Witness Lee visited Hong Kong, conflicts erupted between the followers of both sides at the Observatory Road meeting hall, ultimately requiring police intervention. Later, disputes over the ownership of the hall were taken to court. Eventually, an out-of-court settlement was reached, with Chen Zexin and his followers withdrawing. Those who supported Witness Lee continued to use the Observatory Road hall under the name “The Church in Hong Kong,” while the followers of Chen Zexin referred to themselves as “The Christian Assembly.” In 1991, “The Church in Hong Kong” experienced another split when Elder Feng Zhili issued a statement opposing Lee’s heretical teachings, gaining the support of most believers. A small number of Witness Lee’s followers left and established another assembly, later called “The Church in Hong Kong (The Local Churches).” The original Observatory Road system was renamed “The Church in Hong Kong – Church Assembly Hall (Christian Stewards).”

  1. Evangelical Free Church of China, 1937 (Wang Ronglong 2022: 230-237)

The Evangelical Free Church was founded by The Swedish Evangelical Free Church of the USA during its mission work in China, primarily developing in Guangdong. Due to the war, many Evangelical Free Church members fled southward from Guangzhou to Hong Kong. In the spring of 1937, the church established a branch on Prince Edward Road in Kowloon, named Prince Edward Chapel (renamed Tin Chuen Church in 1952). Pastor Luo Xiaochuan served as the chapel’s leader, with evangelist Ju Jiemin assisting. In its first year, 55 people were baptized. In August 1938, Guangzhou was bombed by Japanese forces and fell in October. In 1939, the church acquired two buildings on Haiphong Road in Kowloon, one serving as the headquarters of the Evangelical Free Church and a relocated Guangzhou Bible Seminary, and the other as a base for evangelical work. In October 1939, the Haiphong Chapel (renamed Yan Chuen Church in 1950) was established. Pastor Luo Xiaochuan concurrently served as the Dean of the Bible Seminary and as the chapel’s leader. Although the Evangelical Free Church had not previously established churches in Hong Kong, the establishment of Prince Edward and Haiphong Chapels due to the war laid the foundation for its development in Hong Kong. After the war, in response to political changes in China, the headquarters moved from Guangzhou to Hong Kong in July 1949. At this time, the church was still under the leadership of Western missionaries from the Swedish Evangelical Free Mission (West Mission). In the autumn of 1949, Guangzhou Bible Seminary relocated to Hong Kong once more, opening a new chapter for the Evangelical Free Church’s development in Hong Kong from the 1950s onward.

  1. Social Service Centre of the Churches, 1937 (Liu Yuesheng 1996: 288-289)

In 1937, in response to the Sino-Japanese War, a large influx of refugees arrived in Hong Kong. Chinese and Western church members in Hong Kong established the Social Service Centre of the Churches, with Bishop R. O. Hall serving as president and J. L. Wilson, the Dean of St. John’s Cathedral, serving as chairman. The center aimed to provide disaster relief and other social services, such as assisting the unemployed and helping refugees return to their hometowns. From its establishment in 1937 to 1940, the center sent 2,294 refugees to refugee camps, provided financial assistance to 5,187 individuals, and supported 3,311 families in need. This was a crucial platform for Hong Kong Christians to unite Chinese and Western forces to engage in relief work during wartime.

  1. Kowloon City Swatow Baptist, 1937 (Kowloon City Swatow Baptist Church, n.d.)

In 1937, during the Japanese invasion of China, many people from Chaoshan fled to Hong Kong. Believer Yao Jianmin moved his family to Pugang Village and, observing that many villagers had not heard the gospel, collaborated with Mr. Lin Zifeng to merge an existing Hakka evangelistic station into the “Kowloon City Swatow Baptist Chapel” in Pugang Village. They applied to the Hong Kong government for land, raised funds to build a wooden house, and completed the chapel in August of the following year. A dedication ceremony was held, and a management committee was formed, hiring Pastor Wu Shide as the minister. The chapel provided medical services, medicine, free education, and worked to improve water supply infrastructure for the community. By the summer of 1940, the congregation exceeded 100, and seating was insufficient.

During the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong, the Pugang area was converted into an airport, forcing the chapel to relocate to 27 Lung Kong Road, where it became the “Swatow Baptist Church.” After the war, the church grew and moved to Prince Edward Road. With further development, land was purchased on Nga Tsin Wai Road, and a new chapel was completed in 1953, named the “Kowloon City Swatow Baptist Church.” The church later expanded to various locations across Hong Kong, including Tsuen Wan, Chuk Yuen, Wang Tau Hom, Ngau Tau Kok, Po Tat Estate, Kwun Tong, Shau Kei Wan, Causeway Bay, and Shun Tin Estate, with Chuk Yuen hosting a service center.

  1. Bethel Bible Seminary, 1938 (Lan Ruxi and Hu Meilin 2000: 131-134)

In 1937, the Sino-Japanese War broke out, prompting Bethel Bible Seminary to relocate from Shanghai to Hong Kong. In 1938, the seminary rented Nos. 43-45 on Grampian Road and purchased No. 47. However, in December 1941, the Japanese occupied Kowloon, making it impossible for the seminary to continue its operations. Starting in 1942, some faculty and students evacuated Hong Kong in groups and relocated to Guixian in Guangxi Province. After the victory of the Anti-Japanese War, Lan Ruxi returned to Hong Kong to reclaim the properties occupied by the Japanese military. In March 1947, the seminary resumed its first Sunday worship services and reopened for classes. In 1948, the seminary established an elementary school for the general public and added a high school department to Bethel High School in 1950. During the 1950s, responding to refugee issues, Bethel Church began setting up rooftop schools in resettlement areas and establishing churches.

  1. Presbyterian Church, 1939 (Liu Yuesheng 1996: 151-153)

After the fall of Guangzhou, Reverend Leung Chiu-fan, General Secretary of the Guangdong Synod of the Presbyterian Church, arrived in Hong Kong intending to gather members to establish a church but was unsuccessful. In 1939, as Jiangmen and Xinhui fell, many Presbyterians fled to Hong Kong. In May, they began meeting at the Guangdong Methodist Chapel on Guangdong Road, forming a temporary congregation. In October, they relocated to No. 21 Mong Kok Road, setting up Sunday schools, revival meetings, and prayer gatherings, gradually developing a structured congregation. By March 1940, due to the inadequacy of the venue, the congregation moved again to the second floor of Nos. 30-32 Prince Edward Road, with over 200 members and a newly established Sung Kwong Elementary School with more than 50 students. At its first anniversary, the congregation established a deacon board and ordained five deacons to oversee church affairs. In 1960, the church finally constructed a new chapel, naming it the Chinese Christian Presbyterian Church.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. John Sung’s Revival Meetings (Levi 1995: 110, 116-117, 210-211, 261)

John Sung (1901–1944) was a renowned evangelist in modern China. In the 1930s, he conducted four revival meetings in Hong Kong.

The first meeting, held from March 6 to 12, 1932, at the Bethel Mission, featured instances such as “many preachers and Bible school students confessing their sins to one another and seeking forgiveness.” One notable event was a “disobedient son reconciling with his mother and praying together.” This meeting was part of the Bethel Evangelistic Band’s South China tour. After leaving Hong Kong, they traveled to Guangzhou, Wuzhou, Guixian, and Yulin. Sung returned to Hong Kong from Guangzhou for another session from April 28 to May 8, also at the Bethel Mission, where 32 individuals requested baptism. Later, he led study groups at the Methodist Church and hosted a citywide union meeting at the Hop Yat Church. During this visit, he prayed for the ailing wife of Lin Hu, shared his message at the Xinguang Blind School, and, at the invitation of Zhang Jisheng, preached to over a hundred workers at the Anleyuan Biscuit Factory, leading many to tears of repentance.

From June 14 to 25, 1936, Sung returned to Hong Kong and was hosted by Lin Zifeng. His sermons on the “Church of Laodicea” criticized Hong Kong churches for “failing to shine for the Lord and squandering God’s grace.” He remarked that “many have time to make coffins but no time to shine for the Lord as witnesses.” Responding to Sung’s call, 50 evangelistic teams were formed, with Lin Zifeng elected as their leader. Upon Sung’s departure, 200 people gathered at the pier to sing hymns as they bid him farewell.

From May 13 to 18, 1938, Sung held meetings at Minsheng Academy in Kowloon, where “117 people experienced spiritual blessings, 164 individuals received the laying on of hands, and 21 new evangelistic teams were formed.”

  1. Indigenous Church Architecture (Xing Fuzeng and Liu Shaolin 2010: 71-74; Wei Keli and Chen Ruiwen 2014: 89-100; Lung Ping-yee 1992: 129)

In 1932, when Ronald O. Hall was appointed as the seventh Bishop of the Diocese of Victoria (Diocese of Hong Kong and South China), he envisioned that future church architecture should adopt a Chinese style externally while incorporating foreign designs internally.

In 1933, when the Hong Kong government informed the Anglican Church that the land for Holy Trinity Church in Kowloon City would be reclaimed for leveling Victoria Peak, Hall supported rebuilding Holy Trinity Church using Chinese architectural styles. Churches with traditional Chinese architectural features could be seen as “China’s Renaissance.” The emergence of such churches was closely tied to the 20th-century Christian indigenization movement. Professor Lung Ping-yee from the Department of Architecture at the University of Hong Kong noted, “During the 1920s, the ‘Anti-Christian’ or ‘Anti-Religion’ movements in China resisted Western religious ideologies. By the 1930s, Chinese churches were established with strong patriotic religious sentiments, seeking independence not only in liturgy and theology but also in administration and finances. Consequently, church buildings began adopting Chinese designs to align with the principles of ‘self-support, self-propagation, and self-governance.’” Ronald O. Hall was a key advocate for this “Sinicized” church architecture. As a British missionary, Hall continuously sought ways to harmonize Christianity with Chinese society and culture.

In 1930, Norwegian missionary Karl Ludvig Reichelt from the Christian Mission to Buddhists founded Tao Fong Shan in Sha Tin. The monastery was designed entirely in the style of a Buddhist temple, aiming to create a dialogue space between Christianity and Buddhism and guide monks to Christ. Hall greatly admired Reichelt’s vision and efforts. Hall’s advocacy for combining Chinese architectural styles with Christian sanctuaries was likely inspired by Reichelt.

During Hall’s tenure, two Anglican churches rebuilt in Hong Kong, Holy Trinity Church and St. Mary’s Church, adopted Chinese architectural styles. Both were designed by Ng Kin-chung.

Additionally, in 1930s Hong Kong, the South China Regional Seminary (1931) of the Catholic Church adopted classical Chinese architectural patterns. The original design was intended as a courtyard-style complex. However, due to economic constraints, the wings and upper stories of the main building were omitted, leaving only the south wing completed (Lam Se-ling 2010: 177-179).

Key Theological Issues

  1. Dialogue and Controversy Between Christianity and Buddhism (Tong Wing-Sze 2019: 287-294; Li Zhihao 2006: 139-177)

After its establishment in 1930, the Tao Fong Shan Christian Monastery initiated inter-religious dialogues in Hong Kong, particularly between Christianity and Buddhism. In its early years, Buddhist monks were the primary audience for its evangelistic efforts, and missionaries offered religious lecture courses to visitors. The founder of Sha Tin’s Ten Thousand Buddhas Monastery (established in 1957), Master Yuet Kai, visited Tao Fong Shan in 1933 as the 38th visiting monk (Huang Dicai 2015).

In the inaugural issue of Tao Fong, Karl Ludvig Reichelt wrote, “Jesus Christ is not merely a sage of one nation, one ethnicity, or one era. He is the eternal Word, without beginning or end, self-existent and everlasting, the foundation of all things, and the source of all life… From this abundant ‘Word’ flows endless living water—some in large streams, some in small streams, some belonging to the East, and some to the West. Each nation and sect, according to their unique forms, flows from this one ‘Word,’ ultimately returning to one truth. It is the hope of this publication that people of all nations and sects may freely drink from this vast spring and benefit from the fullness of the ‘Word.’”

The monastery’s Tao Fong Shan Institute of Religious Studies welcomed both Christians from mainland China and a number of Buddhist and Taoist individuals to engage in discussions on religious truth. Visitors from various religious traditions, including Buddhist monks and laypeople, often engaged in dialogue or even debate about Christian beliefs with those at the monastery. For example, one monk named Liaoyi, who transitioned from Tibetan Buddhism to Zen Buddhism, studied the Bible at Tao Fong Shan and discussed the concept of salvation in Christianity with Reverend Wong King Hing. Liaoyi regarded Jesus as a sage but questioned how Jesus fulfilled the plan to redeem humanity.

Reichelt’s inculturation approach, which included elements of Buddhism, inevitably drew criticism from the broader Christian community, with accusations of syncretism. For instance, the original name of the chapel, “Jingzun Bao Dian” (Hall of Revered Scenery), was misunderstood by some to refer to a Buddhist deity. In 1945, Reverend Wong King Hing renamed the hall as the “Sanctuary” (Sheng Dian). The couplet inscribed on either side of the hall, which originally read “The divine light shines universally; the great vow guides the compassionate vessel,” was changed to “The Word was with God; the wind blows wherever it pleases.” These modifications aimed to address external critiques of Tao Fong Shan’s practices and maintain its Christian identity.
