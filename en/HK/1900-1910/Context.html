Hong Kong: Context (1900-1910)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

(1) Chinese Anglican Church Body Incorporation Ordinance, 1902 (Zhong Renli, 1951:19; Xing Fuzeng & Liu Shaolin, 2010:18-19)

In 1902, the Chinese Anglican Church Body Incorporation Bill was formally submitted to the Legislative Council for deliberation. When explaining the bill, the Attorney General emphasized that the Chinese Anglican Church in Hong Kong was striving for self-support and self-governance, and the bill would confirm its legal status. In July, the bill was passed by the Legislative Council, granting the Chinese Anglican Church in Hong Kong official legal status, including the ability to own its property.

The ordinance’s background dates back to September 1901, when the biannual meeting of the South China Diocese was held at St. Paul’s College in Hong Kong. Under the leadership of Bishop Joseph Hoare, the meeting passed a resolution to nurture the Chinese Anglican Church in Hong Kong. This led to the establishment of the Hong Kong Diocesan Council (The Chinese Anglican Church Body), which oversaw the local Chinese congregations outside of missionary societies. The purpose of this reform was to lay the foundation for self-support and self-governance among local churches and to encourage Chinese believers to financially support and take responsibility for the salaries of Chinese clergy.

The Hong Kong Diocesan Council (The Church Body) was chaired by the Bishop of Victoria, with a vice-chairperson appointed by the Bishop. Other members included all local Chinese clergy (or acting foreign missionaries) and representatives elected by the Vestry of each parish. If the property of a congregation belonged to a missionary society or was associated with its educational or other initiatives, the society’s secretary would also attend. The council established a “General Church Fund,” supported by donations from each parish, to fund the salaries of Chinese clergy. Each local church under the council was required to establish a Vestry, chaired by the rector or acting pastor, with at least three male representatives elected by all male members of the congregation. If the congregation’s property was owned by a missionary society, the society’s secretary was also entitled to attend.

(2) Church Property Vesting Ordinance, 1904 (Hong Kong Law, 1904)

On November 5, 1904, the Legislative Council passed the Church Property Vesting Ordinance, which granted additional powers to the trustees of St. John’s Cathedral in Hong Kong. Under the St. John’s Cathedral Ordinance of 1899, these trustees were incorporated as a corporate body, entrusted with the ownership and management of the cathedral and its site. However, other churches in the Diocese of Victoria faced various difficulties due to the death or absence of trustees.

The ordinance authorized the cathedral trustees to acquire, hold, manage, and dispose of land and other properties to facilitate the work of the Anglican Church in Hong Kong and China in religious, educational, social, or other capacities.

(3) Man Mo Temple Ordinance, 1908 (Wai Ting-ming, 2007:35-39)

The Man Mo Temple Ordinance was the first colonial legislation related to the management of traditional religious institutions, transferring the Man Mo Temple and its associated assets to the management of the Tung Wah Hospital.

The Man Mo Temple, dedicated to the worship of the Civil and Martial Deities (Man Tai and Mo Tai), was built in 1847 on Hollywood Road, Sheung Wan, by early Chinese merchants Lou Ah Kwei and Tam Ah To. The land was nominally acquired from the government for the purpose of establishing a school but was used to construct the temple. In traditional Chinese society, temples often served as sacred spaces for deliberating community affairs. The establishment of the Man Mo Temple symbolized the formation of an early Chinese community in the colony. In addition to arbitrating business disputes among Chinese merchants, the temple also hosted visiting Qing officials and served as an intermediary between the Chinese community and the colonial government.

The Tung Wah Hospital, founded in 1870, initially provided traditional Chinese medicine services to the Chinese community and later incorporated Western medicine after the 1894 plague. Tung Wah was the earliest Chinese charitable organization in Hong Kong and became a central power in the Chinese community. Its board members were often prominent Chinese merchants and leaders who acted as intermediaries between the Chinese population and the colonial authorities.

By the early 20th century, most of the registered managers of the Man Mo Temple had passed away, and the Tung Wah Hospital board had already taken de facto responsibility for temple affairs. As a result, the government decided to transfer the management of the Man Mo Temple to Tung Wah Hospital and enacted legislation to formalize this arrangement.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

The Controversy Surrounding the Introduction of the Pentecostal Movement to Hong Kong (Dai Guanhao, 2020:79-84; Chen Mingli, 2015; Chinese Recorder, 1901)

In 1907, Alfred G. Garr and his wife arrived in Hong Kong, marking the beginning of the Pentecostal movement in the region. Upon their arrival, Garr borrowed the venue of the Chinese Congregational Church on Ladder Street from Charles R. Hager, a minister of the American Congregational Church, to hold Pentecostal meetings. These gatherings focused on confession, repentance, and the baptism of the Holy Spirit. Over several consecutive days, revival meetings were held, drawing hundreds of participants, and Chinese attendees began to experience the baptism of the Holy Spirit.

This development attracted the attention of the broader Hong Kong Christian community and sparked controversy. Rumors spread that Garr employed hypnotism, claiming to “bewitch people under the guise of Christ’s name.” The Chinese Congregational Church, which had lent its venue, began to harbor doubts about Pentecostal beliefs and eventually refused further use of its facilities under the pretext of preparing for Christmas celebrations.

In January 1908, the Chinese Recorder published an article describing the Pentecostal Church’s practices, such as preaching the gift of tongues, criticizing other missionary strategies, and fostering intense emotional displays during worship, which caused disturbances among the Christian community in Hong Kong. The article accused the Pentecostal Church of leading Chinese Christians astray rather than converting non-Christians, stating that it served “to pervert Christian Chinese rather than to convert the heathen.” This reflects the significant impact of the Pentecostal movement, which attracted members away from their original churches, creating challenges for mainstream Christian congregations.

Garr himself remarked in early 1908, “No missionaries in Hong Kong support us.” Another individual, Mok Lai-chee, who had left the Chinese Congregational Church to join the Pentecostal movement, described facing immense “persecution,” “defamation,” and “hatred” due to his involvement.

五旬節運動傳港引發爭議（戴觀豪2020：79-84；陳明麗2015；Chinese Recorder 1901）

1908年1月，《教務雜誌》（Chinese Recorder）刊載消息，形容五旬節教會宣揚方言恩賜（preaching the gift of tougues）、抨擊其他傳教策點（attacking the methods of other Christian missionaries）、嶺拜異常情感激動（strange scenes of excitement）的情況，對香港教會帶來干擾。文章又批評五旬節教會使華人信徒墮落，而非使異教徒歸信（to pervert Christian Chinese than to convert the heathen）。這反映出當時不少教會信徒被吸引，離開本屬教會對主流教會帶來的衝擊。嘉活力在1908年初形容「沒有香港傳教士支持我們」。另一位自美華自理會改投五旬節運動的莫禮智，也形容自己正承受極大的「逼害」、「毀謗」、「憎惡」。

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

Hong Kong and the Decade of Reform and Revolution at the End of the Qing Dynasty(Huo Qichang, 2019:130-146; Ng Lun Ngai-ha, 1988; Liang Shouhua, 2007; Mok Shixiang, 2011; Carroll, 2007:81-84)

The 1900s marked the final decade of Qing rule, characterized by the Qing court’s last-ditch efforts at reform through the New Policies and the flourishing of revolutionary movements in China. This decade can be summarized by two key themes: reform and revolution.

Although Hong Kong was a British colony, it played a significant role in the development of reform and revolutionary movements in China. Intellectually, both reformist and revolutionary ideologies found a space for free expression in Hong Kong. For example, in 1900, True Explanations of New Policies (《新政真詮》) co-authored by Ho Kai and Hu Li-yuan, was published in Hong Kong. Ho advocated for a constitutional monarchy modeled after Britain, where sovereignty would reside with the people.

In January 1900, the Hong Kong-based Revive China Society (興中會) launched the China Daily (《中國日報》), with Chen Shaobai as its editor-in-chief. The newspaper openly promoted revolution and engaged in debates with local conservative papers. In February of the same year, another revolutionary publication, China Weekly (《中國旬報》), was also founded in Hong Kong. In 1903, China Daily reprinted Zou Rong’s booklet The Revolutionary Army (《革命軍》), renaming it Revolutionary Vanguard (《革命先鋒》). The newspaper continued publication until 1913, serving as a platform for disseminating major revolutionary theories, including the first appearance of Sun Yat-sen’s Three Principles of the People. Thus, Hong Kong’s intellectual openness made it a fertile ground for both constitutional and republican revolutionary ideas. Sun Yat-sen later acknowledged in a 1923 speech at the University of Hong Kong that his revolutionary thoughts were rooted in his experiences in Hong Kong.

Beyond intellectual influence, Hong Kong also served as a strategic base for revolutionary activists planning armed uprisings. The Guangzhou Uprisings of 1895 and 1900 and the Huizhou Uprising were orchestrated from Hong Kong. To suppress revolutionaries, the Qing government sent assassins to Hong Kong. For instance, Yang Quyun, the first president of the Revive China Society, was assassinated in Central on January 10, 1901.

In October 1905, the Hong Kong branch of the Chinese Revolutionary Alliance (同盟會) was established at the China Daily headquarters in Central, becoming the first branch outside Tokyo. Between 1905 and 1911, the Hong Kong branch and the Southern Division of the Revolutionary Alliance launched several small-scale uprisings, including the uprisings in Pingxiang, Liuyang, and Liling in Hunan and Jiangxi provinces in 1906, as well as the Huanggang Uprising, the Qiniang Lake Uprising in Huizhou, and the Zhennan Pass Uprising in 1907. These uprisings relied on firearms purchased, manufactured, or smuggled through Hong Kong. Revolutionaries gathered in Hong Kong before the uprisings and sought refuge there after failed attempts.

Financially, Hong Kong’s Chinese merchants supported revolutionary activities. Figures such as Wong Wing-sang, Lee Koon-tong, Lee Yiu-tong, and Lam Chak-man made significant contributions. China Daily was primarily funded by Lee Koon-tong and Lee Yiu-tong, while funds raised overseas were often remitted to Hong Kong first.

However, the British government did not want its colony to become a base for subversion or a threat to British interests in China. The Hong Kong government had to balance various interests without offending the local Chinese community. Following the 1898 Coup of Wuxu, reformist Kang Youwei fled to Hong Kong with the help of the British government, rallying support for the reformist cause. When Kang returned to Hong Kong in November 1899, China’s Minister in London lodged a formal protest with the British Foreign Secretary in December, accusing Britain of harboring Kang. Subsequently, the Qing government issued arrest warrants for Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao, offering a reward for Kang’s capture. Ultimately, the British Foreign Office suggested that Kang relocate to Singapore for his safety, leading to his departure from Hong Kong at the end of January 1900.

In 1907, at the Qing government’s request, the Hong Kong government enacted the Chinese Publications (Prevention) Ordinance, which imposed up to two years of imprisonment or a fine of up to HKD 500 for printing, publishing, selling, or distributing materials in Hong Kong that incited rebellion or crime in China. This ordinance was Hong Kong’s first legislation restricting freedom of expression in Chinese publications, and it primarily targeted China Daily.

Both reform and revolution reflected the strong nationalist sentiments among Hong Kong’s Chinese community. This was evident in the anti-American and anti-Japanese boycott movements of 1905 and 1908. In 1905, Chinese merchants in Shanghai launched an anti-American boycott to protest the U.S.’s exclusionary policies against Chinese immigrants. In Hong Kong, Lee Yiu-tong mobilized support from the business and education sectors to form a “boycott committee.” In 1908, Chinese merchants in Guangzhou initiated an anti-Japanese boycott, and Chinese merchants in Hong Kong joined by closing shops for two weeks. Japan’s exports to Hong Kong dropped by more than one-third as a result. These boycott movements required significant social mobilization, and many participants also became supporters of the revolutionary cause. This demonstrates the close relationship between boycott movements and revolutionary activities.
