Taiwan: Agency (2000-2020)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Dr. Chen Chien-Jen (1951/6/6-)

A Taiwanese epidemiologist, he has served as the 14th Vice President of the Republic of China, the 31st Premier, and the 10th Vice President of the Academia Sinica. His father, 陳新安, was the founder of the “White Faction” in Kaohsiung. He graduated from the Department of Zoology at National Taiwan University in 1973, obtained a master’s degree from the Graduate Institute of Public Health at National Taiwan University in 1977, and received his PhD in epidemiology from Johns Hopkins University in the United States in 1982, specializing in epidemiology, public health, and preventive medicine. He later returned to Taiwan to teach at the Graduate Institute of Public Health and the Institute of Epidemiology at National Taiwan University, focusing on research related to Kaposi’s sarcoma, hepatitis B, cancer, and viruses. (Executive Yuan, n.d.; AsiaNews, n.d.)

From 2003 to 2005, he was appointed as the Director General of the Department of Health, Executive Yuan, to handle the SARS outbreak and to construct Taiwan’s infectious disease prevention system. From 2006 to 2008, he served as the Chairman of the National Science Council, and in 2016 he was elected as the 14th Vice President of the Republic of China, later assuming the role of Premier in 2023. (Executive Yuan, n.d.)

During his university years, he met and married 羅鳳蘋, a devout Catholic, and was baptized into the faith. Dr. Chen noted that he had no religious beliefs prior to university, and only began to explore Catholicism during his studies in the United States. Apart from his public duties, he actively participates in church ministries and is frequently invited to speak on topics related to science and faith. (Central News Agency, 2016)

Due to his contributions to the church and society in Taiwan, he was invested by the Holy See as a Knight of the Order of the Holy Sepulchre of Jerusalem in 2010, honored as a Knight of the Order of St. Gregory the Great in 2013, and appointed as a member of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences in 2021. Because of his status as a Catholic, he has been appointed several times as a special envoy to participate in activities at the Vatican, significantly contributing to strengthening relations between Taiwan and the Vatican. (Central News Agency, 2016)

  1. Rav. Nathaniel Chow (1941/7/20-)

Chairman of the Global Apostolic Network of Living Water and former Senior Pastor of Taipei Living Water Church. Coming from a Presbyterian family, he served in campus evangelistic fellowships from 1965 to 1977, focusing on student counseling. From 1977 to 2011, he served as the Senior Pastor of Taipei Living Water Church, where he established a “team ministry” model, increasing staffing to enhance service capacity. In the 1980s, he introduced the prayer growth model from Korean churches, gradually transforming the church’s structure. In the 1990s, he proposed the “Screen Theory,” advocating for “exercising caution to block mosquitoes, but not hindering the wind of the Holy Spirit from entering the church,” further changing the age-based fellowship nurturing model to small group nurturing and transforming choir worship into praise and worship. He also began sending workers to Kaohsiung, Tainan, and Taichung to expand the church, actively launching church planting initiatives. He later established a theological seminary directly affiliated with the church, training talents in church planting and pastoral work. He actively participated in the evangelistic movement in 2000, further planting churches across Taiwan. By the time he retired in 2011, Taipei Living Water Church had grown to over two hundred branch churches. In the same year of his retirement, he became the chairman of the Global Apostolic Network of Living Water, taking on the role of guiding global Living Water churches. 周神助 is recognized as a pioneer in introducing the growth model of Korean churches and the charismatic evangelism of American Pentecostals to Taiwanese churches. Through sending co-workers to central and southern Taiwan, he significantly expanded Living Water Church from a single location in Taipei to various parts of Taiwan, becoming the third largest denomination in Taiwan after the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan and the Local Churches. In addition to church expansion, he also utilized mass media (such as founding 嘉音 Radio and participating in the founding of GOODTV) to broaden the audience and enhance influence. (Truth Hall, 2011; From Deputy Pastor to Global Church Planting Evangelism; The Acts of the Holy Spirit at Taipei Living Water Church)

  1. Dr. Ying Fuk-tsang (1966/1/28-)

Former Dean of the Divinity School at Chung Chi College, Chinese University of Hong Kong, and Professor in the Department of Cultural and Religious Studies at Chinese University of Hong Kong. A researcher in modern Chinese Christian history. He entered Chinese University of Hong Kong in 1983, majoring in history. After graduating in 1987, he stayed at the university to pursue a master’s degree, writing a thesis on the faith and cultural adaptation of Christians during the late Qing period. After obtaining his master’s degree in 1989, he worked as a high school teacher and returned to CUHK in 1992 to pursue a PhD, writing a dissertation on the patriotic thoughts of Christians during the Republican era, and received his doctoral degree in 1995. Since 1993, he has served as an Associate Professor at Hong Kong Baptist Theological Seminary, and also as the Director of the Research Centre for Christianity and Chinese Culture.In 2004, he transferred to the Department of Cultural and Religious Studies at Chinese University of Hong Kong, where he has served as Deputy Director of the Centre for the Study of Religion and Chinese Society, Professor (Pang Wan-Lun Chair) at Chung Chi College, and Director of the Contemporary Christianity Teaching Resource Centre at Chung Chi College. From 2014 to July 2020, he was the Dean of Chung Chi College. 邢福增 became a Christian during his sophomore year in university and subsequently decided to dedicate his life to the study of modern Chinese Christian history. He has been engaged in research on Chinese Christian history for over thirty years. He began working on contemporary Chinese Christian history research after co-authoring “The Three-Self Movement in the 1950s” with 梁家麟 in 1996. In addition to the Three-Self Movement, he has conducted in-depth studies on important Christian leaders of the time, such as 趙紫宸, 陳崇桂, 倪柝聲, 王明道, and 吳耀宗. After the reform and opening-up, he was able to enter China to connect with individuals related to the Three-Self Movement and house churches, leading him to feel the changes within the Chinese church and dedicate himself to researching the relationship between church and state in contemporary China. After his transfer to CUHK, his research has further focused on issues related to contemporary Chinese Christianity, organizing historical materials, and writing church history in Hong Kong. (Chinese University of Hong Kong, Chung Chi College, n.d.; Between the Past and the Present)

  1. Rav. Chen Zhihong (1963-)

Born in 1963. He became a Christian and was baptized at the age of sixteen, receiving a calling to full-time ministry at eighteen. He studied social work at National Taiwan University and, after graduating, entered the Chinese Evangelical Seminary for theological training, graduating at the age of twenty-nine. He has served faithfully in the Taiwan Lutheran Church. He has been the pastor of 家家歌珊堂, a supervisor in the Taiwan Lutheran Church, and president of the important publishing organization, 道聲出版社. He has also served as the Director of the Theological Education Center of the Taiwan Lutheran Church. (Chinese Evangelical Seminary, n.d.)

In terms of further theological education, Pastor Chen Zhihong holds a Master of Theology (Th.M.) from Trinity Theological College in Singapore and a Doctor of Ministry (D.Min.) degree from the Chinese Evangelical Seminary. He currently serves as the Vice President of the Chinese Evangelical Seminary, where he is also the Director of the Administrative Office and an Assistant Professor of Practical Theology, teaching courses on church leadership and management. (Chinese Evangelical Seminary, n.d.)

However, Pastor Chen Zhihong is known to society for his active participation in social movements and politics in Taiwan. As a pastor of the Taiwan Lutheran Church, he has publicly expressed his opposition to LGBT rights and has served as a spokesperson for the Alliance for the Protection of Families (護家盟), becoming one of the representatives of the Christian community in this movement. He is also involved in politics, being a member of the first presidium of the Faith and Hope Alliance party and served as a rotating chairman. He participated in the legislative elections in 2016. Although he temporarily resigned from his position in the Taiwan Lutheran Church during the establishment of the Faith and Hope Alliance, he resumed his role as a pastor after the elections concluded. (Christian Today, 2016)

Overall, Pastor Chen Zhihong embodies multiple identities as a pastor, theological educator, and social participant, holding a certain level of prominence and influence within the Taiwanese Christian community, especially among conservative evangelicals and in the realm of public affairs. (Christian Today, 2016) (OPH)

  1. Rav. Yang Ning-ya (1952 or 1953-)

Yang Ning-ya grew up in Puli, Taichung, and is a second-generation Christian. His father was a deacon in the local Lutheran church and also a fervent pray-er. He attended National Taiwan University and gathered in the college fellowship at Truth Hall, where he was called to full-time ministry in his junior year in 1974. He earned a master’s degree from the Chinese Evangelical Seminary and a Master of Systematic Theology from Luther Northwestern Theological Seminary in the United States. He has served as a bishop in the Taiwan Lutheran Church and as the founding chairman of the Pastoral Training Association. (Christian Today, 2022)

In 1978, Yang Ning-ya graduated from the Chinese Evangelical Seminary and entered the Lutheran Taipei Student Center to minister to students alongside Pastor 俞繼斌. After a year, when Pastor 俞 went abroad for further studies, 杨 took over as the director of the student center, renaming the originally independent student center to “Truth Hall Galilee Fellowship,” gradually integrating the resources, skills, and potentials of college students with the church. He became the Senior Pastor of Truth Hall in 1991 and served until his retirement in 2022, having ministered for over 40 years. (Christian Today, 2022)

During his tenure as the Senior Pastor of Truth Hall, he actively promoted small group ministry in the 1990s and focused on the training of church leaders, producing many pastors and volunteers. His efforts transformed Taipei Truth Hall from a relatively small church into a large church with significant scale and influence.During his pastoral tenure, he actively promoted the “RPG Revival Prayer Group,” which had a wide-ranging impact in the Taiwan church community. (Christian Today, 2022)

His theological views, in addition to the Evangelical emphasis on “Jesus Christ and Him crucified,” are deeply influenced by evangelicalism and embrace elements of the Renewal Movement. He emphasizes the importance of prayer and actively promotes healing and deliverance ministries within the church, viewing them as integral parts of the Gospel. He is also concerned about the lives and testimonies of Christians in the workplace and is committed to integrating the Gospel into the daily lives of believers, including their marriages, families, workplaces, and communities, encouraging them to exert influence in their respective domains. (Christian Today, 2022)

Pastor Yang Ning-ya has a strong mission regarding the unity and cooperation of churches in Taiwan. He has served as the president of the Taiwan Christian Alliance, working to connect churches across Taiwan’s cities and denominations to collectively address national issues and exert a positive influence in society. He holds a grand vision for missions in the Chinese world. After stepping down as the Senior Pastor of Taipei Truth Hall, he transitioned to become the president of the Asia Missions Alliance, focusing on promoting the global Chinese missionary movement, equipping, and sending missionaries around the world. (Christian Today, 2022)

Overall, Pastor Yang Ning-ya is a particularly influential figure in the Taiwanese Christian community, especially within the realms of large churches and interchurch cooperation since the 2000s, making significant contributions in church pastoral care, leader cultivation, church unity, and mission work. (Christian Today, 2022) (OPH)

  1. Rev. Ewen Chow (1972-)

Rev. Ewen Chow, born in 1972, is the eldest son of Pastor 周神助, chairman of the Taipei Living Water Network, and Pastor 莊碧明, and is an influential pastor and leader in the Chinese church community. He currently serves as the Youth Pastor at Taipei Living Water Church and is also the co-founder and CEO of Asia for JESUS Kingdom Harvest Association, as well as the Deputy Executive Director of the Global Apostolic Network of Living Water. (Asia for JESUS, n.d.)

At the age of twenty-five, he went to the City Harvest Church in Singapore to attend Bible school, later graduating from the Word of Life Bible School founded by Ulf Ekman, dedicating himself to preaching the message of Kingdom culture among Chinese churches. He longs to lead teams and churches with a Kingdom culture, creating a spiritual environment filled with God’s presence, where the Holy Spirit moves freely, and where brothers and sisters grow healthily. (Asia for JESUS, n.d.)

From 1998 to 2019, Rev. Ewen Chow served as the leader of the well-known Joshua Worship Team for twenty years, during which he released over twenty worship albums. In addition to serving in Taiwan, he is also an international itinerant speaker and a core member of the Kingdom Culture Conference team. (Asia for JESUS, n.d.)

Rev. Chow’s ministry particularly emphasizes identity in Christ, the heart of a son, and the importance of healthy father-son relationships in the church and family. He married his wife 璽恩, who previously served as the lead singer of the Joshua Worship Team, and they recently welcomed their first child after many years of marriage. (Asia for JESUS, n.d.)

As the son of Pastor 周神助 (former Senior Pastor of Taipei Living Water Church, currently president of the Global Apostolic Network of Living Water), Rev. Ewen Chow has deep roots in Taipei Living Water Church. He leads the Asia for JESUS team in organizing spiritual meetings, evangelistic rallies, and equipping courses across Taiwan and around the world, dedicated to promoting “Kingdom culture” and spiritual revival. (Asia for JESUS, n.d.) (OPH)

  1. Rev. Jonathan Chow (1975-)

Rev. Jonathan Chow is an important leader among the youth at Taipei Living Water Church and is the second son of Pastor 周神助 and Pastor 莊碧明. Rev. Chow earned a Bachelor’s degree in Psychology from the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), a Master’s degree in Industrial-Organizational Psychology from the University of Nebraska, and graduated from the Word of Life Bible School in Sweden. (Taipei Living Water Church, n.d.)

His ministry journey began in 2001 when he served as a pastor in the youth department. In early 2009, he was ordained as a pastor.In September 2013, Pastor Jonathan Chow took on the role of Dean of the Living Water School of Life and, in August 2015, concurrently served as Deputy Director of Mission and Church Planting. In November 2017, he joined the governance team of the Senior Pastors, participating in church governance under the leadership of Senior Pastor 區永亮. He later became the Special Assistant to the Senior Pastor in October 2018 and was promoted to Executive Pastor in October 2019. In 2020, he was nominated by the board of deacons and unanimously approved by 82 attending and representing deacons at the fifth meeting of the 10th Executive Board, and he was elected as the Senior Pastor of Taipei Living Water Church, officially taking office in 2021. (Taipei Living Water Church, n.d.)

Despite both of his parents being full-time ministry workers, Pastor Jonathan Chow did not rebel in his youth; rather, he loved participating in co-worker meetings. Even during his years studying and working abroad, he always held on to the dream of returning to serve full-time at his home church. For a long time, he has had a deep burden for equipping, teaching, missions, and education, and he has shared the vision of workplace missions around the world, hoping to transform and innovate the existing educational framework. He has led teams on numerous mission trips to serve various churches worldwide. (Taipei Living Water Church, n.d.)

Additionally, Pastor Jonathan Chow serves as the Dean of the Living Water School of Life and as the Deputy Executive Director of Asia for JESUS. As a father of three children, he received a vision in 2015 to establish “Zhuo Ya International Academy,” desiring to prepare a healthy educational environment for school-age children based on biblical truths, the heart of the Father, and values aligned with God’s will, hoping to initiate a movement among Chinese churches to revolutionize the current educational framework. (Taipei Living Water Church, n.d.) (OPH)

  1. Rev. Ewen Yang (1954-)

Pastor Ewen Yang was born in 1954 in an agricultural community in Yunlin County. Growing up in an environment where gambling was prevalent, he was exposed to gambling from a young age. At the age of fifteen, due to financial difficulties in his family, he went to Taipei alone to seek fortune, becoming addicted to gambling in pursuit of quick wealth, and later moved to Kaohsiung for work. (福氣教會, n.d.)

While working in Kaohsiung, a Christian colleague’s contact and sharing planted the seeds for his future faith transformation. In 1983, Ewen Yang was baptized and became a Christian. In 1999, he received a vision and, along with his wife and coworkers, founded 福氣教會, becoming the Senior Pastor of Kaohsiung Christian 福氣教會 and insisting on serving in this role to this day. (福氣教會, n.d.)

Starting in 2017, his promoted “Happiness Group” model gained widespread resonance among churches in Taiwan and among overseas Chinese communities, with a 2018 seminar attracting participation from tens of thousands. In 2023, 福氣教會 collaborated with the Holy Light Theological Seminary to offer professional courses, hoping to develop the “Happiness Group” into a model for Christians’ daily lives and to promote revival in Chinese churches. (福氣教會, n.d.) (OPH)

National Prayer Breakfast in Taiwan

National Prayer Breakfast in Taiwan

The National Prayer Breakfast is a global religious and political exchange event held in various countries modeled after the American version. This event showcases the complex interaction between religious groups and political power, while also sparking much controversy. In Taiwan, the National Prayer Breakfast is organized by a preparatory committee composed of representatives from various denominations, with the participation of the International Christian Businessmen’s Association (CBMC) starting in 2015. The core power is concentrated in three pastors—周神助, 寇紹恩, and 張茂松—and their affiliated church systems, with 陳偉望 and 洪善群 serving as conveners for multiple sessions within this system. (Wikipedia, n. d.; Christian Today, 2016; Storm Media, 2020; Christian Forum, 2015; Up Media, 2020; Christian Today, 2019)

The origins of the National Prayer Breakfast can be traced back to 1935, initiated by Swedish Methodist pastor Abraham Vereide in Seattle, inviting local businesspeople to share their faith, study the Bible, and build relationships. This model quickly expanded, and by 1944 it had developed into a joint prayer breakfast for congressional members. In 1953, President Eisenhower participated in a prayer breakfast primarily for heads of government agencies and legislators, establishing the basic form of the modern National Prayer Breakfast, organized by the evangelical religious and political organization known as The International Foundation (formerly the Fellowship Foundation, also referred to as The Family). Since then, it has become an annual important event for the U.S. government and the Christian community. (Wikipedia, n. d.; The Family, n. d.; Netflix, 2019)

South Korea started holding its National Prayer Breakfast in 1966, mainly driven by Pastor Joongon Kim, who maintained close ties with the American Fellowship Foundation while studying in the U.S. However, the National Prayer Breakfast in Korea has historically faced severe criticism, accused of supporting military authoritarian governments in exchange for government subsidies and favors. The most controversial moment came after the Gwangju Uprising in 1980 when the National Prayer Breakfast continued to be broadcast nationwide, with Pastor Kyungchik Han publicly praising 전두환 as 'the Moses given to Korea by God,' which was criticized as endorsing violent suppression. (Wikipedia, n. d.)

In Taiwan, the National Prayer Breakfast was established in 2000 after President 陳水扁 took office, inspired by the Korean National Prayer Breakfast in 2000. Notably, past leaders such as 蔣中正, 蔣經國, and 李登輝 were Christians, and they had a habit of regularly gathering with pastors and believers for prayer. Thus, when non-Christian 陳水扁 was elected President, marking Taiwan's first peaceful transfer of power, the Christian community might have initiated the National Prayer Breakfast to maintain its political influence. This was not only for a collective prayer but could also be a strategic action by religious leaders to influence the political direction of Taiwan. (Wikipedia, n. d.; Christian Forum, 2016; Storm Media, 2020; Up Media, 2020)

Supporters argue that the National Prayer Breakfast is a rare opportunity for different denominations to set aside their positions and prejudices to pray collectively for the nation and its leaders. It is also an important occasion for the church to demonstrate its social concern and influence, promoting positive interaction between religion and politics. (Christian Today, 2016; Taiwan Church Cooperation Association, n. d.)

Critics point out that the political-religious relationship behind the National Prayer Breakfast may lead to a 'mutual back-scratching' problem. As revealed in the 2019 Netflix documentary 'The Family,' the Fellowship Foundation in the U.S. has been accused of being an extremely conservative anti-LGBT organization, and the National Prayer Breakfast often becomes a 'political white glove,' allowing elites from various countries to bypass normal diplomatic procedures to form alliances. Some Christians criticize this act as contrary to the spirit of Jesus, who scorned colluding with political power during his lifetime. As stated by Su Nan-chou of the Wilderness Magazine, Jesus would not 'spend large amounts of money and create a spectacle for the rulers to stand and applaud, welcoming and sending them off.' To this day, the National Prayer Breakfast remains a unique platform for the intersection of religion and politics, but its value and operation warrant ongoing attention and discussion from society.

台灣基督教聯盟（Taiwan Christian Alliance）

台灣基督教聯盟（Taiwan Christian Alliance，簡稱台基盟）設立於2014年，是一個致力於促進台灣各城市、各宗派教會之間連結、合一與合作的重要組織。在2000年至2020年期間，特別是後半段時期，該組織展現了顯著的活動力和社會影響力。(台灣基督教聯盟, n. d.; 國度復興報, 2014)

台基盟的核心使命在於建立台灣基督教會之間的緊密網絡，整合教會資源，共同關注台灣社會的公共事務。聯盟期望透過教會的團結合作，發揮轉化國家社會的力量，並共同完成基督信仰的宣教使命，包括推動跨文化宣教工作。(台灣基督教聯盟, n. d.; 國度復興報, 2014)

聯盟主要透過舉辦「台灣牧者國是論壇」等會議，為各地牧者和教會領袖提供交流、分享與討論的平台。這些活動不僅強化了教會間的橫向連結，也讓基督教界能夠就重要社會議題形成較為一致的聲音。台基盟特別關注家庭價值、教育改革及職場宣教等議題，同時努力成為教會與政府之間溝通的有效橋樑。(台灣基督教聯盟, n. d.; 基督教今日報, 2017)

台基盟的一個顯著特徵是匯聚了台灣基督教界中許多大型、具有影響力教會的主要牧者。這些教會和牧者多半與靈恩或福音派的發展趨勢有密切關聯（OPH）。台北真理堂的主任牧師楊寧亞曾擔任台灣基督教聯盟協會的理事長，展現了大型教會在推動教會聯盟和合作方面的重要角色。雖然該會的董監事名單涵蓋了靈糧堂體系、浸信會、信義會等不同宗派背景的牧者，但其中幾位理事長和常務理事級別的人物，都與台灣一些大型、有影響力且多半帶有靈恩或福音派色彩的教會有關。聯盟成員名單體現了跨宗派和跨區域連結的努力，同時也反映了在特定社會議題上，聯盟成員與台灣保守派基督教界的立場有所關聯，特別是在家庭價值觀和道德倫理等議題上。(台灣基督教聯盟, n. d.; 基督教今日報, 2014)

總結而言，台灣基督教聯盟在2000年至2020年間作為台灣基督教界的重要合作平台，不僅促進了教會間的資源共享與合一，也提升了基督教在台灣社會公共事務中的參與度和影響力，同時凸顯了靈恩與福音派教會在台灣基督教社群中的重要地位和社會關懷取向。(台灣基督教聯盟, n. d.)（OPH）

  1. The Contradictory Relationship Between the Taiwanese Church and the LGBTQ Movement

Around 1990, Taiwan experienced a wave of change marking a new era; it wasn’t just political freedom that emerged, but significant transformations across various social spheres. In terms of gender issues, the LGBTQ movement in Taiwan also began during this era, with the religious LGBTQ movement starting in 1996, marked by the birth of the first Christian LGBTQ group, 約拿單團契, which is also the first religious LGBTQ group in Taiwan. (Wikipedia, n. d.; 关键评论网, 2019; 法律白话文运动, n. d.)

The relationship between the Taiwanese church and the LGBTQ movement has long been characterized by tension and conflict, particularly becoming more pronounced in the late 2010s. Many believe that the church only began to voice organized opposition to LGBTQ issues following the 2011 自真爱联盟 incident, but in reality, the interaction between the church and gender/LGBTQ movements has a long history; it only entered a more extensive and systematic phase of collective action afterward. (风传媒, 2020; 关键评论网, 2016)

The opposition of Christian groups in Taiwan to LGBTQ rights can be observed through several key events. In 2011, 自真爱联盟 launched its first large-scale actions against gender equality education, which is seen as the starting point for church groups publicly gathering to oppose gender equality. In 2013, the 护家盟 went further and organized the public to rally on 凱道, expressing opposition to marriage equality. In 2014, the Taiwan Presbyterian Church officially adopted an “Anti-Same-Sex Marriage Pastoral Letter,” providing a theological basis for the church’s position on LGBTQ marriage issues. (风传媒, 2020; 基督教今日报, 2011; Wikipedia, n. d.; 台湾基督长老教会总会, 2014)

By 2015, Christian groups formed the “信心希望联盟” political faction, formally entering the central representative elections in an organized manner, bringing their opposition to LGBTQ rights into the political realm. In 2016, five presbyteries from the southern region formed the “南部大会” and participated in actions surrounding the Legislative Yuan, expressing strong opposition to the marriage equality bill. On December 3, 2016, Christians even participated in a cross-religious rally led by 护家盟 titled, “One Million Families Stand Up! Marriage and Family, a Decision for All.” In January 2017, various presbyteries took measures to impeach regional legislators who supported modifications to the civil code, attempting to obstruct the legislative process of marriage equality. In the same year, the “61st General Assembly” passed a motion requiring the Taiwan Church News Agency to follow the opposition to same-sex marriage in its reporting, reflecting the control of church organizations over internal media. (Wikipedia, n. d.; 关键评论网, 2016; 内政部, 2015; 自由时报, 2015; 台湾基督长老教会总会, 2016; 基督教今日报, 2016; 公视新闻网, 2016; 沃草, 2017; 台湾基督长老教会总会, 2017)

Historically, the Christian attention to LGBTQ issues and its responses have often been closely related to specific social events. The emergence of 同光教会 around 1996, the Taipei Pride Festival starting in 2000 and LGBTQ civil movement, as well as the release of Ang Lee’s “Brokeback Mountain” in 2005-2006, which won international awards, all triggered reactions from the church community. In particular, the Taiwan Pride Parade themed around “family” further stimulated Christian groups to defend “family values.” (风传媒, 2020; Wikipedia, n. d.; 法律白话文运动, n. d.)

Since the 自真爱联盟 incident in 2011, the church and gender movements have exhibited a state of “mutual mobilization,” where actions from one side often prompt responses from the other. This interaction model has persisted from 2011 through the 2013 护家 movement, the local elections involving both sides in 2015, and continuing through the same-sex marriage legislation and 民间爱家公投 events from around 2016 to 2018, influencing Taiwan’s social discussions and practices on gender equality. (风传媒, 2020; Wikipedia, n. d.; 关键评论网, 2019)

The contradictory relationship between the Taiwanese church and the LGBTQ movement not only reflects profound societal divisions on gender issues but also showcases the tension between religious beliefs, traditional values, and equality movements. Church groups advocate for the preservation of traditional family values and faith positions, whereas the LGBTQ rights movement fights for equal rights and social recognition.This tension is not only reflected in street protests and public discourse but also extends to political participation and the influence on the legislative process, becoming an important and profound public issue in Taiwan’s democratic society. (风传媒, 2020; 关键评论网, 2019; Wikipedia, n. d.) (OPH)

  1. The 150th Anniversary Celebration of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church

As the largest Christian denomination in Taiwan, the Taiwan Presbyterian Church celebrated its 150th anniversary of missionary work in Taiwan in 2015 with a series of grand events to commemorate this historically significant milestone, which has survived through the Qing Dynasty, Japanese rule, and the government of the Republic of China.

The celebration activities centered around regional thanksgiving services across Taiwan. On April 5, 2015, Easter Sunday, over 15,000 people gathered at the Lin Kou Sports Center in New Taipei City for the northern regional thanksgiving service. On June 21, the southern five presbyteries (Chiayi, Tainan, Kaohsiung, Shoushan, and Pingtung) held a joint thanksgiving service at the Kaohsiung Hanshin Arena, with simultaneous events in Hualien’s 德兴小巨蛋 and Taitung Sports Complex, drawing a total of about 26,500 participants. (台湾基督长老教会总会, 2015b)

On October 25, the “Passing the Torch Sending Service” was held at the Tainan City Sports Center, where parade participants from three routes came together, uniting thousands of attendees. The celebration of the 150th anniversary of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church’s missionary work not only demonstrated the church’s profound impact on Taiwanese society but also highlighted its core values of diversity, inclusiveness, and care for the land. Through various celebration activities, the Presbyterian Church reexamined its past missionary footprint while looking forward to its future missionary mission. (OPH)

  1. The Development of Struggles between the Christian Conservative Movement in Taiwan and Gender Issues

The Christian conservative movement in Taiwan, as an emerging counterforce, centers its discourse on marriage, sex, and family, challenging the achievements of Taiwan’s gender equality movement over the years and sparking a culture war. This movement emphasizes the significance of heterosexual marriage and family as traditional norms, firmly opposing non-heterosexual forms of marriage, family structures, and parenting methods.

In 1996, Taiwan’s first same-sex exclusive church, “同光教會” (Tongguang Church), was established. However, by the late 1990s and early 2000s, the Christian-background “台灣走出埃及輔導協會” (Taiwan Exodus Counseling Association) and several non-profit organizations led the Christian community’s attitudes towards homosexuality. These organizations viewed homosexuality as a personal illness, believing that it could only be “healed” through the power of the Holy Spirit, and provided religious guidance, psychological counseling, and emotional support for those troubled by their sexual orientation. (Wikipedia, n.d.; Legal Language Movement, n.d.; Taiwan Exodus Counseling Association, n.d.)

Since 2011, the Christian-based “真愛聯盟” (Alliance for True Love) has actively opposed the inclusion of LGBTQ rights content in elementary and secondary school curricula by participating in public hearings, lobbying the Ministry of Education, and requesting legislators to sign petitions. Prior to the amendment of same-sex marriage laws, there had been multiple conflicts between the two sides: in 2000, Christian groups opposed Taipei City’s support for the “LGBT Civil Movement”; in 2006, they advocated for introducing a “consultation period” and “reflection period” for abortion; in 2011, the “真愛聯盟” opposed the implementation of gender equality education guidelines; on November 30, 2013, pastors opposing homosexuality jointly promoted a large-scale parade, firmly opposing the amendment of Civil Code Article 972, against legislation regarding diverse families, and insisting on a monogamous marriage. Conveners included 楊寧亞 (Yang Ningya), 周神助 (Zhou Shenzhu), 張茂松 (Zhang Maosong), 寇紹恩 (Kou Shaoen), and 牧師夏忠堅 (Pastor Xia Zhongjian), with 牧師劉群茂 (Pastor Liu Qunmao) serving as the Secretary-General, alongside 副總幹事 (Deputy General Secretaries) 顧其芸 (Gu Qiyun), 張振華 (Zhang Zhenhua), 陳志宏 (Chen Zhihong), and 張復民 (Zhang Fumin) each responsible for different affairs. These events accumulated conflicts between gender activists and Christian groups, foreshadowing the intensity of future debates over “marriage equality.” (Wikipedia, n.d.; Storm Media, 2020; Christian Today, 2011)

The position of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan on this issue is quite complex. In 2004, the church published the “Report on Research Projects Regarding Homosexuality,” expressing an open attitude towards understanding the LGBTQ community. However, in April 2014, the General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church passed a pastoral letter against same-sex marriage, prompting protests from the “Presbyterian Youth Front” and 同光同志長老教會 (Tongguang LGBTQ Presbyterian Church), arguing that the church should walk alongside the marginalized rather than join the oppressors. In November 2016, the General Assembly held a meeting stating that whether in support of or opposition to same-sex marriage, everyone is part of the church and should respect each other, with no preset position on modifying the Civil Code or establishing a separate law. (Taiwan Presbyterian Church General Assembly, 2014; Wikipedia, n.d.; Taiwan Presbyterian Church General Assembly, 2016)

In 2012, Taiwan’s theological seminary held a workshop titled “Christian Attitudes and Perspectives on Homosexuality,” where although theological professors had different interpretations, they unanimously agreed that discussions on homosexuality must start from the Bible and require broader dialogue. In 2014, the Presbyterian Church’s “Gender Justice Committee” held a care seminar, calling on pastors to first understand and accept the LGBTQ community before discussing love and care. (OPH)

  1. The Dispute between Christianity in Taiwan and The Church of Almighty God

After entering Taiwan at the end of 2012, the Church of Almighty God (also known as “Eastern Lightning” or “New Song Church”) has generated severe conflicts with mainstream Christianity in Taiwan. This dispute mainly revolves around two aspects: doctrinal differences and evangelistic methods, leading to unprecedented united actions within the Taiwanese Christian community. (Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum, 2013)

The core doctrines of the Church of Almighty God fundamentally diverge from orthodox Christian beliefs. The church denies the authority of the Bible, asserting that it is outdated and has been replaced by its own writings such as “The Word Appears in the Flesh,” directly challenging the basic Christian stance that considers the Bible to be the highest authority. In terms of divine perception, the Church of Almighty God claims that “Almighty God” is the second incarnation in female form, completely opposing the core doctrines of the Christian Trinity and the belief in Jesus Christ as the sole Savior. Its Christology is viewed as a severe distortion, as the church believes that Jesus only completed the work of redemption, while the “female Christ” of the last days can fulfill judgment and purification, which is regarded as a serious theological error by the Christian community. (Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum, 2013; Wikipedia, n.d.)Aside from the doctrinal disputes, the development methods of the Church of Almighty God in Taiwan have also provoked strong dissatisfaction. It has been accused of using infiltration strategies, posing as seekers or Christians to enter churches, secretly contacting and recruiting believers, particularly targeting those with less stable faith foundations. In the early stages of evangelism, they often concealed their true identities and doctrines, gradually steering connections towards conversion only after relationships were established, which has drawn criticism for its deceptive practices. Moreover, the Church of Almighty God has extensively placed large advertisements in mainstream newspapers, disseminating content that has been criticized as biased and distorted, confusing the public’s understanding of orthodox Christianity. In December 2019, it was further alleged that they impersonated the Keelung Baptist Church on Facebook by creating a page under its name, marking the first direct case of misusing a specific church’s name, which raised greater awareness. (Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum, 2013; Taiwan China Christian Cross-Strait Exchange Association, n.d.; Christian Today, 2019)

In response to the challenge posed by the Church of Almighty God, the Christian community in Taiwan has taken unprecedented united action. On February 25, 2013, various Christian denominations gathered for a joint meeting at the Taipei Bread of Life Church, a rare occurrence in the history of Christianity in Taiwan. During the meeting, a total of 56 major Christian denominations, churches, seminaries, institutions, and media in Taiwan jointly signed a statement categorically designating the Church of Almighty God as a “cult and heresy.” This joint statement was published as a full-page advertisement in six major newspapers (United Daily News, China Times, Liberty Times, Apple Daily, Commercial Times, Economic Daily News) on March 2, 2013, publicly condemning the Church of Almighty God and demonstrating an unprecedented unified stance among the Taiwanese Christian community on this issue. (Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum, 2013)

On the practical action front, churches have strengthened biblical truth teaching and pastoral care for believers, hosting various thematic lectures and training to help believers root themselves in pure faith and enhance their ability to discern heresies. The churches have also actively monitored and reported in online spaces, such as the Chinese Christian Internet Development Association calling on believers to jointly report counterfeit church pages to prevent more people from being deceived. (Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum, 2013; Christian Today, 2019)

This controversy is essentially a battle for the preservation of doctrinal purity. The Taiwanese Christian community views the Church of Almighty God as a serious threat to orthodox faith, thus breaking denominational boundaries and undertaking unprecedented united action to clearly cut off and prevent its influence from spreading. This cross-denominational solidarity is extremely rare in the history of the Taiwanese Christian community, reflecting the severity of this controversy. The conflict between the Church of Almighty God and the Taiwanese Christian community involves not only fundamental theological differences but also concerns about evangelistic ethics and social responsibility. This has sparked such a strong and unified response from the Taiwanese Christian community to protect believers from what they perceive to be harmful doctrinal influences. (Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum, 2013) (OPH)

  1. Indigenous People as the Spiritual Firstborn of Taiwan

This concept emphasizes the unique spiritual status and mission that the indigenous peoples of Taiwan, as the earliest residents, may possess. (Christian Today, 2018). The emergence of this idea is closely related to the early spread of Christianity in Taiwan. Local churches and indigenous Christian leaders have gradually adopted and promoted this notion. (Christian Today, 2018).

In 2017, indigenous representative Pastor 賴安淋 (Lai Anlin) proclaimed during a prayer meeting that indigenous people will assume their “firstborn” status. (Christian Today, 2022). Elder 章啟明 (Zhang Qiming) from the David’s Tabernacle Prayer Center explicitly pointed out that Taiwanese indigenous people are considered the firstborn among the Austronesian language family. (Kingdom Prayer Network, 2025). Pastor 吳大衛 (Wu David) has also repeatedly referred to indigenous people as “spiritual firstborns.” (Christian Today, 2018). The popularity of this concept aligns with the rise of local consciousness in Taiwan and the development of the indigenous rights movement.The notion of “Indigenous People as the Spiritual Firstborn of Taiwan” has a wide range of applications, primarily reflected in the following aspects: promoting national reconciliation and unity: this concept is often used to foster solidarity and reconciliation between indigenous and Han communities within the Christian faith, emphasizing the importance of “walking together”; empowering indigenous people with dignity: many indigenous Christians embrace this statement, viewing it as a source of strength, dignity, and spiritual significance, which helps enhance ethnic pride; highlighting Taiwan’s unique spiritual role: some believe that restoring the “firstborn” status of indigenous people is key to Taiwan’s revival and can lead the gospel from the Austronesian language family back to Jerusalem; supporting indigenous rights movements: this concept may also provide a spiritual and moral foundation for advocating indigenous rights, self-determination, and historical justice, emphasizing their priority status in Taiwan’s history and culture; connecting Austronesian narratives: as Taiwanese indigenous people are viewed as the origin of the Austronesian peoples, this concept also implies their potential spiritual leadership within the broader Austronesian world. (Christian Today, 2022).

Despite the widespread acceptance of this concept in the Taiwanese Christian community, there are also differing perspectives and criticisms. Some Christian commentators express caution about the simplistic application of the “firstborn” metaphor and question who has the authority to grant such significant status. (Christian Forum, 2020). Within the indigenous community, there are varying views on this concept, with some expressing skepticism about its practical benefits in addressing ongoing inequalities. (Namoh Ising, 2015). There are concerns that this notion could create a superficial sense of spiritual superiority without addressing actual needs and rights. Church of Almighty God (Eastern Lightning)

Church of Almighty God (Eastern Lightning)

The Church of Almighty God is a religious organization originating from mainland China that has drawn widespread attention and controversy since the 1990s. The organization is based on Christianity but has developed unique theological views and structures, which are regarded as heretical or cult-like by the Chinese government and most Christian denominations. (Wikipedia, n.d.; ETtoday News Cloud, 2020; BBC News Chinese, 2014; People's Daily, 2014; Phoenix Network, 2012)

The formation of Eastern Lightning can be traced back to 1989 when Zhao Weishan, a former member of the "Shouting Sect," and several followers established the "Yongyuan Church" (also known as "Lord of Power") in Harbin, Heilongjiang. In 1991, Yongyuan Church was designated as an illegal organization by the Chinese government and was shut down, leading to Zhao Weishan being wanted. To evade capture, Zhao Weishan fled to Ruyang, Henan, where he met a woman known as "Female Christ," Yang Xiangbin. Through Yang Xiangbin, Zhao Weishan established the later Church of Almighty God and its control system. (Wikipedia, n.d.)

Since its establishment in Henan in the 1990s, the organization has rapidly developed. Reports indicate that by 2004, apart from Tibet, the church had established a presence in various provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China. The number of followers is difficult to determine accurately, with media reports varying from tens of thousands to millions. In 2000, leader Zhao Weishan arrived in the United States and was granted political asylum by the U.S. government in 2001, subsequently establishing headquarters in New York. Besides mainland China, the organization is also active in over 20 countries and regions including Hong Kong, Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, the United States, and Canada, vigorously developing online evangelism. (Wikipedia, n.d.; Voice of America, 2014)

The core mission of Eastern Lightning is to claim that Jesus Christ has returned to the world in the form of a Chinese woman. The name "Eastern Lightning" is derived from Matthew 24:27: "For as lightning comes from the east and shines as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of Man." (Wikipedia, n.d.; BBC News Chinese, 2014)

The organization does not believe in the Christian doctrine of the Trinity but instead teaches a form of millennialism. They describe human history as "God's six-thousand-year management plan," divided into three stages: the Age of Law, the Age of Grace, and the Kingdom Age that began in 1991. In the Kingdom Age, they believe that God reveals all truth through the incarnation of Almighty God, dedicated to liberating humanity from sin. The revelations of Almighty God are primarily compiled in the book "The Word Appears in the Flesh," which serves as the main evangelistic book of the church. (Wikipedia, n.d.)

The organizational structure of Eastern Lightning is very tight, with "supervisors" responsible for overseeing regional and sub-regional leaders, who in turn supervise local church leaders. Members hold weekly "fellowship meetings," which are held in private residences in China and in "community centers" abroad. Since 2001, the organization has begun focusing on online evangelism, appearing on social media. (Wikipedia, n.d.)

Eastern Lightning has also been accused of holding strong anti-communist views, believing that the "great red dragon" mentioned in Revelation represents the Chinese Communist Party. Chinese media have described Eastern Lightning as "the most dangerous cult." The organization has been linked to several violent incidents, including the 1998 Nanyang attack, the large-scale kidnapping accusations in 2002, the doomsday riots in 2012, and the high-profile murder case of Wu Shuangyan in 2014. In the Wu Shuangyan case, although the Chinese government linked the assailants to Eastern Lightning, church representatives denied any involvement, and some scholars have questioned whether the assailants actually belonged to different groups. Due to these controversies, the People's Republic of China has listed the Church of Almighty God as a cult since 1995. However, members of the organization deny all accusations, arguing that they are victims of religious persecution by the Chinese authorities. (Wikipedia, n.d.; BBC News Chinese, 2014; People's Daily, 2014; Phoenix Network, 2012)

Eastern Lightning has not only captured government attention but has also faced strong opposition from major religious groups, with mainstream Christian denominations generally viewing it as heretical. In 2013, the Vatican Media "Today Vatican" warned global Catholics to be wary of the Church of Almighty God, alleging that it employs violent and kidnapping methods. In the same year, the Water Flow Ministry condemned the Church of Almighty God for misappropriating the ministry of Li Changshou, while the Christian Journal also joined various Protestant denominations in Taiwan to label it as a "cultic heresy."

Missionary Guo Meijiang accused Eastern Lightning of abducting a large number of followers in Taiwan, while the Taiwan Chinese Christian Cross-Strait Exchange Association stated that the organization infiltrates Taiwanese churches using money and sexual means. (Wikipedia, n.d.; Christian Today, 2013; Christian Forum Journal, 2013; Taiwan Chinese Christian Cross-Strait Exchange Association, n.d.) (OPH)

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

Taiwan did not experience a comprehensive anti-Christian movement between 2000 and 2020, but there were indeed specific social controversies on certain issues, with some Christian groups becoming the focus of criticism. These controversies mainly revolved around specific social issues rather than Christian beliefs themselves, reflecting the tension between religious values and secular perspectives.

The most prominent controversy occurred around the issue of marriage equality. As awareness of gender equality increased in Taiwanese society, some conservative Christian groups actively participated in the movement against the legalization of same-sex marriage. These groups expressed their stance on preserving traditional family values through the formation of alliances (such as 護家盟), organizing parades, holding rallies, and promoting referendums. This led to strong opposition from the pro-marriage equality camp, who criticized these Christian groups for attempting to place religious doctrine above secular law. This social movement became the largest scale of public dissent against specific Christian groups during this period, although the focus of the controversy was on specific social actions rather than Christian beliefs themselves.

Another phenomenon that attracted attention was the conflict with groups such as “全能神教會,” which are viewed as “cults.” These organizations, originating from mainland China and defined as heretical by mainstream Christianity, raised alarms with their activities in Taiwan. Mainstream Christian churches adopted a cautious attitude toward these groups, believing that their doctrines deviate from the Bible and that their proselytizing methods may involve deception. Although this situation mainly concerns the definition of heresy within Christianity, it also impacts the public image of “Christianity” in the minds of some people.

Additionally, discussions regarding the role of religion in schools and the public domain occasionally arose. Issues included whether schools should incorporate religious education and the appropriate boundaries for religious groups in campus evangelism, reflecting the exploration of balance between secularism and religious freedom.

At the same time, some former members or commentators criticized the internal operations, power structures, or doctrinal interpretations of certain churches. In the internet age, these voices can draw attention and pose challenges to church systems or traditional views.

In summary, between 2000 and 2020, while Taiwan did not have a comprehensive anti-Christian movement, there was indeed significant opposition to specific social actions by certain Christian groups on issues like marriage equality, reflecting the competition and dialogue of diverse values in Taiwanese society. These situations reflect opposition to “specific Christian practices” or “actions of specific Christian organizations,” rather than a broad and organized “anti-Christian movement.” (OPH)
