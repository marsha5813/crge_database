Taiwan: Agency (1990-2000)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Shan Kuo-His (單國璽) (Paul Shan Kuo-His, S.J., 1924/12/2-2012/8/22)

Cardinal of the Catholic Church and Jesuit. He was the first Catholic clergyman in the history of the Taiwanese Church to be appointed a cardinal while serving as the bishop of a Taiwanese diocese. Born in Puyang City, Henan Province, China, he passed away in New Taipei City, Taiwan, at the age of 88.

Both of his parents were devout Catholics, deeply influencing his faith development. He joined the Jesuits in Beijing in 1946 and was ordained a priest in Baguio, Philippines, in 1955. From 1959 to 1961, he studied spirituality at the Pontifical Gregorian University in Rome, earning a Doctorate in Theology. After obtaining his degree, he was assigned by his order to serve at the Thủ Đức Jesuit Novitiate in Vietnam.

In 1963, he came to Taiwan and served as the president and novice director of the Jesuit Changhua Jing Shan College and Novitiate. In 1970, he became the principal of Taipei Catholic Xu Hui High School, and in 1976, he was appointed president of Guangqi Society. In 1980, he was appointed as the bishop of Hualien by the Vatican, and in 1991, he transferred to the bishopric of Kaohsiung. During this period, he also served as the president of the Taiwan Bishops’ Conference (1987-2006) and chairman of Fu Jen Catholic University (1990-1993, 1999-2006). In January 1998, he was appointed a cardinal by Pope John Paul II and was allowed to retire in 2006.

Throughout his pastoral career, he engaged in education and mass communication work, completing significant infrastructure development while serving as president of Guangqi Society, laying the foundation for its growth. During his time as a cardinal, he participated in religious dialogue and mass communication efforts, contributing to the stabilization of Taiwan-Vatican relations. (Shan Kuo-His, 2012)

  1. Shia Chung-Chien (夏忠堅) (Rev. Shia Chung-Chien, James, 1948/9/10-)

A native of Qimei in the Penghu Islands, Taiwan. His ancestors, Xia Zai (夏宰) and Xia Chuan (夏傳), played key roles in bringing the Gospel to Qimei. His father, Xia Yu (夏禹), was the first preacher in Qimei to receive theological education and devoted his life to serving churches in Penghu and other parts of Taiwan. Due to poverty, he and his brother were sent to a children’s home on the main island of Taiwan in 1957, where they lived until university.

He has stated that his experiences in the children’s home profoundly impacted his faith life. He attended the Chinese Department at the private Chinese Culture University for his university studies and once strayed from his faith. Later, he was deeply moved by the sermon of Elder Wu Yong at Nanjing East Road Church and decided to dedicate his life to full-time ministry. In 1974, he joined the Campus Evangelistic Association and participated in church growth work, promoting the “I Found It, You Can Find It Too” campaign, which attracted public attention through advertisements, encouraging society to engage with the Christian faith. In 1985, he joined the Li-Evangelical Church, and in 1990, he became involved in the “2000 Evangelical Movement,” serving as the Secretary-General of the Chinese Christian Evangelical Advancement Association (until 2021) and the Secretary-General of the Chinese Christian Relief Association (1998-2024), among other positions.

Shia Chung-Chien is one of the leaders of the contemporary church unity movement in Taiwan. The “2000 Evangelical Movement,” which he led, was the first cross-denominational missionary outreach in Taiwanese church history, promoting a “total mobilization evangelism” model that integrated churches across Taiwan to hold evangelistic meetings using the same materials simultaneously. The Evangelical Advancement Association also laid the groundwork for future church unity efforts in Taiwan. The Chinese Christian Relief Association he established prompted Chinese churches in Taiwan to begin engaging in social concern work. During the 921 Earthquake, he coordinated resources from various churches to assist in reconstruction efforts in the disaster areas. In addition to spreading the Gospel widely, he also expanded the influence of Christianity in society. (Ke Dao, n. d.; Senior Coach: Chinese Christian Evangelical Advancement Association, n. d.; Christian Weekly, n. d.) (OPH)

  1. Stephen Tong (唐崇榮) (Rev. Stephen Tong, 1940/3/22-)

An Indonesian Chinese Reformed theologian, evangelist, and founder of the Reformed Evangelical Church in Indonesia, he was born on Gulangyu Island, Xiamen, China. His father passed away when he was three years old, and at nine, he moved to Surabaya, Indonesia, with his mother, Chen Zhi-Niang (陳織娘), where he completed his primary and secondary education. Chen Zhi-Niang was a devout Christian, and her attitude toward faith deeply influenced Stephen Tong. Later, when Pastor Ji Zhiwen (計志文) came to Indonesia to hold a revival meeting, Stephen Tong decided to dedicate himself to preaching.

However, influenced by his education, he momentarily denied Christianity in favor of communism. After a period of introspection and struggle with his beliefs, he returned to faith.

From 1961 to 1964, he studied theology at the Southeast Asia Bible School in Malang, Indonesia. After graduating, he taught at seminaries in Indonesia, Hong Kong, and Taiwan and served as a pastor at the Surabaya Min Language Christian Church until 1979.1978, Stephen Tong (唐崇榮) established the Stephen Tong Evangelistic Ministry International (STEMI), conducting evangelistic meetings and Gospel lectures worldwide. In 1989, he founded the Reformed Evangelical Church in Indonesia (IREC). In 2008, he was awarded an honorary Doctor of Theology degree by Westminster Theological Seminary in Philadelphia, recognizing his long-standing contributions to the promotion of Reformed theology.

For many years, Stephen Tong has traveled across Southeast Asia, Hong Kong, and Taiwan, promoting Reformed theology through expository lectures and evangelistic meetings and establishing seminaries in Indonesia and Taiwan to train pastoral staff in Reformed theology. He is also an advocate of the “Reformed Evangelical Movement,” emphasizing the development of Reformed theology based on the biblical principles established by John Calvin (約翰．加爾文) during the Reformation movement. (Christian Weekly, n. d.; Taiwan Reformed Evangelical Television, n. d.; Reformed Evangelical Movement, n. d.) (OPH)

  1. Tin Gion-gun (鄭仰恩) (Rev. Dr. Tin Gion-gun, 1957-)

Born in 1957 in Taipei, Taiwan, Tin Gion-gun is the son of Professor Zheng Lianming (鄭連明) (1931-1977), a significant figure in the post-war Taiwanese church, who passed away at a young age while serving at Dongmen Church. His eldest son, Tin Gion-gun, studied history at National Chung Hsing University, and after graduation, entered Taiwan Theological Seminary, where he obtained a Master of Divinity degree in 1984. He later went to the United States, where he earned a Doctorate in Church History from Princeton Theological Seminary in 1994, after which he returned to Taiwan to work at the seminary. (National Central Library, 2016; Taiwan Theological Research Institute, n. d.; Christian Weekly, 2016; Central News Agency, 2016)

His research focuses on the history of Christianity, the history of Christian theological thought, the Reformation movement, the history of Christian social thought, and the history of Christianity in Taiwan. He has published numerous books, papers, and edited works of notable achievement. He has also held various positions at Taiwan Theological Seminary, including acting president, academic vice president, and dean of academic affairs, while also serving as an adjunct professor at National Taiwan University for many years. (Central News Agency, 2016; Christian Weekly, 2016; National Central Library, 2016)

In addition to teaching, he has served as the director of the Church History Center, collecting and constructing historical materials related to the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (PCT) and Taiwan Theological Seminary, making significant historical contributions to the church community. He has dedicated 37 years to Taiwan Theological Seminary until his retirement in 2022, after which he became the pastoral director of theological education at Jinan Christian Presbyterian Church. He has also participated actively in church affairs, holding key positions in the PCT’s Faith and Church Order Committee, the Committee on World Mission, and the Church History Committee. (National Central Library, 2016; Christian Weekly, 2016)

Beyond teaching and denominational work, Tin Gion-gun has been deeply involved in global affairs, holding numerous titles: he served as a member of the World Council of Churches (WCC) Faith and Order Commission (1998-2005), co-chair of the Commission on Mission and Unity of the Christian Conference of Asia (CCA) (1995-2005), convener of the Theological Commission of the Ecumenical Association of Third World Theologians (EATWOT) (2001-2006), president of the Asian Congress of Calvin Research (2011-2016), and a member of the Central Committee of the World Council of Churches (WCC) (2013-2021), among others. (Taiwan Theological Research Institute, n. d.; Christian Today, 2013)

Tin Gion-gun has become an influential figure in the international Christian community, playing a vital role in the global work of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan. He participated in drafting the prophetic declaration on economic and environmental justice, the Accra Confession (阿克拉信仰告白), by the World Alliance of Reformed Churches in 2004. He has also been involved in key dialogues and testimonies between the PCT and the Chinese Christian Council (CCC) within the WCC. Domestically, he has contributed to cultivating global leaders within the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, remaining active in the Presbyterian Church and the Taiwanese Christian community. (Christian Weekly, 2016; Taiwan Theological Research Institute, n. d.) (OPH)

  1. Lô͘ Chùn-gī (盧俊義) (Rev. Lô͘ Chùn-gī, 1946-)

Born in 1946 in Kaohsiung, he entered the Theological Department at Tainan Theological College in 1966 and graduated in 1974, volunteering to serve in the eastern part of Taiwan, assigned to Guanshan Church in Taitung.In 1979, during the “Kaohsiung Incident,” Lô͘ Chùn-gī (盧俊義), who had just arrived in Birmingham to study at Selly Oak Colleges, gathered with fellow pastors Huang Zhang-Hui (黃彰輝), Song Quan-Sheng (宋泉盛), Zhao You-Yuan (趙有源), and former vice presidents of Tainan Theological College, An Mu-Li (安慕理) and Mi Di-Li (彌迪理), in London to discuss response strategies. Lô͘ Chùn-gī was introduced to Amnesty International (AI) by Mi Di-Li, and together with Pastor An Mu-Li and his wife, who were proficient in Taiwanese, they translated documents, analyzed the situation, and collaborated with some Taiwanese students in London to initiate international rescue efforts through AI. (Church Bulletin, 2013)

Upon his return to Taiwan, Lô͘ Chùn-gī successively served churches in Chiayi (嘉義) and Taipei (台北) from Dongmen (東門) until his retirement in February 2013. He also served as an adjunct lecturer at Tainan Theological College, Taiwan Theological College, Yu Shan Theological College, Hsinchu Bible College, and Taiwan Baptist Theological Seminary. Throughout his pastoral work, he emphasized Bible study and sermon content, accumulating extensive notes on the Scriptures that were later published as study resources for the 66 books of the Bible. After retiring, he continued to hold Bible study classes in various churches, guiding lay members in reading the Bible. Additionally, he continued to write, with his articles appearing in print and various online platforms. (Christian Today, n. d.; Lô͘ Chùn-gī’s Office, n. d.)

Throughout his ministerial stages, Lô͘ Chùn-gī also participated in many social work initiatives, such as serving as an instructor at Kaohsiung Juvenile Rehabilitation Institute and Taitung Wuling Prison, and as a member of the committee for the construction of Taiwan’s first “228 Memorial Monument.” He established the “Care for Life Association” along with Buddhist monk Shi Zhao-Hui (釋昭慧) and Father Wang Jing-Hong (王敬弘) of the Catholic Church. (Church Bulletin, 2013; Care for Life Association, n. d.)

He has been involved in media evangelism, serving as the editor-in-chief of the Church Bulletin of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan from 1987 to 1993 during his pastoral tenure, and has long hosted programs on GOOD TV. After retirement, he has hosted several mainstream media programs, including “Taiwan Studies: These People, These Matters,” “The Story of Genesis,” “The Road to the Cross,” and “The Footsteps of Jesus’ Disciples in Evangelism” on Min TV. He has also collaborated with the younger generation to produce podcast programs like “Pastor Lô͘ Chùn-gī Discusses Proverbs,” “Pastor Lô͘ Chùn-gī Talks about the Gospel of Luke,” and “Pastor Lô͘ Chùn-gī Explains Genesis.” (Church Bulletin, 2013; Wikipedia, n. d.; SoundOn, n. d.; Apple Podcasts, n. d.)

As of now (2025), he continues to serve as a chaplain at Taipei Christian Hospital, member of the Ethics and Palliative Care Committee, and member of the Ethics Committee at Taipei Medical University Hospital, as well as hosting the program “These People Who Devote Their Lives to Love” at the Voice of Lutheran Radio Center. (OPH)

  1. Hong-Hsin Lin (林鴻信) (Rev. Dr. Hong-Hsin Lin, 1955-)

Born in Taichung City, Hong-Hsin Lin grew up in the Chung Hsiao Road Presbyterian Church, with parents Lin Dong-Hsun (林東薰) and Jiang Jin-Hua (江錦華), both elders. He studied at the Philosophy Department at National Taiwan University, and after graduation, he entered Taiwan Theological College, where he earned a Master of Divinity degree. He later went to the United States to study at Fuller Theological Seminary (M.A.) and Yale Divinity School (S.T.M.), and in 1990, he obtained a Doctorate in Theology majoring in Systematic Theology from Tübingen University in Germany, followed by a Ph.D. in Philosophy from Nottingham University in the UK in 1998. (Taiwan Theological Research Institute, n. d.; Tian Dao North America Online Bookstore, n. d.; Christian Weekly, n. d.)

Returning to Taiwan after his studies in Germany in 1990, he began teaching at Taiwan Theological College. His research focuses on Christian theology, modern Christian thought history, and the theological thoughts of Calvin, Luther, Tillich, Barth, Moltmann, Lewis, and Kierkegaard. He has held various positions, including Director of Church and Society, Dean of Academic Affairs, Acting President, President, Director of Theological Studies for Lay Leaders, Director of the Center for Christian Research, and Director of graduate programs, while also being an adjunct professor in the Philosophy Department at National Taiwan University. Lin Hong-Hsin has played a vital role at Taiwan Theological College for over 37 years and retired in 2022. (Christian Weekly, 2022)

Beyond institutional teaching, since 1993, he has led students at Taiwan Theological College in the Christian Elite Movement and Elite Camps, attempting to spark a revival movement among youth in the Presbyterian Church. Through these camps, he facilitated the subsequent establishment of the Elite Association, which enabled the first graduating class from Taiwan Theological College to be sent to Myanmar as missionaries.In 2020, approaching retirement, Hong-Hsin Lin (林鴻信) founded a Christian research think tank, aiming to explore the theoretical and practical applications of the fundamental beliefs associated with God’s Kingdom values. This initiative seeks to promote the dissemination, communication, and dialogue of these values in response to societal trends. Hong-Hsin Lin is not limited to the Presbyterian Church, actively connecting with various denominations and emerging churches, and remains involved in the Christian community in Taiwan. (OPH) (Christian Weekly, n. d.; Christian Research Think Tank, n. d.)

  1. Ya Hui, Yang (楊雅惠) (Rev. Ya Hui, Yang, 1963-2008)

Yang Ya-Hui graduated with a Master of Divinity from Tainan Theological College in 1989 and went on to study a Master of Theology at Chicago Theological Seminary in the United States in 1992. After graduating, she returned to Taiwan to serve at the Hsinchu campus of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan until leaving in July 1996. During this time, she called upon a group of gay Christians through the internet to establish Taiwan’s first Christian group for sexual minorities: the “Jonathan Fellowship” at Yiguang Presbyterian Church in Taipei. (Wikipedia, n. d.; Legal Commons Movement, n. d.)

On May 5, 1996, members of the Jonathan Fellowship established Taiwan’s first church for sexual minorities, “Tong-Kwang Light House Presbyterian Church,” at the Tamkang University Chapel, under the witness of Pastor Xu Cheng-Dao (許承道). Yang Ya-Hui was hired as the first resident pastor in August 1996 and served until July 1998 when she left due to facing a bottleneck in her pastoral work. (Tong-Kwang Light House Presbyterian Church, n. d.; Legal Commons Movement, n. d.)

Yang Ya-Hui subsequently served at Nuan Nuan Church of the Qixing Presbytery and at Zhongshan Church of the Changhua Presbytery. From 2006, she served at Jifeng Church in the Taichung Presbytery of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan until early the following year.

Her open attitude towards gay Christians was not acceptable within conservative Christian church circles in Taiwan. In early 2008, she reached out to the Taiwan Church News Society, hoping to publish her autobiography titled “The Female Pastor Carrying the Cross: Pastor Yang Ya-Hui’s Faith Journey.” However, her family noticed symptoms of unconsciousness in late April to early May and urgently hospitalized her. After being discharged, the Taichung Presbytery commissioned the student center of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan to provide special care for her. (Legal Commons Movement, n. d.; Wikipedia, n. d.)

On May 20, 2008, she submitted all manuscript materials for her autobiography to the Taiwan Church News Society and settled the publication fee before losing contact. When her colleagues visited her residence, they found her deceased at home. A memorial service was held for her at the Taichung Municipal Funeral Home on May 27, 2008, after which her body was cremated and buried by her family in the family cemetery. (Wikipedia, n. d.) (OPH)

  1. Daniel Rao (饒孝楫) (Rev. Daniel Rao, 1938-)

Daniel Rao was born in Beijing in 1938 and moved to Taiwan with his family in 1948. He committed himself to faith during his first year of university and enrolled in the Department of Architecture at National Cheng Kung University in 1959. During his studies, he experienced financial difficulties after his father’s business setbacks and was invited by missionary Wei De-Kai (魏德凱) to stay at the Tainan Olive Mountain Camp. There, he and another student from Cheng Kung University assisted Wei De-Kai in managing the camp while participating in Bible school activities that Wei had started in 1958. (Campus Evangelistic Fellowship, n. d.)

Rao graduated at the top of his class from the Department of Architectural Engineering at Cheng Kung University. Although he dreamed of becoming a distinguished architect, after completing his military service, he responded to God’s call, giving up the opportunity for a lucrative career to become a full-time minister. In 1965, he joined the Campus Evangelistic Fellowship, founded by David Chao in 1957, and began his evangelistic work among students. (Campus Evangelistic Fellowship, n. d.; Wikipedia, n. d.)

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

The Garden of Hope Foundation (勵馨基金會)

The Garden of Hope Foundation (勵馨基金會) was established in 1988 by American missionary Angie Golmon (高愛琪) and a group of Christians. It was originally founded in response to the issue of Taiwanese girls being trafficked. The foundation’s development can be divided into several important stages:

Early Stage: Halfway House Services (1988-1992)

This group of Christians recognized the needs of unfortunate girls and established Taiwan’s first halfway house—Hope Garden (勵馨園), initiating shelter services to provide a home for these girls.

Anti-Child Prostitution Movement Period (1992-1995)

The Garden of Hope Foundation (勵馨基金會) promoted the “Anti-Child Prostitution Jogging on Huaxi Street” social movement, mobilizing fifteen thousand people to participate in the grand parade on Huaxi Street, dedicating efforts to the anti-child prostitution movement. These efforts led to the passage of the Garden of Hope version of the “Prevention of Sexual Transactions Involving Children and Adolescents Act” (now the “Prevention of Child and Adolescent Sexual Exploitation Act”) by the Legislative Yuan in 1995. The foundation also supported the abolition of public prostitution in the Taipei City legal reform.

Girl Protection Action Period (1995-2001)

The foundation established the National “Girl Hope Network” and founded the “Dandelion Therapeutic Center,” offering counseling and therapy services to victims of sexual assault and their significant others.

Empowerment of Girls and Women Period (2001-2008)

Through years of frontline service, the Garden of Hope Foundation (勵馨基金會) recognized the deep-seated needs of its service targets, continuously voicing support and participating in gender justice advocacy. The foundation actively practiced the concept of “Empowerment” and initiated preparatory workplace assistance for women to connect to employment.

Co-Creating Gender Justice Period (2008-Present)

The Garden of Hope Foundation (勵馨基金會) joined global anti-violence actions and called for the establishment of the Asia Network of Women’s Shelters (亞洲婦女安置網路, ANWS). The foundation continues to advocate for and work towards ending all forms of gender-based violence and oppression in society, striving to create a society based on gender justice.

Since the 1990s, the Garden of Hope Foundation (勵馨基金會) has repeatedly organized various anti-child prostitution activities, symbolized by daisies as the representation of their commitment to protecting children. From halfway houses for girls to expanding services for domestic violence, sexual assault, and other forms of gender violence, they currently operate over 50 service locations in 15 counties and cities nationwide. Each year, they assist nearly 20,000 survivors of gender violence in escaping violent situations and starting new lives, becoming an essential advocacy organization for women and children’s rights in Taiwan. (OPH)

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The Gospel Movement in 2000:

The origins of this movement can be traced back to 1987, when the Taiwanese church community believed that as they approached the twenty-first century, the church in Taiwan should take action. Therefore, Pastor Xia Zhongjian (夏忠堅), who was serving at the Taipei Bread of Life Christian Church (台北靈糧堂), invited Pastor Zhou Lianhua (周聯華) and Pastor Gao Junming (高俊明), representing the Mandarin-speaking church and the Presbyterian Church respectively, to jointly invite various churches in Taiwan to participate in discussions. From November 30 to December 1 of the same year, the “Gospel Movement Seminar for the Year 2000” was held in Taoyuan. The attendees reached a consensus on cross-denominational cooperation to establish churches and spread the Gospel, setting specific targets to establish 10,000 churches in Taiwan, grow the number of believers to two million, and send out 200 cross-cultural missionaries by the year 2000. At the same time, they registered as a corporate body under the name “Chinese Christian Evangelical Alliance” (中華基督教福音協進會) to serve as a permanent organization to promote this movement.

During the movement, in addition to holding united evangelistic meetings across various churches in Taiwan using slogans like “Jesus is the hope for today’s people” and “Jesus loves you,” they also organized various large-scale gatherings targeting different demographic groups (young people, youth, women, etc.), church growth workshops, and promoted workplace evangelism, while assisting churches in developing related growth plans. Besides conducting evangelism and promoting church growth, the movement also conducted a church survey, publishing the “1989 Church Growth Report of Taiwan” in September 1990, and started a census every two years which was subsequently compiled and published. In terms of social welfare, the alliance established the “Chinese Christian Relief Association” (中華基督教救助協會) to collaborate with local churches in community relief work.

By the end of the movement in December 2000, the goal of sending 200 missionaries had been achieved (the number of overseas cross-cultural missionaries sent by Taiwanese churches reached 204 by 1999, and support for over 2,000 local missionaries overseas had been secured); however, the targets of two million believers and ten thousand churches were not met. In terms of the number of believers, it grew from 557,483 to 780,529, with a growth rate of 40%; the number of churches increased from 3,127 to 3,961, with a growth rate of 26.67%. After the movement ended, the Evangelical Alliance and the Relief Association continued their operations, assisting churches, developing ministries, promoting evangelism, and social relief efforts.

Overall, this movement marked the first large-scale cross-denominational evangelistic and unifying movement in the history of the Taiwanese church, as previously independent churches began to interact and collaborate on various ministries. Meanwhile, emerging denominations (such as the Bread of Life Christian Church) gradually gained a voice in the church community, becoming a formidable force that challenged traditional denominations (like the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan). (Huang Bohe 黃伯和, n. d.; Xu Shugeng 許書耕, n. d.; Chinese Christian Evangelical Alliance—Understanding Us 中華基督教福音協進會—認識我們, n. d.; Weng Jiayin 翁佳音, n. d.)

Key Theological Issues

Sino-Theology:

Sino-Theology has both broad and narrow definitions. The former generally refers to academic papers or works related to Christianity that are written in the Chinese language, tracing back to the Tang dynasty’s Nestorian period, encompassing all writings related to the Christian faith in ancient and modern Chinese; the latter refers to the studies conducted by scholars in the Chinese humanities and social sciences since the late 1980s concerning Christian beliefs, developing a theological discourse distinct from traditional church teachings. This theological movement began to take shape in the early 1990s, with representative figures including Professor He Guanghu (何光滬) from the Department of Philosophy at Renmin University of China and Liu Xiaofeng (劉小楓), who was a researcher at the Hong Kong Chinese Christian Culture Research Institute at the time. (Chinese Christian Culture Research Institute, n.d.)

Regarding the connotation of Sino-Theology, Li Qiuling (李秋零) believes that the publication of “Daofeng: Journal of Sino-Theology” by the Research Department of the Hong Kong Daofeng Mountain Christian Centre in June 1994 marks the beginning of Sino-Theology. Their “Reopening Statement” explains Sino-Theology as follows:

  1. To develop Christian theology and its culture using historical philosophical resources and social experiences from Chinese culture to form a Christian theological culture that embodies the style of Chinese thought and culture;

  2. To establish a theological discipline within the realm of Chinese thought, engaging in academic dialogue with Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, and various modernist ideologies; contemporary Chinese academia (especially in philosophy, sociology, history, political science, and cultural studies) is actively constructing its own academic space and paradigms, not merely aligning with Western academic interests; Sino-Theology should also create its own academic space and paradigms, making Christian theology a structural element of Chinese cultural thought and a component of the humanities;

  3. It is a common endeavor of Chinese religious scholars across various regions in the Chinese-speaking world (Mainland China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Malaysia, and the Chinese community in North America).

Liu Xiaofeng argues that Sino-Theology differs from past attempts to harmonize Christianity with Chinese culture through indigenization theology; instead, it focuses on individual faith existentialism and modernity issues as its awareness, rather than using Christianity as a tool for promoting nationalism or ethics. He Guanghu views Sino-Theology as a type of “mother tongue theology,” where “the theologian’s own mother tongue or primary language serves as the medium, drawing upon the existential experiences and cultural resources expressed in that language, primarily serving the users of that language,” thus breaking through the regional, class, or situational foundations emphasized by past contextual and indigenous theologies. (Chinese Christian Culture Research Institute, n.d.; Zeng Qingbao 曾慶豹, 2006; Chinese Christian Culture Research Institute, n.d.)

Anti-Christian Leaders

Ven. Shih Chao-hwei (釋昭慧法師, 1957/5/28-)

Her secular name is Lu Qiongzhao (盧瓊昭). She was born in 1957 in Yangon, Myanmar, and her ancestral home is Meixian, Guangdong Province. She returned to Taiwan in 1965. In 1975, she entered National Taiwan Normal University, majoring in Chinese Literature, with the highest score in her class. In the summer of 1978, during her junior year, she became a nun and graduated from the university the following year. In 1980, she received full ordination at Long Quan Temple in Kaohsiung, where she was given the Dharma name Rong Dao (融道) and the honorific title Zhao Hui (昭慧), studying under Master Yin Shun (印順法師). She has profound academic attainments, with specialties in ethics, Buddhist ethics, the study of Buddhist precepts, early Buddhism, sectarian Buddhism, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, and Tiantai studies, and has also lectured on the study of Tathāgatagarbha. She is currently a professor in the Department and Graduate Institute of Religion and Culture at Hsuan Chuang University (玄奘大學) and is the founder of the Buddhist Hongshi Academy. (Hsuan Chuang University, n.d.)

In the Buddhist community, Master Zhao Hui is seen as a reformer unafraid of authority. She is active in the feminist movement, supports the legalization of same-sex marriage, initiated the “Abolish the Eight Respect Methods” Buddhist reform movement, and has served as a key convener of the “Anti-Gambling Legalization Alliance.” Her social participation extends beyond the realm of religion, actively voicing her opinions on issues such as opposition to nuclear power, animal protection, environmental ecology, and human rights, authoring books and leading various social actions. She has led several nationally prominent social movements, among the most notable being the “Sifan” incident at the end of 1988, the “Guanyin Statue Incident in Da’an Forest Park” in 1994, and the “Buddha’s Birthday Holiday Movement” in 1999. (Concern for Life Association, n.d.)

The Da’an Forest Park Guanyin Statue relocation incident was a conflict with Christianity that took place between 1992 and 1994, marking a significant clash between church and state. The situation arose when the Taipei City Government began constructing Da’an Park, which involved demolishing buildings on the site while leaving the Guanyin statue in place, causing dissatisfaction among affected residents. Many of these residents were Christian believers, leading religious groups from both sides to pressure the city government regarding the status of the Guanyin statue, resulting in a three-way dispute. In June 1993, someone threw excrement and sulfuric acid on the statue, worsening the situation. In January 1994, the Taipei City Government notified the Daxiong Buddhist Temple that it must relocate the Guanyin statue by the end of March at the request of the council. The Buddhist community immediately launched a large-scale protest, with Master Zhao Hui and Lin Zhengjie (林正傑) staging a hunger strike in front of the statue to protest. Ultimately, the statue was donated to the Taipei City Government and retained in Da’an Park, but it was simply treated as an artwork in the park and religious rituals such as incense burning and worship could not be performed before it. (China Daily, 1994)

However, Master Zhao Hui should not be viewed solely as an “anti-Christian leader.” She has long devoted herself to inter-religious cooperation and dialogue; for instance, in June 2011, she attended the “Day of the German Churches” in Germany with representatives from the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan and engaged in religious discussions with German spiritual leader Father Gulen in Uzwil. She is often considered a “troublesome figure” in the Buddhist community because she dares to challenge authority, expressing differing opinions even in front of the highly respected Master Wei Jue (惟覺和尚). Although her reform ideas and social activities frequently provoke controversy, they have also promoted self-reflection and transformation of Buddhism within modern society. (Dharma Drum Culture, 2011)

In 2021, Master Zhao Hui received the 38th Taneichi Peace Award, recognizing her contributions to religious exchange, social justice, and environmental protection. As a Buddhist nun, scholar, social activist, and reformer, she exemplifies a new paradigm for religious figures engaging in contemporary social issues through her pursuit of truth and critical spirit, injecting new vitality into the development of Buddhism in modern society. (Taneichi Peace Foundation, 2021)

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

The Daan Park Guanyin Statue Incident: The Controversy of Religion and Public Space

In 1994, an important controversy arose in Taipei regarding religious symbols in public spaces, known as the “Daan Park Guanyin Statue Incident.” This nearly two-foot tall statue (with a total height of nearly three hundred centimeters including the pedestal) is the work of internationally renowned artist Yang Yingfeng (楊英風) and was initiated for installation in 1985 by lay Buddhist Qiu Huijun (邱慧君) of the Daxiong Monastery (大雄精舍). At that time, the landowner Mr. Lin Zongxian (林宗賢) unconditionally provided the land for the statue. The sequence of events is as follows:

In 1992, the Taipei City Government began land acquisition for the construction of Daan Park. The Abbot of the Daxiong Monastery, Master Ming Guang (明光法師), petitioned the city government to preserve the Guanyin statue. At that time, Mayor Huang Dazhou (黃大洲) issued a statement in September 1993, indicating that considering it was a work by Yang Yingfeng, and in order to maintain the artistic and cultural atmosphere, it was approved for preservation under the premise of donation for maintenance and management by the city government.

However, the nearby Christian Living Water Church expressed strong dissatisfaction, arguing that a public park should not have the color of any specific religion, leading them to protest to the city government. In June 1993, individuals even vandalized the Guanyin statue with excrement and sulfuric acid, escalating the conflict between the two sides. Under continued pressure, in January 1994, the Taipei City Government, at the request of the city council, notified the Daxiong Monastery that they must relocate the Guanyin statue by the end of March.

The Buddhist community immediately launched large-scale protests, including holding an event titled “Guanyin, Don’t Leave” in front of the statue. Master Zhao Hui (昭慧法師) and legislator Lin Zhengjie (林正杰) even staged a hunger strike in front of the statue to demonstrate their opposition. Master Xingyun (星雲法師) played a crucial coordinating role during this process, attempting to negotiate with Pastor Lin from the Living Water Church while also arranging a meeting with Mayor Huang Dazhou and Pastor Zhou Lianhua (周聯華) through Taipei City Councilor Jiang Shuoping (江碩平). Master Xingyun mentioned at that time that the Fo Guang Shan (佛光山) organization had over a million members, and mobilizing them could trigger a large-scale social movement. (The News Lens 關鍵評論網, 2016)

Ultimately, through negotiations, the incident was resolved in 1994, achieving four points of consensus:

The Guanyin statue was donated to the Taipei City Government.

The Guanyin statue was retained within Daan Park.

The statue was designated only as a piece of park art, prohibiting religious ceremonies.

The Buddhist community ceased all protest activities (Xiao Zishuan, 蕭子莙, 1995).

This controversy not only reflects the interaction between religious groups and the government but also highlights the sensitivity of religious symbols in public spaces and the issues of religious tolerance in a multicultural society. (OPH)
