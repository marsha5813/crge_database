Taiwan: Agency (1970-1980)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

Billy Franklin Graham (葛理翰, 1918.11.7-2018.2.21) was born in Charlotte, North Carolina, USA, and was a renowned evangelical preacher. He enrolled in Bob Jones University (鮑伯瓊斯學院) in 1936 but later transferred to the Florida Bible Institute (佛羅里達聖經學院) due to difficulty adapting to the strict fundamentalist atmosphere, and in 1940 he attended Wheaton College (惠頓學院). Initially from a Presbyterian background, he converted to Baptist while studying at the Florida Bible Institute and was ordained as a preacher by the St. John’s Baptist Association in 1939. In 1950, he established the Graham Evangelistic Association and began holding evangelistic meetings worldwide, becoming a prominent figure in international evangelicalism. In the 1950s, influenced by Graham’s relatively open and inclusive stance regarding doctrinal interpretation and his attitudes toward groups despised by fundamentalists, such as Catholics and Pentecostals, the “New Evangelicalism” began to take shape, which, although still part of the conservative faith community, was more open and resistant to anti-intellectualism. In 1956, he founded Christianity Today (今日基督教雜誌), a publication representing the New Evangelical position (Martin 2020: 259-260; Dong Jiangyang 2004: 32). Graham held a strong anti-Communist stance and became involved in Taiwan’s politics and diplomacy starting in the 1970s. When the Republic of China withdrew from the United Nations in 1971, he played a mediating role between Taiwan and Washington and interacted with figures like Henry Kissinger and Richard Nixon. After Chiang Kai-shek’s (蔣介石) death in 1975, Graham officiated a memorial service for him in Washington. From late October to early November of that year, he visited Taiwan to hold a large evangelistic meeting, originally planned to coincide with Chiang’s birthday, intending to celebrate it during the evangelistic event and organize a tribute team to visit the Presidential Office. However, Chiang passed away in April. Zeng Qingbao believes that “party-state Christians” close to the Kuomintang and Chiang utilized Graham’s influence for both evangelism and diplomacy, combining his evangelistic meeting in Taiwan with the birthday tribute to Chiang. Nevertheless, due to Graham’s distancing from fundamentalists, the “party-state Christians,” who maintained a fundamentalist theological basis, gradually drifted away from him (Zeng Qingbao 2016: 236-239).

Kao Chun-ming (高俊明, 1929.6.6-2019.2.14) was from Tainan, a pastor in the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, and the grandson of Kao Chang. He graduated from Tainan Theological College and studied at Selly Oak College in Birmingham, England. He served as the president of Yu Shan Theological College from 1957 to 1970. Between 1970 and 1989, he was the general secretary of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, during which the General Assembly issued three famous declarations: “The Statement and Suggestions of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan on National Affairs” (1971), “Our Appeal” (1975), and “The Human Rights Declaration of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan” (1977). The government of the Nationalist Party, which was still in its authoritarian phase, closely monitored these declarations’ discussions and aftermath, conducting recordings, surveillance, and threats, even intervening in the church meeting’s agenda discussions. As a general secretary and one of the drafters, Kao faced immense pressure from the government, even receiving offers to persuade him to resign and leave Taiwan. Fearing for his safety, he left a letter declaring his intentions. After the third declaration was issued, he was monitored by the government but received protection from missionaries Mr. and Mrs. Dan Whallon (Kao Chun-ming 2001: 262-263; Lin Fangzhong 2007: 249, 339). In fact, the government perceived the third declaration as a “Taiwan independence” stance, and on August 19, the Police Administration established the “819 Project” command center to negotiate handling strategies with relevant parties. The Police Department labeled Kao as a “conspirator,” claiming to induce his “repentance” and collaborating with “patriots” within the church to remove his position. If that failed, they suggested exiling Kao and his “followers” abroad or collecting evidence against him to take legal action (Huang Kexian 2021: 30). In 1979, during the Kaohsiung Incident, the government actively sought to capture related individuals, including significant figure Shih Ming-te (施明德), who evaded arrest for several days. During Shih’s escape, Kao and Elder Lin Wen-jen provided him with shelter. Kao was arrested on April 24, 1980, for harboring a criminal and was sentenced to seven years in prison, losing his civil rights for five years, but he was released early in August 1984.During his imprisonment, Pope John Paul II sent Monsignor Paul Giglio to visit him, highlighting the international attention to the matter (No Author 1982: 54-55; Kao Chun-ming 2001: 271-275). Kao Chun-ming stood courageously against the authoritarian government during the tumultuous 1970s in Taiwan, advocating for human rights, democracy, and the future of the nation, and he is still regarded as a symbolic figure of social justice within the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan. Additionally, he was deeply concerned about the evangelization of indigenous peoples and nurtured several indigenous preachers.

Wang Hsien-chih (王憲治, 1941.6.6-1996.3.29) was from Kaohsiung and was an Anglican priest who was baptized at Truth Lutheran Church in 1963. He graduated from Tainan Theological College in 1968 and taught there from 1975 to 1993. In 1973, he earned a Master of Theology from the General Theological Seminary of the Episcopal Church in New York; in 1978, he received a Doctor of Theology from Southeast Asia Theological Seminary, becoming its first theological doctor, with Gu Hansong as his advisor. Notable works include “The Natural View and Theological Significance of the Dao De Jing” and “Papers on Taiwanese Local Theology,” establishing him as an important scholar of indigenous theology. In 1977, the Presbyterian Church published the “Human Rights Declaration of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan,” which was approved at the 25th General Assembly the following year. Wang was hired as a theological consultant for this meeting, where he interpreted the church’s “Human Rights Declaration” to attending government officials from the perspective of democratic politics and Christian theology. Wang not only committed to the localization of theology but also participated in the democratic movement. In 1978, following the establishment of diplomatic relations between the United States and China, he was entrusted by senior officials of the General Assembly to attend a congressional hearing on the Taiwan Relations Act with Peng Ming-min and Huang Zhanghui in 1979. At a seminar held by the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan and the Asia Church Association’s theological committee in 1979, he officially proposed the term “Local Theology,” a contextualized theology centered on “local,” advocating that “local” refers to one’s hometown or people, while “land” refers to territory or natural resources. The combination of “local” and “sovereignty” constitutes a nation, which he used as the foundation for Taiwanese national theology (Zhuang Yatang 1997: 21-25).

Wang Richard Ching-hung (王敬弘, 1934-1999.2.21) was from Hankou, a priest and a Jesuit. He graduated from the Civil Engineering Department at National Taiwan University and decided to enter the priesthood in 1958, enrolling in a seminary. He studied at the Theological Seminary in Baguio, Philippines, from 1962. After being ordained in 1971, he became involved in the Catholic Charismatic Renewal Movement, becoming the first Chinese priest to participate in this movement after it entered Catholicism in 1967. However, during the 1970s, Taiwan’s Chinese Catholic bishopric initially took a reserved stance towards the Charismatic Renewal Movement, leading to doubts about Wang’s work. His practice of offering “release” prayers at the Kao Hsiung Institute of Technology was prohibited by Jesuit leaders. It was only when Zhang Chunshen explained to the bishopric about the movement, referencing the views of Francis Sulivan, a theology professor at the Gregorian University in Rome, that the bishopric accepted the Charismatic Renewal. The Jesuits did not change their negative impression of Wang and the movement until the 1990s, when Wang was engaged in research at Fu Jen Catholic University and lived alongside Jesuits there. Through close interaction and conversation, he alleviated many of their concerns and attracted younger Jesuits, such as Wu Ching-chi, Wu Po-jen, and Su A-yuan, to join the movement; these individuals became the main leaders of the Charismatic Renewal in Taiwan after Wang’s passing. Although Wang dedicated himself to promoting the Charismatic Renewal, emphasizing activities such as “baptism in the Holy Spirit” and healing prayers, his faith did not draw a strict line between the “present world” and the “next world” or neglect social participation due to a focus on the spiritual aspect. After he began engaging in “inner healing” prayers, he discovered that the cases of individuals receiving prayer and counseling encompassed various trauma narratives, including intergroup conflicts, ethnic tensions, and the unblessed marriages between external province people and native residents, which led him to understand these issues not only from a personal spiritual perspective but also to attempt to grasp the social structures behind them. In the late 1980s, he began to address human rights issues, advocating for the families of victims of the February 28 Incident and collaborating with various denominational and systemic Christians to organize commemorative services, exhibits, and discussions.The February 28 Incident was a sensitive topic at the time, and the bishopric explicitly prohibited priests and nuns from engaging in political activities, putting Wang Qinghong under significant pressure from both the party-state system and the bishopric. Wang also pointed out that the Catholic Church in Taiwan was primarily composed of émigrés, which made it easier to identify with the Kuomintang government and limited its understanding of the historical pain experienced by local Taiwanese people. He advocated that understanding the suffering of local people should be the starting point for spiritual healing. Given that some of his relatives had ties with the party-state, he was very aware of its various issues and problems, leading him to criticize the Kuomintang loudly during his tenure as president of the Kao Hsiung Institute of Technology, which resulted in insults from church members. Additionally, his “inner healing” services were seen as a way to address the lack of mental health resources in Taiwanese society before the 1980s. Starting in 1978, he collaborated with experts from the Huaming Psychological Counseling Center to integrate prayer and psychological counseling, which was reportedly quite effective (Lin Jing-yi 2018: 116-122; Jesuit Youth Apostolate Working Group 2004: 127-129, 284-286). Wang Qinghong’s contributions extended beyond promoting the spiritual aspects of the Charismatic Renewal Movement in Taiwan; they also encompassed structural social issues and sought social justice, demonstrating the holistic nature of his faith commitments and overturning the preconceived notion that the Charismatic Movement and social justice could not coexist.

Lin Chi-Ping (林治平, 1938.5.22-2024.4.27) was born in Changsha, Hunan, and moved to Taiwan in 1949. He was a historian of Christianity, a co-founder of the magazine Cosmic Light (宇宙光), the founder of the Graduate Institute of Religious Studies at Chung Yuan Christian University, and one of the Ten Outstanding Young Persons of the Republic of China. He graduated from the National Chengchi University with a master’s degree in diplomacy in 1966, with his thesis titled “Research on the Lidao Incident.” In 1973, veteran Christian writer Liu Yiling wrote to Lin Chi-Ping, asking him to revive Cosmic Light magazine to continue the Gospel magazine Lighthouse, which had ceased publication in 1970. Lin took on the founding work and operated the magazine through a cultural missionary approach called “Gospel Pre-Service,” which was innovative in the conservative Chinese church scene at the time. However, this went against Liu Yiling’s intended direct promotion of Christian beliefs, leading to skepticism from church leaders, including Wu Yong. Lin Chi-Ping believed that overt Christian symbols and direct critiques of Chinese culture were not beneficial for evangelism (Lin Chi-Ping 2023: 114-126). In addition to founding the Gospel Pre-Service magazine, Lin began leading the trend of Chinese Christian history research in Taiwan during the 1970s, publishing significant works such as “Christianity and China’s Modernization” and “Christianity and China” in 1970 and 1975, respectively. Lin’s focus during this period was to address the core issues of anti-Christian movements through the themes of indigenization and modernization. He thus shifted from research on Sino-British diplomatic relations to studying the history of the Chinese church. In “Christianity and China’s Modernization,” he cited examples like James Legge, Ding Weiliang, and Sun Yat-sen to illustrate the relationship between Christianity and China’s modernization, defending Christianity in the process. He argued that Christianity had not been treated fairly in the Sinological academic circles at the time, especially regarding its contributions, and pointed out that conflicts between Christianity and Chinese culture might stem from certain elements deeply embedded in culture. Therefore, he emphasized indigenization (Tao Feiya and Yang Weihua 2009: 169; Zeng Qingbao 2011: 19-25). Furthermore, he established a cultural and historical fellowship at his home to encourage Christian students to pursue studies in the humanities, inviting university students from humanities departments to read and translate Western books on Chinese Christian history and missionary memoirs. Participants included several scholars who would later become leaders in related fields, such as Li Jinqiang, Zha Shijie, Wei Waiyang, and Chen Yiping (Lin Chi-Ping 2023: 204). Lin played a significant catalytic role in the study of Chinese church history in China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan, and in 2007, he received the “Academic Achievement Award” from the Hong Kong Chinese Church History Research Community in recognition of his contributions and achievements in the field.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

China Evangelical Seminary (中華福音神學院)

The school originated from a suggestion by Clyde W. Taylor (泰勒), the General Secretary of the World Evangelical Fellowship, during a summer conference held by the Taiwan Missionary Fellowship at Sun Moon Lake in 1965, proposing that Taiwan should establish a seminary with graduate-level standards. The school was officially founded in 1970, beginning a three-year Master of Divinity program, with its first graduating class consisting of seven students. The first president was James Taylor III (戴紹曾), the academic dean was Zhao Tian’en (趙天恩), and the chairman of the board was Wu Yong (吳勇). In 1975, the first missionary department in Taiwan was established. Initially, the school borrowed facilities from the Baptist Bible Fellowship on Fulin Road in Shilin and the Holiness Church on Xingfu Street for its campus. In 1976, the board decided to find a permanent campus and began fundraising at the end of January, receiving a donation commitment of $12,000, breaking ground on Christmas. The Hua Shen Building on Dingzhou Road was completed and inaugurated in 1979, continuing until its demolition and reconstruction in 2024 (Wang Chunyi 2024; Li Muquan 2020: 10–13; Rubinstein 1991: 96–99). It was the first seminary in Taiwan to exclusively admit undergraduate graduates, primarily because at that time, seminaries became a second option for believers who could not enter colleges, aiming to prepare for future pastoral work. Some missionaries in Taiwan were not pleased to see the seminaries decline into such a situation (Rubinstein 1991: 96). The establishment of the seminary not only aimed to train knowledgeable youth to become preachers but was also linked to the context of the Taiwanese Chinese evangelical church’s desire to counteract liberal theology and the ecumenical movement. The seminary advocated for a non-denominational approach (some scholars argue it was anti-denominational), distinguishing itself from the seminaries established by foreign missionary societies and emphasizing localization; on the other hand, it also distinguished itself from the ‘liberal’ seminaries associated mainly with the Presbyterian Church and the World Council of Churches. From its inception, the seminary adhered to a conservative evangelical stance, particularly emphasizing “the inerrancy of Scripture” to counteract “liberalism,” while also serving as an alternative for conservative Christians to attend seminary. In 1973, Zhao Tian’en convened a meeting with Teng Jinhui, Wang Yongxin, James Taylor III, and Lin Daoliang, attempting to merge China Evangelical Seminary with the China Theological Institute in Hong Kong, but this plan was halted following an assessment by Wu Yong and others regarding the educational needs of both locations (Zeng Qingbao 2016: 245–257, 278–283; Lin Shuqing 2004: 15).

Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (臺灣基督長老教會)

During the 1970s, the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan experienced turmoil and crises due to events such as its withdrawal from the United Nations, the severing of diplomatic relations between Taiwan and the United States, the establishment of relations between China and the US, and the death of Chiang Kai-shek. In this climate, it issued three famous declarations. In 1971, it published the “Statement and Suggestions of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan on National Affairs,” initially co-drafted by Baptist Zhou Lianhua, Methodist T. Otto Nall, and Presbyterian Daniel Beeby, later joined by Lutheran Bishop Zhang Qitang, with Gao Junming serving as convener. It was intended to be released under the name of the “Church Cooperation Committee,” but various groups such as the Catholic Church, Methodists, Lutherans, and Anglicans were unwilling to sign. Gao Junming then brought the matter back to the Presbyterian Church for discussion, where modifications were made, and the declaration was issued by the Presbyterian Church on December 29, 1971. The declaration called for international attention to Taiwan’s human rights and right to self-determination, pointing out the danger of being annexed by a communist regime, while advocating for a complete re-election of central people’s representatives. Zhou Lianhua withdrew from the “Church Cooperation Committee” in protest due to the revisions made by the Presbyterian Church (Wu Changxing 2008: 284; Gao Junming 2001: 229–230). Anticipating government reactions at the time of issuing the declaration, the church leveraged its transnational religious network, sending the statement to religious leaders or organizations abroad, such as the Pope, the US State Department, the Canadian Presbyterian Church, and the Japan Christian Council, to garner international support and strengthen the legitimacy of the declaration (Huang Kexian 2021: 27). In 1975, the Presbyterian Church issued “Our Appeal,” addressing the government’s seizure of the Romanized Bible that year and urging the government to prioritize religious freedom, while emphasizing the importance of a positive interaction between the church and the state.In 1977, the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan published the “Human Rights Declaration of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan,” advocating that “the future of Taiwan should be decided by the 17 million residents of Taiwan,” and expressing the appeal to “make Taiwan a new and independent country.” That year, several public opinions and reports criticized the Presbyterian Church for advocating for independence and “jeopardizing the safety of the church and the nation.” The government deemed this declaration a clear reflection of pro-independence sentiments; on August 19, 1977, the National Security Bureau established the “819 Special Project” command center to enhance surveillance of the Presbyterian Church and Gao Junming. However, on March 31, 1978, the General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church publicly clarified, criticizing the aforementioned reports and interpretations as “not understanding the facts, failing to comprehend the background and motives behind the human rights declaration, leading to irresponsible reporting that severely harms the church’s reputation and may cause church divisions,” and pointed out that the background and motive for the declaration were to “remind President Carter not to sell out the human rights of 17 million people in Taiwan,” urging the United States not to ignore Taiwan’s existence in its eagerness to “normalize” relations with the Communist Party of China. The church clarified that “a new and independent country” was unrelated to independence, but referred to the identity of citizens of the Republic of China, “calling on the government to take effective measures to build an ideal nation” (No Author 1977; No Author 1978; Zeng Qingbao 2015: 312–316; Wang Xianzhi 1988: 414–418; Lin Jingshui 2003: 347–348; Zheng Muqun 2017: 68–72). However, later interpretations and readings by leaders and scholars of the Presbyterian Church suggested that this declaration could be seen as the beginning of the Church’s pro-independence stance, with the earlier “clarification” being a result of pressure from the times (Lin Fangzhong 2007: 116). Furthermore, in 1979, the Kaohsiung Incident erupted, with numerous pastors, members, and seminary students from the Presbyterian Church participating. Gao Junming was sentenced for hiding Shih Ming-teh, leading the Presbyterian Church to begin aligning itself with the opposition movement.

Cosmic Light Holistic Care Organization (宇宙光全人關懷機構)

Cosmic Light Magazine was founded in September 1973, with many of its founders being Chinese Christians who had traveled to the United States. The initial publisher was Han Shijun, with Liu Yiling serving as editor; he became the president in 1975 and was succeeded by Lin Zhiping in 1977. Initially, the organization used a corner of a Japanese-style bungalow on Xinsheng South Road in Taipei as its office. In 1974, Fu Jikuan and his wife Xiu Zelan offered a 16-ping room in a building on Roosevelt Road for Cosmic Light’s use, symbolically charging a rent of one New Taiwan dollar and donating the space. Under Lin Zhiping’s promotion, Cosmic Light Magazine began developing in the direction of evangelistic work, while also exploring the relationship between Christianity and Chinese culture, as well as the history of the Chinese church, publishing related books. The organization thus became an important center for literary publication within the Taiwanese church. Additionally, Cosmic Light placed great importance on care and counseling. In the early 1970s, Lin Zhiping established the “Ours Coffee House” in a derelict space called “Friendship Corner” at the site of Taipei’s Hsin-Yu Church, using it for youth care and counseling work. After the founding of Cosmic Light Magazine, it relocated to the office on Roosevelt Road. Lin Zhiping believed that psychological counseling in the community overemphasized the professional aspects of psychology while neglecting life values and dignity, merely helping seekers live in a tangible reality. Therefore, around 1972, Lin began advocating his “holistic concept” in the counseling field, attempting to incorporate religious values into counseling work. In 1975, Cosmic Light Magazine planned a “psychological counseling” column and began establishing the “Cosmic Light Counseling Center,” which would later become the precursor to the Cosmic Light Holistic Care Organization (Lin Jingshui 2003: 404–407; Hou Mingzhi 2012: 25–33; Lin Zhiping 2023: 137–142).

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The Translation and Publication of the Modern Chinese Version

The Bible Society published the Modern Chinese Version (現代中文譯本) in 1979, emphasizing the translation into contemporary language for easy understanding by modern people, especially those outside the church. In addition to striving for simple and readable language, this translation also symbolizes the collaboration and unity between the Catholic Church and Protestant denominations, which is significant for church unity in Taiwan and can be seen as a result of the Second Vatican Council (梵蒂冈第二次大公会议). Prior to 1970, under the promotion of the Taiwan Bible Society, the Catholic and Protestant churches had meetings to discuss a joint translation; however, difficulties in standardizing proper nouns hindered further progress. In October 1971, the work of translating and proofreading the Modern Chinese Version officially began, led by Xu Mushi (許牧世), with Luo Weiren (駱維仁), Zhou Lianhua (周聯華), Wang Chengzhang (王成章), and Jiao Ming (焦明) as translators, while Liu Xutang (劉緒堂), Chen Wei-tong (陳維統), Han Chengliang (韓承良), and Fang Zhirong (房志榮) participated in proofreading. The New Testament was published in 1975, with distinct editions for Protestant and Catholic versions; the only differences were that “God” was changed to “Lord” and “Holy Spirit” to “Holy Ghost.” The Archbishop of Taipei, Luo Guang (羅光), signed off on the Catholic translation and wrote the preface. In 1979, the complete Bible, both Old and New Testaments, was finalized and released, but the Catholic Church only accepted the earlier New Testament translation, as the deuterocanonical books were not included. However, fundamentalists like Wang Yongxin (王永信) strongly criticized the Bible Society’s collaboration with the Catholic Church, deeming it a manifestation of “modernist” beliefs, particularly pointing out that this translation could include the deuterocanonical books and thereby affect the authority of the biblical canon (Zeng Qingbao (曾慶豹) 2016: 323). The release of this translation faced severe criticism from the anti-union movement and fundamentalists who viewed the Catholic Church as “heretical,” reflecting the atmosphere of the era, where “anti-communist apologetics” and fundamentalist opposition to “modernism” were intertwined.

The Translation and Publication of the “Red Cover Bible”

The “Taiwanese Vernacular Bible Translation” (高陳臺灣白話聖經譯本), commonly known as the “Red Cover Bible” (紅皮聖經), is the only Taiwanese New Testament translated jointly by Catholics and Protestants and can be seen as a result of the church’s response to the Christian unity movement promoted by the Second Vatican Council. The Vatican II Council changed the relationship between the Catholic Church and other Christian groups, leading to collaboration in various fields, including Bible translation, joint prayers, and social services. However, the Catholic Church in Taiwan did not respond actively, focusing primarily on northern China in its “localization” efforts, neglecting the local Minnan and Hakka cultures. The Marian Fathers (瑪利諾會) can be said to be the earliest and most active Catholic group to engage and cooperate with the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan after the Vatican II Council, partly due to their usual use of Taiwanese in missionary work. Therefore, the Marian Fathers sought not to be satisfied with merely translating the Chinese Bible but hoped to translate the Taiwanese Bible, resulting in the “Red Cover Bible.” This Taiwanese version adopted the principle of “Functional Equivalence” and was completed in two phases. The first phase, from 1965 to 1967, involved a team of teachers from the Marian Fathers’ language school translating the four Gospels, after which they invited four scholars from the Presbyterian Church Bible Society - Wang Chengzhang (王成章), Cai Renli (蔡仁理), Guo Dielie (郭得列), and Zhang Dexiang (張德香) - to assist in proofreading. The second phase involved teachers Gao Jihuan (高積煥) and Chen Bangzhen (陳邦鎮) from the language school collaborating with Presbyterian scholars Luo Weiren (駱維仁), Zheng Liankun (鄭連坤), and Wang Chengzhang (王成章) to translate the remaining 23 books of the New Testament while revising the four Gospels, culminating in the completion of the “Taiwanese Vernacular New Testament” (臺灣白話新約譯本) in September 1972, commonly referred to as the “Red Cover Bible.” This version’s Romanized phonetics differ from the Xiamen dialect usually employed by the Presbyterian Church; it uses the predominant Zhangzhou dialect common in the Taichung region, chosen for its wider coverage and larger speaking population at that time. Regarding the coordination of translation terms between the Catholic and Protestant traditions, the “Red Cover Bible” adopts the Protestant term “Holy Spirit” instead of the term “Holy Ghost” used by both the Catholic and Presbyterian Church, with all names and place names using Protestant translations, and “Lord” being created by combining one character from each of “God” (上帝) and “Lord” (天主). Father Dai Daohui (戴道輝) remarked that “historically, this is the first time Christianity and Catholicism have patiently and humbly worked together to complete a Taiwanese New Testament over seven years” (Huang Yiying (黃懿縈) 2024: 185-198).

Original Holy Spirit Revival Movement in the Presbyterian Church

In 1972, the Presbyterian Church of the Atayal tribe in Tianpu, Hsinchu County, experienced a grassroots revival movement similar to the charismatic movement, which subsequently spread to Atayal communities in Hsinchu, Taoyuan, Yilan, and Taichung through circuit evangelism. Alongside occurrences of prophecy, healing, and other charismatic phenomena, this revival movement particularly emphasized “repentance,” with many prophetic messages pointing in this direction.According to a report by Canadian Presbyterian missionary Clare Elliot McGill (穆克禮), the prominent role of women in this movement was very evident as they led prayer meetings and organized revival evangelism. The religious phenomena displayed a continuity or syncretism with “pre-Christian” faiths, often claiming to see their own and others’ sins in “visions,” and proclaiming this in a manner akin to oracles, calling people to “repent,” sometimes including clergy among the targets. This created a certain degree of tension with the predominantly male clergy. However, these charismatic women generally complied with the system and did not intentionally challenge it. The emphasis on “repentance” in this revival movement resonated with the circumstances and crises faced by the Atayal people at the time, such as alcoholism, family disintegration, the collapse of traditional values, and even the selling of daughters into prostitution. At the same time, the prominence of women led to a transformation in identity and a reversal of gender traits, continuing the Atayal tradition of valuing women’s spirituality and enhancing the role of female laypeople in the church. This revival movement was not introduced by external Pentecostal or charismatic evangelists but was a spontaneous indigenous revival. Clergy who experienced spiritual renewal and the children of the previously mentioned charismatic women became leaders of a new wave of Spirit revival among the Atayal in the 1980s. This new wave of revival was more influenced by international evangelists and the global Pentecostal or charismatic movement, shifting back to a male-dominated leadership (Shi Suying (石素英) 2015: 128-133).

Self-Reliance and Mutual Aid Movement

Following the Baijia Movement, the General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan (臺灣基督長老教會) promoted the “New Century Mission Movement” from 1966 to 1971, which was subdivided into the “Three H Four Self Movement” (Three H stands for Cooperation, Mutual Aid, Training; Four Self for Self-transmission, Self-governance, Self-support, Self-initiation), “Five-Year Plan for Training Believers,” and “Gospel Spreading Movement.” It was divided into five units: Urban Evangelism Year, Rural Evangelism Year, Industrial Evangelism Year, Mountain Evangelism Year, and Overseas Evangelism Year. From 1971 to 1975, a new missionary project, the “Faithful Servant Movement,” was launched, aiming to reflect on the role of the church and presenting three basic spirits: the church’s mission in the current era, the image of the church as faithful servants, and the entrusted spirit of the church. In 1976, the General Assembly combined the two previous movements and introduced a three-year “Self-Reliance and Mutual Aid Movement.” The self-reliance aspect was further divided into faith and economic parts. In terms of faith, since the onset of the New Century Mission Movement, the church had gradually lost its traditional values and spirit due to inter-denominational collaboration. In response to this situation, the goal of faith self-reliance was proposed, hoping to strengthen the church’s understanding of its own faith tradition. In the economic aspect, this responded to the relative wealth of local churches compared to the presbytery and general assembly, aiming to restructure the church’s economic framework to achieve economic self-sufficiency. Regarding mutual aid, the focus was on improving ministry and service performance, hoping to enhance “unity” and “community” aspects, promoting “sister church relationships” between congregations, and moving towards the goal of unity between mountain and flatland churches (The Editorial Group for Understanding the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 2010: 21-23; Lai Shengwen (賴聖玟) 2012: 17-24).

The Three Major Declarations of the Presbyterian Church

In light of the United Nations’ resolution 2758 passed on October 25, 1971, which accepted the People’s Republic of China to replace Chiang Kai-shek’s seat in the UN, and following U.S. President Nixon’s announcement of a visit to China, the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan issued a statement titled “Statement and Suggestions Regarding National Affairs” on December 29, 1971, amidst a crisis atmosphere. This draft declaration was initially proposed by the Church Cooperation Association, but due to a lack of acceptance by a majority of denominations and churches, it was rejected one by one, leading to its publication solely by the Presbyterian Church. This declaration is divided into two parts: one expresses a desire to live in peace, freedom, and justice while rejecting communist rule; the other suggests reforming domestic policies to maintain international standing, emphasizing comprehensive elections for central people’s representatives in “free areas (Taiwan, Penghu, Jinmen, and Matsu)” before “national unification.” During this time, several Presbyterian leaders like Huang Zhanghui (黃彰輝) and Huang Wudong (黃武東), who were overseas due to the universal church controversies of the 1960s, initiated the Taiwanese self-determination movement. This movement began in the United States, leading to the establishment of nearly 30 local headquarters across North America, and extended to Europe, Japan, and Brazil, closely cooperating with Taiwan independence activists. In 1975, the government confiscated the Atayal and Taiwanese romanized versions of the Bible, while international circumstances became increasingly severe, and U.S. President Ford planned a visit to China.In the same year, the General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church convened the third “Church and Society” seminar under the “World Relations Committee,” discussing the national situation at that time. Participants presented “Our Appeal,” urging the government to respect religious freedom and return the newly translated vernacular Bible, as well as demanding permission to continue publishing the Bible in any language. At the same time, the Presbyterian Church called for the government to allow the church to join international church organizations to remedy Taiwan’s recent crisis in international political status. The full text was published on September 28 in the “Taiwan Church News,” and on November 18, the General Assembly adopted this declaration as the position of the church. On August 16, 1977, the Presbyterian Church issued the “Human Rights Declaration of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan,” advocating that “the future of Taiwan should be decided by the 17 million residents of Taiwan,” and calling for “making Taiwan a new and independent country.” Given that U.S. President Carter advocated for “human rights” as a foreign policy guideline in 1976, while the Chinese regime threatened to use force to annex Taiwan, the Presbyterian Church leveraged Carter’s “human rights” policy to remind the U.S. not to forsake the rights of the 17 million people of Taiwan. These three major declarations caused strong shock and dissatisfaction from the government, resulting in increasingly tense relations between political and religious entities (Huang Kexian (黃克先) 2021: 25; The Editorial Group for Understanding the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan 2010: 25-28).

Key Theological Issues

Rural Theology

Wang Xianzhi (王憲治) introduced the concept of “Rural Theology” in 1978 during an alumni continuing education program at Tainan Theological College. He formally used “Rural Theology” to represent the local theology of Taiwan at a meeting held by the Asian Christian Conference’s theological committee in Taipei in September 1979. In the 1970s, a group of cultural figures in Taiwan attempted to articulate rural literature using Taiwanese national consciousness, and the concept of “Rural Theology” reflected the zeitgeist of that era. Wang Xianzhi pointed out that Rural Theology is an effort to identify and construct Taiwan from a theological perspective, employing contextual theology methods to explore a path for Taiwanese theology based on Taiwan’s history and the unique circumstances of the Bible. Wang’s construction of Rural Theology employs concepts similar to Song Quansheng’s (宋泉盛) dislocation theology, viewing Taiwanese people crossing the Black Water River as the chosen people of God, akin to the Israelites crossing the Red Sea, striving for freedom towards the “promised land,” while referencing the historical experiences of the Israelites in the Bible—exiting Egypt, nation-building, falling, and rebuilding. He indicates that “Rural” refers to the hometown or the people, while “Soil” refers to land or natural resources. With the addition of “sovereignty” to “Rural Soil,” a nation is formed. Therefore, the “Rural Politics” espoused by Christianity prioritizes the people as the subject, choosing their government based on the “sovereignty” bestowed by God. Thus, the themes of Rural Theology are people, land, power, and God, interpreted through the lens of the 1970s context. Concerning “people,” he noted that under the authoritarian rule of the Kuomintang, Taiwanese people lost their human dignity and need “to become new creations in Christ,” receiving grace and courage from God for liberation. Regarding “land,” Taiwanese people have the right to determine the future of their land and welcome all ethnicities to participate in its development. He believes this focus on land distinguishes Taiwanese Rural Theology from Latin American liberation theology. As for “power,” he argues that Taiwanese people must learn lessons from history to avoid the corruption of power, particularly under foreign regimes, necessitating a theological analysis of the nature, structure, and use of power. Regarding “God,” Rural Theology critiques the deification of individuals, especially the sanctification of political figures or the idolization of power (Wang Xianzhi 1988: 20-22; Zheng Junhao (鄭浚豪) 2010: 369-375; Qiu Kaili (邱凱莉) 2021: 128-131; Zhuo Xinping (卓新平) 2007: 224-225). Fundamentally, Rural Theology closely ties to the situation in Taiwan during that time, particularly concerning the future of the country and issues of self-identity. Although it speaks of “Rural/Soil,” it also engages in political concern and the establishment of ideologies, serving as a liberation theology for the Taiwanese context. The identification with Taiwan’s rural nature stems from the tension between the Taiwan Presbyterian Church and the Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石) regime and its representation of the Greater China nationalism ideology, the people’s anti-colonial struggles, and the overall atmosphere following the lifting of martial law (Qiu Kaili 2021: 131).

Contextual Theology

In 1973, during his tenure at the Theological Education Fund of the World Council of Churches, Huang Zhanghui (黃彰輝) and Aharon Sapsezian (撒普塞茲安) jointly proposed the concept of “theologizing in context.” Huang Zhanghui argued that churches in the Third World first need to “de-contextualize” Western churches and their theology, and then “re-contextualize” their faith and theology within their own contexts to construct the subjectivity of Third World theology. Huang Zhanghui described the theological method of “contextualization” as an ongoing interaction between the text of the Christian Scripture and the context to generate new biblical interpretations. He believed that genuine theological reflection can only arise within contexts, asserting that all theology is contextual theology. Christians must interpret socio-political contexts, discern the signs of the times, and engage in critical theological reflection based on their circumstances. Viewing Christian theology as contextual means seriously addressing the contextual dimensions of the Christian gospel and responding with critical theological thought. Huang Zhanghui posited that contextual theology could replace traditional indigenizing theology, which merely concerns cultural adaptation and integration, neglecting the socio-political contexts and historical changes in theological development. Contextualization deeply engages with secular, technological, and economic developments, and advocates for human justice struggles—these processes are significant characteristics of historical moments for Third World countries. Contextual theology adequately responds to social and cultural changes, rather than only addressing traditional cultures.Huang Zhanghui (黃彰輝) also pointed out that erroneous contextualization is blind adaptation, thus forming a type of cultural faith; true contextualization is prophetic and arises from the genuine encounter between God’s Word and the world He created, while simultaneously developing through rooting and commitment to a specific historical context in the direction of challenging and transforming the environment (Qiu Kaili 2021: 131; Zhuo Xinping (卓新平) 2007: 224; Kwan 2014: 41; Pears 2010: 23). Contextual theology in the 1970s facilitated several theological concepts that considered and addressed the current social and political situations in Taiwan, including the enhancement of Taiwanese consciousness and criticism of the Kuomintang (國民黨) regime, even touching upon the issue of Taiwan independence. Furthermore, contextual theology also stimulated the rise of Asian theology, influencing contemporary Christian thought (Zhuo Xinping 2007: 224).

Anti-Christian Leaders

Chen Guoying (陳鼓應) (July 1, 1935~) is a Taiwanese philosopher born in Changting, Fujian. He moved to Taiwan in 1949. In 1964, he obtained a master’s degree from the Department of Philosophy at National Taiwan University (國立臺灣大學, Guó lì Tái wān Dàxué), studying under Yin Haiguang (殷海光) and Fang Dongmei (方東美). After graduation, he worked as a lecturer in the same department and later became an associate professor. He published essays advocating liberalism with Wang Xiaobo (王曉波) in the University Magazine (大學雜誌), promoting the Diaoyu movement and encouraging university students to participate. At that time, the government suppressed student movements out of fear of chaos, particularly concerned that the Chinese nationalism sentiments might connect with the Chinese Communist Party (中共, Zhōnggòng). This led to purges of several liberal scholars, including Chen Guoying, which became known as the National Taiwan University Philosophy Department Incident (台大哲學系事件). In 1973, Chen Guoying and Wang Xiaobo were detained by the police for “propaganda for the bandits” and were subsequently dismissed. After the lifting of martial law, this incident was redressed, and in 1997, Chen Guoying returned to teaching at National Taiwan University. He currently holds the position of Chair Professor of Humanities at Peking University (北京大学, Běijīng Dàxué) (Chen Shichang (陳世昌) 2015: 107-108).

From 1968 to the 1970s, Chen Guoying engaged in intellectual debates with various Christian scholars and clergy, including Tang Youzhi (唐佑之) and Wang Jinghong (王敬弘). He asserted his argument based on humanism and Eastern humanistic spirit, interpreting the Bible literally, criticizing it for containing numerous unreasonable accounts that contradicted common ethical understanding, and claiming that the God depicted in the Bible is authoritarian and bloodthirsty. Chen Guoying believes that God is a creation of humans, existing according to human needs, and thus God’s actions must conform to human nature; the God created by humans should reflect human ideals and therefore possess basic characteristics such as benevolence. A “perverse” God should be discarded by humanity. He further pointed out that God’s love is not unconditional; it requires humans to love God first before God loves them; thus, humanity must become God’s servants to receive rewards, indicating that the God of Christianity is not “charitable.” His statements prompted several Christians to offer counter-arguments, but Chen Guoying chose not to respond, mainly because he believed that the respondents were all believers who refuted his points from a faith perspective, which he found unproductive. At that time, his academic focus was on researching Zhuangzi (莊子), leaving him no time to continue the debate (University Magazine Editorial Committee (大學雜誌編委會) 1970: 1-13, 141-142; no author (無作者) 2011). Christianity was not a topic of interest for Chen Guoying, and related discussions showed his limited academic understanding of it. However, his critiques initiated many discussions, particularly prompting Christian intellectuals to consider how to engage in dialogue with the intellectual community.

Anti-Christian Organizations

Republic of China Government (ROC Regime)

Beginning in the 1960s, an atmosphere of “Anti-Communist Protecting the Faith” emerged, driven by the combination of fundamentalist groups like the International Council of Christian Churches (萬國教聯) and anti-communist forces, which portrayed the World Council of Churches as a group “betraying pure faith” and being pro-Communist and accommodating towards Communism. As a result, the Taiwan Presbyterian Church was forced by the government to withdraw from the World Council of Churches in 1970. Under pressure at that time, the Presbyterian Church established an “Anti-Communist Promotion Committee” (撤銷 in 1973) and held a series of “Anti-Communist Lectures” in 1970 at various presbyteries, striving to eliminate the government’s concerns over its perceived pro-Communist stance, publicly declaring its anti-communist position through action. After withdrawing from the World Council of Churches, the “Anti-Communist Promotion Committee” continued to hold several anti-communist gatherings and, in 1972, sent letters to all its congregations expressing full support and cooperation with the activities of the Second World Christian Anti-Communism Coalition. Even the declaration published in 1971 titled “Taiwan Presbyterian Church’s Statement and Suggestions Regarding National Affairs” firmly proclaimed its anti-communist position and expressed to foreign groups and the United States its refusal to compromise with the Communist Party; however, these efforts did not receive governmental recognition. Presbyterian missionary Mi Dili was expelled from the country on March 5, 1972, for having a part in drafting the declaration. In the period before and after the first declaration was published, the party-state’s intelligence system recorded, followed, and threatened the Presbyterian Church, even intervening in the discussion of church meeting agendas. In 1975, the government confiscated the Bibles and hymnals of the Indigenous Presbyterian Church, as well as the Taiwanese romanized Bible, deepening the Kuomintang’s (國民黨) clampdown on religious freedom and local culture. This led the Presbyterian Church to issue “Our Appeal,” urging the government to respect religious freedom and eliminate distrust toward the church. In 1977, in light of the international situation, the Presbyterian Church proposed a “Human Rights Declaration.” As it discussed a “new and independent country,” it further infuriated the Kuomintang regime, which resulted in the confiscation of that issue of the Taiwan Church News, intensifying scrutiny on the church’s leaders. The three major declarations of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church in the 1970s challenged the power of the ruling authorities at the time. The actions of the church leaders overseas, along with their interactions with foreigners, were closely monitored by intelligence units, mainly aiming to isolate them from connections with overseas dissident groups opposing the Kuomintang regime or Taiwanese independence groups. However, the Presbyterian Church did not yield, continuing to interact with overseas dissident organizations and issuing statements in response to inappropriate government actions domestically while skillfully leveraging the advantages of transnational religious networks to exert pressure on the Kuomintang regime through foreign forces. Thus, the church gradually embarked on a path of confrontation with the government (Zeng Qingbao (曾慶豹) 2016: 194-217; Qiu Kaili (邱凱莉) 2021: 165-167; Huang Kexian (黃克先) 2021: 25-26).

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

Seizure of the Romanized Bible

In 1974, during a Sunday service at the Bo’ai Presbyterian Church (博愛長老教會) in Heping Township, Taichung County, the police forcibly entered the church and took away the Tayal (泰雅族) language Bible and hymns. On January 15, 1975, the government confiscated 1,638 copies of the newly printed Romanized Taiyu New Testament (紅皮聖經) and 655 copies of the reprinted Baker’s Mandarin Bible, citing violations of the national language policy. The news spread abroad, attracting media coverage. On May 31, the Ministry of Education issued an explanation, justifying the seizure by claiming it was due to “violations of language policy,” asserting that “the Chinese government had no intention of interfering or restricting religious freedom,” and stating that “the ban was because of violations of language policy.” On June 2, the director of the Bible Society, Cai Renli (蔡仁理), petitioned the Ministry of Education, requesting the return of the confiscated Baker’s Mandarin Bible. The Ministry returned it in mid-June and allowed its sale, but continued to detain the newly printed “red-cover Bible” under the principle of “a complete ban on distribution.” Meanwhile, Reverend Lester Kim (金牧師), chair of the Asian Presbyterian Caucus of the United Presbyterian Church in the United States, wrote to the President of the Republic of China, Yan Jiachang (嚴家淦), expressing dissatisfaction with the authorities’ seizure of the Taiyu Bible and the deprivation of the Taiwanese people’s freedom to read the Bible in their mother tongue. The Presbyterian Church in Taiwan expressed discontent with the government’s policy of only returning the Bible while continuing to prohibit the Romanized Bible. In a statement issued that same year titled “Our Appeal,” the first point emphasized “maintaining the freedom of religious belief granted by the Constitution,” specifically addressing the issue of the Bible confiscation. The statement pointed out that “the government is petitioned to uphold constitutional freedom of belief, return the newly translated Mandarin Bible, and allow the continued publication of Bibles in any language.” On November 18, the General Assembly of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church passed “Our Appeal” as the Assembly’s position and urged the Bible Society and the Standing Committee to actively negotiate with the government for the return of the remaining seized Bibles, but permission was not granted. However, the government showed more tolerance towards the Catholic Church, allowing it to use the new Romanized “red-cover Bible” for “missionary purposes,” possibly due to the lesser readership of the Bible among Catholics compared to Protestants and the better relationship between the Catholic Church and the government (Huang Yiying (黃懿縈) 2024: 188-205).
