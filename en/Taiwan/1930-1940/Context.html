Taiwan: Context (1930-1940)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

Temple Organization Movement

In 1936, the Governor-General’s Office implemented the “Civic Virtue Movement,” promoting the slogan of “breaking superstitions and improving customs,” and carried out reforms in various aspects such as lifestyle customs and religious beliefs. In 1937, it entered the assimilation movement, with the ultimate goal of replacing Taiwan’s traditional religions with Japanese State Shinto. The Governor-General’s Office reformed religious customs, promoted Shinto, and enforced visits to shrines while venerating the Grand Shinto Hemp (神宮大麻). Emphasis was placed on the construction and elevation of shrines, leading to the construction of 38 shrines between 1937 and 1943, representing more than half of the 68 shrines built during the entire Japanese occupation. The number of shrines in Taiwan surged during the assimilation period, clearly indicating that State Shinto had a significant impact on religious customs.

In daily life, the Governor-General’s Office forced Taiwanese families to venerate the talisman of “Amaterasu Omikami” (天照大神), known as the Grand Shinto Hemp (神宮大麻). According to statistics, the number of those venerated was approximately 235,500 in 1936, increasing to 569,500 in 1937, and reaching 739,378 in 1941. At that time, Taiwan had 1,075,498 households, and according to official data, around 70% of households venerated the Hemp. However, the formal significance outweighed the actual effect, as the authorities believed that if a household received the Hemp, it was counted as veneration, while the actual practice by the Taiwanese fell short of official claims.

Suppressing traditional religions was another aspect of the “reform.” Officials aimed to destroy and consolidate local temples through “temple organization” to eliminate Taiwan’s traditional religions. This policy was implemented by local governments, with Hsinchu (新竹州) being particularly intense, adopting an outright abolition policy, which provoked significant backlash. 1

The official tendency for “temple organization” to be “governed independently” was pointed out by Jiamura Seiji (加村政治), then head of the Shrine and Temple Division of the Governor-General’s Office. He criticized certain local officials for not considering what belief system would replace the “temple organization.” He warned that if they recklessly transplanted Shinto styles, Buddhist elements, or Christianity, problems would arise. Jiamura stated, “While trying to abandon old beliefs, they are forsaking them constantly; seeking to find new beliefs, yet struggling to find a path; with perplexed minds, they wander along the road of assimilation. As a result, some lean towards Buddhism, while others immerse themselves in Christianity.” 2 Jiamura also pointed out that the “temple organization” primarily targeted traditional Taiwanese Buddhism, Taoism, and folk beliefs, having little impact on Christianity. After the Taiwanese lost their objects of faith, it allowed room for Christianity or local Japanese Buddhist sects to “seize territory.” 3 In other words, alongside the temple organization, the issue of Christianity’s “entrance and exit” — the matter of persuading people to convert — was not just about the ebb and flow between Shinto faith and traditional religions, but also the development of Christianity became a topic of concern for the police bureau and the cultural education bureau of the Governor-General’s Office. 4

However, from historical materials, Christians did not display secret joy or glee upon seeing idols burned and temples demolished. Instead, they viewed this with compassion and adopted a more positive attitude, believing this was a good opportunity to spread the gospel. This was true not only on Taiwan Island but also in various places in the Penghu (澎湖) archipelago, where contemporary witnesses pointed out: “Recently, with the civic virtue movement eliminating idols, many people have found themselves without a god to rely on, so they turn to the church to find the true God.” 5 Reports indicated that the impact was more significant on Qimei (七美), while places such as Huazhai (花宅), Jiangjun’ao (將軍澳), Magong (馬公), Wadong (瓦硐), Zhugao Bay (竹篙灣), and Dongji (東吉) experienced a smaller impact, thus the situation of temple organization varied across regions.

Faced with this change in religious belief, Christians urgently called for the necessity of religious reliance, asserting that before humanity can build an ideal and peaceful nation, a high level of culture should be cultivated in advance. They argued that without acknowledging religion, such a state could never be achieved. “High culture” is defined as the connection between human souls and God, where self-discipline is paramount, emphasizing overcoming selfish desires. 6

Similarly, foreign missionaries understood that the common people primarily adhered to Buddhism, Taoism, or folk beliefs, venerating deities such as the Buddha, ancient Chinese heroes, and the God of the Land (土地公). The Taiwanese people’s reliance on traditional religions was profound. 7 Moreover, missionaries expressed warmth and compassionate sympathy towards the Taiwanese, criticizing the “spiritual damage” that resulted in people losing their sources of reliance. The impact of State Shinto on religious belief was severe, affecting both Christians and non-Christians alike. When the Taiwanese lost spiritual comfort, missionaries did not aggressively recruit or forcibly promote the faith. Instead, they patiently waited for those who lost support from traditional religions to turn to Christianity and provided sincere assistance, attempting to guide them towards spiritual peace.

A series of changes in religious customs led to societal unrest, prompting the Governor-General’s Office to begin reviewing its policies. The office concluded that religious issues should not be handled by officials, thus consulting Taipei Imperial University’s Chancellor Mutsu Yamamoto (幣原坦) and folklore anthropology professor Mitaka Kojiro (移川子之藏) to select experts in political science, philosophy, and religion to address this matter. 8 After Miyamoto Nobuhito (宮本延人) took office as the social affairs officer in the Cultural Education Bureau, the Governor-General’s Office notified all provincial governors to temporarily suspend the temple organization movement. Miyamoto and his colleagues conducted field surveys of major temples throughout Taiwan for nearly a year. Local governments also began investigating whether Christianity was accepting Taiwanese who changed religions after the temple organization, thereby expanding its “territory.” For instance, in May 1941, Tainan (臺南) Governor Ichinokai Yoshikazu (一番ケ賴佳雄) wrote to the police bureau chief and various governors, attaching a report on the behavior of Chen Jinran (陳金然), the pastor of the Guiren Church (歸仁教會), and county councilman Xin Xizhun (辛西准) 9 to point out the movements of Taiwanese after the temple organization. 10 The key points included three main aspects:*

During this period, the worship rituals of Christians also underwent slight changes. Christians regarded the birthdays of Jesus and the Ascension Day as “great feast days,” but December 25 coincided with the memorial day of Emperor Taisho (大正天皇), 11and churches in the southern regions, responding to the political climate, urged Christians to avoid celebrating Christmas on that day; besides the worship service, it was best to refrain from holding other activities. 12To proceed with caution, they even published public announcements to inform the congregation. 13Christmas is one of the most important holidays in Christianity, and the church handled it discreetly to avoid sensitive moments and ensure that the activities of its believers and the church remained low-key amidst the tense international situation.

On the other hand, Christians took the opportunity to emphasize the need to reform the spirit of Christmas celebrations, avoiding secularism and ensuring that the spirit, words, actions, and content of the overall process had a sacred presentation to glorify God. 14 Christians stated that the celebration of Christmas must reflect its true meaning and avoid falling into errors. The meaning of “Christmas” is the sending of the Holy Son by God to the world to save humanity from the depths of sin; thus, the holiday should not seek excitement, novelty, or personal gain, nor should it mix in sinful behaviors. Christmas reveals God’s grace, and should be observed with gratitude for redemption, humility in confession, and a serious yet joyful celebration. 15 The behavior of Christians should align with their faith, closely connecting their loyalty. 16 An article titled “The Religion of Christ’s Christmas” points out that before 1937, during the reign of the 11th Emperor of Japan, Emperor Showa (崇神天皇), religion encompassed all of life and was symbolic of dedication. The Christmas of Christ embraces joy, childhood, family, and dedication. Precious things joyfully belong to Jesus; religion not only requires listening and nurturing but also offering what is held dear—body, mind, spirit, and all material possessions—to the Lord. Offering body and material to God enables the fulfillment of the significance of spiritual sacrifice. When celebrating Christmas, one should draw closer to Christ, ensuring words and actions align, serving wholeheartedly and dedicating one’s life to the Lord. 17

In summary, the reform of religious customs had a less significant impact on Christianity. Influenced by State Shinto, Christians handled the traditional Christmas celebration discreetly due to it coinciding with the memorial of the Japanese Emperor, shifting towards the meaning of “spiritual sacrifice.” When ordinary people lost the support of traditional religions and experienced spiritual emptiness, Christians introduced Christian doctrines further, guiding people towards new faith experiences, maintaining warmth toward the public, and understanding the pain of the “uprooting of traditional religions and culture.” Under the influence of societal trends, the Christians’ resolve to “preach and serve the nation” grew stronger, believing it was crucial to seize opportunities for evangelism, rejecting idol worship and the diminishing of God’s glory, thereby expanding the reach of Christianity.

National Mobilization Law

In the mid-Meiji period, the Japanese government attempted to unify and regulate religious activities through legislation. Although the Meiji Constitution stipulated freedom of religion, the state still retained the authority to restrain popular religions, therefore the government claimed that legislative control fell within a reasonable scope. Dominated by the Buddhist community, traditional Japanese religions believed that legalizing religious organizations would elevate the status of Christianity, diminishing the relative advantages of traditional religions. This led to efforts to obstruct the legislative process, resulting in the proposed bills being repeatedly rejected by the National Diet in 1899, 1927, and 1929.

Regarding policies on Christianity, in 1899, the Japanese government granted legitimacy to Christianity through the Ministry of Home Affairs Order No. 41 titled “Provisions on Religions Other than Shinto, Buddhism, and Taoism and Their Places of Worship.” This document brought Christianity under the administration of religious affairs. However, religious activities and the establishment of churches remained subject to the approval and investigation of the Ministry of Education and local authorities. Hence, while Christianity became a legal religion, it was still under state supervision and control.

In the 1930s, with the rise of fascism and the events ranging from the February 26 Incident to the Marco Polo Bridge Incident, Japan became mired in war, and control and suppression of religion became increasingly severe. In April 1938, the Japanese government issued Law No. 55, the “National Mobilization Law,” clearly stating that the country had transitioned from a “quasi-war period” to a “war period.” To consolidate national defense and maximize the national power, it was essential to control and utilize all resources to achieve military objectives. Regarding people’s organizations, due to the law’s mandatory nature, regardless of whether the endeavors or groups were homogeneous or heterogeneous, the government could establish, merge, change, or abolish them, or limit participants under control agreements. Additionally, the Japanese government also had the authority to approve the conditions or regulations of organizational members, and could increase control through imperial decree if necessary.

Religious Organizations Law

In 1939, with the aim of religious control, the Japanese government issued Law No. 77, the “Religious Organizations Law.” During the legislative process, driven by rising nationalism, opposing voices were suppressed. Prime Minister Hiranuma Kiichiro (平沼騏一郎) stated: “To allow religion to fulfill its original functions, it must be granted protection and supervision. Every religion must integrate with our national identity.” Minister of Education Araki Sadao (荒木貞夫) emphasized in the proposal’s explanation: “In extraordinary times, the state should supervise, control, protect, and nurture religion, so that it can more effectively align with the demands of war; thus, the Religious Organizations Law is indispensable.” Matsuo Nagakazu (松尾長造), the director of the Religious Bureau of the Ministry of Education, also said: “If religious organizations or their teachers disseminate doctrine that rejects shrine worship or instructs others to refuse worship, this clearly violates public order and harms the public interest, and will be severely punished in accordance with this law.” From this, it is evident that the Religious Organizations Law was, in effect, a wartime regulation for the control of religion.

The Religious Organizations Law consists of a total of 37 articles (with Articles 29-37 as supplementary provisions). The first chapter stipulates that “religious organizations” are divided into five types: Shinto sects, Buddhist sects, Christian denominations, temples, and churches. The first three are organized religious groups, while the latter two refer to independent temples or churches. The second chapter defines “religious associations” as gatherings formed for the purpose of proclaiming religious doctrine, conducting rituals, etc., excluding “religious organizations,” such as folk religions. The third chapter sets forth “penalties,” including fines for “violating restrictions and prohibitions.” The fourth chapter includes supplementary provisions such as the enforcement date and repeal of previous laws.

This legislation aimed to “supervise and protect religious organizations, enabling them to develop healthily and contribute to society, strongly promote spiritual cultivation and ideological guidance, and prevent various evils from occurring.” The regulatory content specified penalties and possessed coercive force; any transgressions during the proclamation or execution of doctrines that violated public order, obligations of citizens, legal orders, or public safety would be punished. Article 26 stipulates that those violating related prohibitions shall be punished with imprisonment of up to six months, confinement, or a fine of up to 500 yen. Article 27 stipulates that those violating related provisions of association applications shall be fined up to 300 yen. Article 28 stipulates that those violating provisions related to corporate registration shall be fined up to 200 yen. Clearly, this law nearly encroached upon the edge of religious freedom, with the fines being quite substantial.

In May 1940, the Religious Organizations Law was officially implemented. In September, the Ministry of Education convened representatives of Shinto, Buddhism, and Christianity for a conference titled “Strategies for Religious Activities in Response to Current Political Conditions,” and requested that each religion internally proceed with mergers. Buddhism, which originally had 13 sects and 56 factions, was reduced to 13 sects and 28 factions by 1941 under the government’s directive of “unifying sects into a single organization.”

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Return of Missionaries

After the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Japanese government tightened its control over foreigners. In 1939, the Ministry of Internal Affairs announced Provincial Order No. 6 titled “Regarding Foreigners’ Entry, Stay, and Departure” (外国人ノ入国、滞在及退去ニ関スル件). The Governor-General of Taiwan immediately enacted this provincial order as Order No. 50, which required foreigners traveling or staying in Japan to report to local police within a specified time. In August 1939, when German forces invaded Poland and war erupted in Europe, Japan formed a military alliance with Germany and Italy in September 1940, prompting the government to issue a series of decrees to repatriate missionaries and reorganize theological schools.

In December 1941, following Japan’s attack on Pearl Harbor, the Pacific War began, and Japan declared war on Britain, the United States, Canada, and Australia. The cabinet immediately revised Provincial Order No. 32 titled “Regarding Foreigners’ Entry, Stay, and Departure” and the “Handling Guidelines for Enemy Nationals” (敵国人取扱措置要領), which the Governor-General of Taiwan promptly enacted as Order No. 153. 18 The content stipulated that all foreign nationals residing in fortress areas and around military bases must leave, while the rules were more lenient for neutral countries like Spain. However, for enemy country missionaries, harsh measures were imposed, including forced detention or expulsion orders. Many British and American missionaries who remained on the Japanese mainland were under house arrest or forcibly detained. Subsequently, the British Church began receiving notifications of missionaries being expelled from various locations, leading to letters being sent to all missionary regions, but the situation was already beyond control. As Japan provoked the war, the global situation changed drastically within three months, and British missionary institutions in the Far East faced challenges in continuity, with areas like South China and Malaysia being the most severe. 19 As stated earlier, the missionaries in Taiwan left entirely by the end of 1940, not only leaving behind detailed contingency plans but also avoiding many unpredictable dangers.

  1. Missionary Training Conference

From March 26 to April 1, 1943, the Bureau of Education of the Governor-General’s Office, under the name of the “Taiwan Christian Service Corps,” held the “Core Teacher Training Conference” at the Dazhi National Spirit Training Institute. 20 The first round included 47 participants; the second and third rounds were conducted on June 10 and June 24, respectively, with 57 participants each, lasting one week.

The Teacher Training Conference emphasized the “thought reform” of clergy, aiming to instill the principles of Shinto and national spirit. The second and third rounds began on June 10 and 24, respectively, lasting one week, with 57 participants each. Each training session began at 9 AM on the first day, where members gathered at the Meiji Bridge (now the Zhongshan Bridge area) in Taipei, followed by a visit to the Taiwan Shrine for the opening ceremony before the “Great Amaterasu” (天照大神). The guiding officer, Nakanishi Akira (中西旭), an authority on Shinto, was well-versed in the Bible and emphasized that the Japanese emperor was a “manifest deity” (現人神), akin to Jesus Christ, a tangible divine being, teaching content that twisted Christian doctrines to fit Shinto beliefs. Practical training focused on “harsh training and asceticism” to grasp the “Japanese spirit” and the “essence of national polity.” Early mornings involved cold water immersion for “misogi” (禊), followed by meditation, recitation of Shinto prayers (のりと), as well as meetings, group discussions, self-reflection, and bonfire ceremonies (かがりび), 21 and writing poetry for praise. 22 The vast majority of missionaries strongly resisted this coercive “brainwashing session,” deeming it “the most painful brainwashing incident faced during wartime.” Some expressed, “This was a ‘brainwashing session’ that completely contradicted Christian beliefs. At that time, we could not refuse, and to this day, recalling it brings profound guilt before the Lord, becoming a lifetime regret.” 23 Only a few missionaries managed to escape using excuses of illness or other reasons. 24

Judging from the items of the training conference, they undoubtedly greatly conflicted with Christian beliefs. However, the Governor-General’s Office, through the Bureau of Education, carried out a comprehensive spiritual indoctrination under the name of the “Christian Service Corps,” making it genuinely difficult for individual clergy to voice any dissent. The speakers at the conference were cultured, polite, and respectful, not forcing participants to worship Shinto, 25 but attempting to blend the tenets of both religions, emphasizing that “Christianity and Shinto do not contradict; the divine view of Shinto is also that of a Trinity; the greatest deity is called ‘Tenchu no Omo no Kami’ (天中御主神), whom the Japanese do not worship but merely leave a seat for at the center, and this deity is what Christians refer to as ‘Yahweh’; meanwhile, ‘Amaterasu’ (天照大神) is the deity who came to the world as a person, passing down the Japanese imperial lineage, in addition to one deity equivalent to what Christians call the ‘Holy Spirit.’” This deliberate distortion was hard for Taiwanese Christians to accept.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

General International Diplomatic Relations

With the outbreak of the Lugou Bridge Incident (盧溝橋事變) in 1937, the Japanese government sought to consolidate national strength by promoting a series of national policies such as the “General Mobilization Movement for National Spirit” and the “New System Movement,” based on social education. The religious policy of the Governor-General’s Office transitioned from initial “hands-off tolerance,” to mid-term “systematic organization,” and eventually to “religious control” during the war. During the rise of militarism, Japan’s attitude toward Christian nations and Christians gradually turned hostile. Western religions in Japan and Taiwan were introduced by foreign individuals, thus the Japanese government formulated regulations taking into account both foreign relations and religion. As the war expanded in 1941, the Japanese government adopted a “diplomacy first, religion second” approach in handling the residency of foreign nationals. Among them, Spanish clergy from the Dominican Order remained due to maintaining neutrality, but were still monitored and restricted in their residence, and were asked to cooperate with the war efforts and national policies. In Taiwan, most foreign Christians from “enemy nations” like Britain and the United States were expelled, forcing Taiwanese Christians to become self-governed.

Diplomatic Relations with Christian Nations

The term “Manchuria” refers to the territories of Kanto Province (關東州) established in 1905 (later known as Kanto Office), and Manchukuo (滿洲國) established in the year following the September 18 Incident (九一八事變) in 1931 (1932). In other words, this corresponds to the three eastern provinces of the Qing Dynasty or the nine northeastern provinces of the early Republic. Due to colonial rule, Taiwan has a connection to Manchuria, which is about 2,000 kilometers away. According to statistics, there are approximately 5,000 Taiwanese people in Manchuria, with nearly one-fifth of their life stories gradually becoming known, shedding light on this obscure history.

Many Taiwanese with experience in Manchuria have a church background. The origins of Christianity in Manchuria are similar to those in Taiwan, occurring around the same time, both stemming from overseas missions in the 19th century and initially facing a clash between Western culture and local traditions. Manchuria predominantly had Presbyterianism, with British missionary William C. Burns (賓惠廉) arriving in 1867, followed by the Irish Presbyterian Church entering Manchuria in 1869, the Scottish Free Church in 1872, and the Union of the two missionary societies in 1891, culminating in the establishment of synods in 1907 and joining the national assembly in 1925. By 1941, there were nearly 300 Presbyterian churches in Manchuria, with over 30,000 believers, accounting for about half of the overall Christian population.

The Taiwanese Christians who went to develop in Manchuria were mostly middle-class individuals, particularly in the medical field, alongside personnel from industries and academia, a phenomenon related to colonial policies. At that time, Japan actively recruited skilled professionals to develop new territories, and the Taiwanese demonstrated a pioneering spirit. In Manchuria, they ranked just below the Japanese, above the Manchus, Han Chinese, and Koreans, and faced fewer constraints than they did on the island of Taiwan. Notably, despite social mobility among Taiwanese Christians, they rarely became arrogant or self-important, instead practicing their faith by using their professional skills, especially medical knowledge, to assist local people. This led to a high reputation for Taiwanese physicians in Manchuria, earning Taiwan the titles of “Doctor Island” (醫師島) and “Great Immortal Island” (大仙島), recognized positively by Manchurian society, contrasting sharply with the Japanese colonizers.

However, after the war, Taiwanese Christians with experience in Manchuria faced the threat of being judged as “traitors” (漢奸), and thus most hid this period of their past, making related information sparse and difficult to find. Even those who fortunately returned to their homeland encountered a perilous political environment, as the stigma of “Manchukuo” was not on the side of political correctness. Thus, in this unique temporal and spatial context, the Manchurian experiences of Christians and the legacies of colonialism profoundly influenced their life stories. 26

  1. National Nationalism Situation

During the Japanese colonial period, the Governor-General’s Office consistently employed differentiated and discriminatory measures; however, it is undeniable that Taiwan gradually transformed from a civilian society under its governance, achieving relative stability in the political, economic, cultural, and educational landscapes. Since the mid-1930s, Taiwanese Christians, due to the expansion of their influence, experienced a series of revival movements in both the north and south, accumulating numerous achievements and foresight, leading to a certain increase in their faith identity. As a result, Christians generally held an active and enterprising mindset, believing they had a responsibility for the development of both the church and society, striving for the “progress” of both and beginning to learn from Japanese practices in church governance. Consequently, the church’s advocated goals of self-support, self-propagation, and self-governance were achieved earlier due to the changing circumstances. The practice of “preaching and serving the nation” advanced the ideals of the kingdom of heaven significantly.

During this period, with changing international relations, the situation in Taiwan grew increasingly tense, prompting the Governor-General’s Office to implement a series of social education movements aimed at enhancing the productivity of rural communities and improving public health, as well as promoting the use of the Japanese language, reverence for the Emperor, and better living conditions. The social education measures promoted by the government aligned with Japan’s overall national policies, structuring, utilizing, and controlling the populace. Beyond advocating for industrial economic revitalization, these measures exhibited strong characteristics of a spiritual movement and improvement in living conditions.

With the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, to coordinate with Japan’s domestic “General Mobilization Movement for National Spirit,” Taiwan initiated the “Japanization Movement” (皇民化運動), inheriting the content of social education movements, marking Japan’s implementation of a series of “Japanese loyal subject transformation” measures in its colony. The Japanization Movement continued the key points of social education, intensifying the use of the Japanese language, mandating shrine worship, family dedication to “Shinto talismans” (神宮大麻), and promoting Japanese lifestyles. Starting in 1940, it further encouraged “name changes” to strengthen Japanese cultural cultivation. In the field of education, it prohibited classical Chinese language courses in schools, abolished classical Chinese columns in newspapers, banned traditional operas, implemented temple renovations, and improved Taiwanese family main halls, attempting to erase Han ethnic characteristics. Governor Kobayashi Taizo (小林躋造) clearly articulated “Japanization, industrialization, and southern advance base establishment” as the three principles for his rule over Taiwan. The Japanization Movement significantly impacted various social strata, covering a wide range of aspects and touching every detail of colonial life, while the Sino-Japanese War thrust colonial Taiwan into a wartime regime alongside the motherland. To secure Taiwan’s crucial strategic position, the Governor-General’s Office attempted to assimilate the Taiwanese into true “imperial subjects,” instilling in them the patriotism and spirit of sacrifice characteristic of Japanese citizens. Clearly, the Japanization process had a profound impact on the daily lives of the Taiwanese, and Christians were no exception.
