Taiwan: Agency (1930-1940)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

(1) 满雄才 (Mǎn Xióngcái)

满雄才 (William E. Montgomery, 1875-1968), a Northern Irish, graduated from Queen's University Belfast (贝尔法斯特的皇后学院). He received theological training at Princeton University (普林斯顿大学) in the United States. Full of knowledge and character, he earned considerable respect and was sent to Taiwan by the Presbyterian Church of England in 1909, joining the ranks of missionaries. His main work involved managing the Tainan Theological School (台南神学院) and also served as the acting principal of Chang Jung Christian University (长荣中学). He was also the chief editor of the Fucheng Church News (府城教会报) and a council member of the first South-North United Presbyterian Church Assembly of Taiwan (南北联合的长老教会台湾大会). He left Taiwan due to the war but returned to Tainan Theological Seminary after the war, serving as president for a year and a half until he resigned due to illness. He ultimately passed away in Canada.1

(2) 孙雅各 (Sūn Yǎgé)

孙雅各 (James Ira Dickson, 1900-1967) graduated from Princeton Theological Seminary (普林斯顿大学神学院) in 1927. In the same year, he married 孙理莲 (Sūn Lǐlián) and, influenced by the student volunteer missionary movement, responded to the call of the Presbyterian Church in Canada, arriving in Taiwan in October. He served as the acting principal of Tamkang Middle School (淡江中学) and later as the president of Taipei Theological Seminary (now Taiwan Theological Seminary, 台湾神学院). In northern Taiwan, he assisted the younger generation of the church in promoting the church reform movement, known as the New Person Movement (新人运动). In 1940, due to pressure from the Governor-General’s Office amidst World War II, he left Taiwan with 孙理莲 (Sūn Lǐlián) and ministered in British Guiana in South America. He returned to Taiwan after the war and served again as president of Taiwan Theological Seminary from 1948 to 1965. During his tenure, he relocated the campus from the Shuanglian area of Taipei City to its current site in Yangmingshan (阳明山). He dedicated himself to missionary work among indigenous people, founding 385 churches and facilitating the establishment of Yushan Theological College (玉山神学院). Additionally, in 1947, he went to Shanghai to invite a medical team from the Mennonite Central Committee to serve the indigenous people in the mountainous areas of Taiwan; the following year, he successfully brought the Mennonite Mountain Medical Team to Taiwan and, together with 孙理莲 (Sūn Lǐlián) and others, initiated medical and relief work in those mountainous areas. He long supported social welfare efforts by assisting his wife, 孙理莲 (Sūn Lǐlián), through resources from the Taiwan Presbyterian Church. In 1967, he passed away due to a brain tumor and was buried at Taiwan Theological Seminary. 孙理莲 (Sūn Lǐlián) carried on his legacy by establishing the Burning Bush Overseas Mission Association the following year, becoming the first to send indigenous Taiwanese missionaries to places such as Sarawak in Malaysia for mission work.2

(3) 上与二郎 (Shàng yǔ Èrláng)

上与二郎 (Yojiro Kami, 1884-1984) became the fourth pastor of the Taipei Church of the Japan Christian Church (now Jinan Presbyterian Church, 濟南長老教會) in 1918. In 1933, to expand the mission, he established a preaching station in Dadaocheng, inviting Taiwanese to participate. It officially became known as the "Taipei Church of the Japan Christian Church" in 1935. After the outbreak of World War II, the Japanese government rejected British and American influence. In 1937, the churches in Taiwan combined with nine Japanese churches to form the "All Taiwan Christian Service Association for the North China Incident," with Pastor Kami serving as president. In 1940, Japan declared war on Britain and the United States, leading to the departure of foreign missionaries from Taiwan. The churches they led were taken over, the Tainan Theological School was forced to close, and students transferred to Taipei Theological School, which was managed by Japanese principal Oikawa Masani until he left in February 1944, at which point Pastor Kami assumed the principal role. On April 29, 1944, all Japanese and Taiwanese churches on the island were instructed by the Cultural Affairs Bureau of the Governor-General's Office to merge into the "Japan Christian Church Taiwan Synod," completely falling under the same command system, with Kami as the administrator, who dedicated himself to loyalty to the Emperor under the active guidance of the Governor-General's Office. After Japan's defeat in 1945, Pastor Kami was repatriated and founded Chitose Church (千岁教会) in Tokyo in 1947.3

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

(1) The Three-Year Movement of the Northern Church

During the Japanese occupation, the Northern Church gradually transitioned from its initial establishment phase to an organizational nurturing phase. The missionaries who were born in this era witnessed significant transformations within the Northern Church. In the 1930s, young missionaries were deeply influenced by the "Kingdom Movement" of Japanese Christian socialist He Chuanfeng (賀川豊彥) and learned from the experiences of Chinese Christians promoting the "Five-Year Movement," absorbing the concept of "Revival Evangelism." They observed the experiences of Japanese Christian churches and learned from their rigorous procedural protocols to discuss the revitalization of the church.

Therefore, in 1934, during the 11th Northern Missionary Conference held in Tamsui (淡水), young missionaries proposed the idea of the "Three-Year Movement," focusing on the responsibilities of evangelism. They aimed to reorganize the church’s faith and establish the essence of Christianity. At the same time, considering that the Canadian mother church had assisted the local people for over 60 years, Christians were increasingly eager for self-governance and independence. In 1935, the Canadian mother church successfully passed this plan, and the Northern Conference officially approved the proposal for the three-year movement submitted by local missionaries.

In November 1935, the "Northern Christian Three-Year Movement Department" was officially established, with missionary Ming Youde (明有德) as the head and Xiao Leshan (蕭樂善) as the secretary general. This movement was positioned as a "total mobilization of the church," which means "reviving faith, invigorating the spirit of Christ, guiding people to return to the Lord, and establishing a church filled with the life of God," clearly indicating the direction of their efforts.

In January 1936, the Three-Year Movement commenced, with the work divided into three departments: the Revival Department, focused on encouraging members to earnestly progress and emphasizing internal renewal; the Soul-Saving Department, which hoped members would invite others to believe in faith, stressing external expansion; and the Youth Department, aimed at invigorating Sunday schools and youth groups, targeting specific age groups to cultivate the most impressionable young people, aspiring to become the backbone of the church.

As the Three-Year Movement entered its second phase in 1937, after the efforts of the previous year, Northern Christians sensed signs of revitalization in the church and anticipated continual self-reflection and improvement to avoid jealousy, disputes, or underestimating the Bible. Xiao Leshan further encouraged believers to "speak the truth in love," practicing justice and kindness to avoid falling short of God's grace. He believed that Christians should train themselves and serve God faithfully, ensuring the correct faith of this "Bible movement" would be spread throughout Taiwan. He called upon Christians to embrace the mission of evangelism, guiding and assisting one another, allowing both missionaries and lay believers to find their places to contribute.

In 1938, entering the final phase of the Three-Year Movement, Christians continued to emphasize the development of "church" internal matters. Xiao Leshan pointed out that while the Northern region had more than 70 churches appearing strong, the actual spirit of spreading the gospel needed to be strengthened. Faced with an increasingly tense international situation, especially during financial difficulties for the Canadian mother church, Christians needed to remain vigilant. Thus, he encouraged everyone to undertake the role of "pioneers," emphasizing that only through action could progress and development be achieved, hoping that Christians would grow in their love for the Lord, harmony, and walk righteously without fear of obstacles, making the Three-Year Movement a model for future generations.

Overall, the implementation effects of the Three-Year Movement reflected that the first year had various activities such as distribution ceremonies, lectures, and special gatherings, divided into three main objectives: Revival, Youth, and Soul-Saving, with more systematic execution and enthusiastic participation. In the second and third years, the focus shifted to "literary evangelism." Many printed materials were published during the three years, but the form of gatherings remained predominantly static.

In fact, the reasons for the fluctuations in the Three-Year Movement were due to changes in the political environment. Impacted by World War II, Christians found it difficult to concentrate on the revival movement, and organizing public gatherings became challenging. In 1938, Xiao Leshan lamented that many believers were requisitioned for "arduous public service" to build fortifications, leaving them with little time to maintain church life,

Clearly, the "political factors" limited large gatherings, and the activities held were mostly static, such as lectures and Bible study groups, similar in nature to regular Sunday services. Christians passively listened and studied, lacking the "outreach" necessary for evangelism, and the opportunities for direct interaction with non-Christians naturally diminished. However, Xiao Leshan (蕭樂善) always hoped that members would have a stronger biblical foundation and strengthen their Bible study through publications. This idea was undoubtedly the foundation of their faith.

When Northern Christians reflected on the Three-Year Movement, they did not discuss it in much detail; some former leaders merely summarized, "Although several small publications were issued, due to the tense period of war, expected results could not be achieved." In fact, the variety of publications issued was numerous, yet there remained room for improvement in their actual effectiveness when reaching the believers.

Nonetheless, it cannot be denied that under the guidance of Pastor Xiao Leshan and others, the Three-Year Movement did help improve both the quality and quantity of faith identification among Christians. Through newspapers and magazines, discussions on faith concepts and practices were deepened, benefiting self-reflection and encouragement among Christians. In 1938, the Taipei Theological School and the Tamsui Women’s Institute officially established their boards, further solidifying educational institutions. The number of believers reached 9,667 in 1938, which also led to the Northern Conference being divided into three separate conferences: Taipei, Hsinchu, and Eastern, laying the groundwork for the elevation of the "General Assembly." The direction of the Three-Year Movement holds significant meaning in the history of the church in Taiwan.

(2) The Tainan Theological School Closure Incident

At the end of May 1940, just a week after Oda Masashige (大川正) took office as principal in Taipei, the Tainan Education Department requested the Tainan Theological School to appoint a Japanese principal as well. On June 11, the Clergy Association held its regular meeting, where acting principal Xi Liji (希禮智) reported on the meeting with the education department head. The Clergy Association disagreed with the official request and decided to abandon the operation of the theological school. They outlined three key points: first, to arrange for male students to transfer to Taipei Theological School; second, to have students from the Women's Bible Society transfer to Tamsui Bible School; and third, to notify local church leaders of this decision and convey the Clergy Association's views concerning the union theological school.

After reaching a preliminary consensus, the Clergy Association, in order to understand the opinions of southern church leaders, asked Yang Shiyang (楊士養) to privately inquire about local leaders' views. Many had privately discussed the issue of whether to close the theological school all night, ultimately expressing their agreement. Local leaders stated, "However, if we must close because a Japanese person is to be the principal, it poses a significant danger given the current situation. We must proceed very cautiously." As a result, it was conveyed to Pastor Xi that if the Clergy Association meant to shut down, sending students to Taipei was agreeable to them.

On June 21, 1940, a standing committee was convened in the south, during which the Clergy Association's decision to cease operations of the school was heard, and locals were temporarily noncommittal, feeling reluctant to let go. A group of ten convened a "Missionary Training Committee" to discuss countermeasures. On July 7, Wan Ronghua (萬榮華) immediately informed the British mother church, "COLLEGE CLOSED STUDENTS GOING NORTH SEPTEMBER EDWARD BAND" (學校關閉學生九月北上萬榮華).

On the morning of July 17, the Missionary Training Committee held a meeting in Tai Ping Jing, Tainan City, where both Erlang (二郎) and Fan Jiang Tiexiong (番匠鐵雄) encouraged the continuation of the theological school. In the afternoon, the Southern Assembly convened a standing committee that expressed a desire to continue the operation of the theological school but required that the annual funding of 10,000 yuan be divided among the four conferences, with a deadline for response. If unable to meet the financial burden, the students would be sent to Taipei for study, and it was hoped that both schools' assets could be allocated for the establishment of a union theological school.

Looking at the church finances for the year 1940, the Kaohsiung Conference had a total income of 5,613.55 yuan, total expenditures of 4,219.26 yuan, leaving a balance of only 1,394.29 yuan. The Chiayi Conference had a total income of 4,695.07 yuan, total expenditures of 3,771.80 yuan, leaving a balance of just 923.27 yuan. The total budget for the past two years for the Southern Assembly was only 1,590 yuan. This financial situation was clearly inadequate to support the operational costs of the theological school. Thus, the Tainan Conference decided on July 22: "This standing committee cannot bear the operating costs of 2,500 yuan for the theological school, but hopes the union theological school can be realized under the Taiwan Assembly." On July 23, the Kaohsiung Conference decided: "This conference, due to the heavy financial burden, cannot support this; our conference unanimously agrees to the committee's decision." The responses from the Taichung and Chiayi Conferences were similar, clearly indicating that none of the Southern Four Conferences could bear the substantial financial costs.

The desire of the locals to independently manage the theological school proved unfeasible, leading them to focus on processing the closure with the faculty and students, arranging for teachers to transition, students to transfer, and applying to the governor's office for the school's dissolution. During the closure ceremony, participants felt a heavy and solemn atmosphere, with some church elders expressing that their "spiritual state was disturbed." This outcome left Gong Neizhang (宮內彰), the successor candidate for the principal position, unemployed, causing great displeasure to the introducer Erlang (二郎). Public opinion asserted that foreign influences were withdrawing, allowing local individuals to rise up in their place.

Missionaries summarized, "For a long time, we genuinely wished for the unification of the Northern and Southern theological schools, but unfortunately, this was not successful. This time the authorities want us to appoint a local person as principal, but fortunately, Taipei Theological School has already appointed the excellent Mr. Oda (大川), so it is entirely appropriate for our students to go there. This is the first step towards unification. Additionally, we can save a lot of funds, as historically, most of the theological school’s finances have been subsidized by Britain, but due to the extraordinary times, there are difficulties in funding, so they fear they may not be able to sustain it each year."

Anti-Christian Leaders

(1) Imagawa Fuchi (今川淵)

Imagawa Fuchi (今川淵) (1886-1955), from Fukui Prefecture in Japan. He graduated from the Political Department of Tokyo Imperial University in 1912 and passed the Higher Civil Service Examination the following year, after which he took a position in the Taiwan Governor-General's Office. In July 1919, Imagawa Fuchi succeeded Fudshima Genji as the head of the Taipei Office, overseeing administrative affairs in areas including New Taipei City, Yilan, and Keelung. From March 1932 to February 1936, he served as the Governor of Tainan Prefecture, followed by positions as Governor of Taipei Prefecture and Director of the Monopoly Bureau. Additionally, the architect responsible for the Governor-General's Office in Taiwan, Ide Kaoru (井手薰), was his brother-in-law.4

In 1931, Tainan Presbyterian Middle School was criticized by public opinion for refusing to worship at a Japanese shrine. At that time, Tainan Prefectural Governor Imagawa Fuchi sought to seize both the boys' and girls' schools under the pretense of an emergency, and with the help of Japanese followers, he temporarily alleviated the crisis by hiring Japanese individuals as the principals and chairmen of both schools. However, the two campuses in Tamsui, Taipei, faced relentless attacks, suffering slander from newspapers, particularly from the "Kōseikai" (皇政会), a black market organization mostly composed of members of the "Black Dragon Society." They held anti-Danzhong rallies in Taipei, alleging that the land of Danzhong Middle School was a "perpetual leased land" resulting from unequal treaties during the Qing Dynasty. They questioned why, in the so-called "Shōwa Holy Era," such outmoded leased land was still permitted to remain on Japanese "imperial soil," attempting to force the church to abandon its management of the school.

In August 1936, the church managing Danzhong Middle School made necessary compromises to cope with the increasingly challenging circumstances, replacing Principal Xie Ruilian with Pastor Mei Youde (明有德牧師) (Hugh A. MacMillan), who was fluent in Japanese, and hiring a retired Japanese army second lieutenant as an instructor to implement military training courses. They hoped that such reforms would ensure the safety of the two schools.

Unexpectedly, two years after he played a leading role in the Tainan Presbyterian Middle School incident, Governor Imagawa Fuchi was transferred north to become the Governor of Taipei Prefecture. At this time, Suzuki Isamu (鈴木勇), a Japanese instructor at Tamsui Middle School, took advantage of his resignation to return home to accuse the church-managed Tamsui Middle School of not complying with the situation and promoting "unpatriotic sentiments." This turmoil reignited the issue of Danzhong Middle School's survival and provided an opportunity for the scheming Imagawa Fuchi. He immediately pressured the church to transfer Tamsui Middle School and Tamsui Women’s Academy to an organization he had formed, while insinuating that there would be serious consequences if the church did not comply. He also used the press to slander Danzhong, distorting claims that students and Taiwanese followers were unhappy with the church and that conflicts had erupted, even resorting to dispatching military police to question two teachers under the pretext of "unstable remarks and anti-national thoughts."

In August 1936, as churches in Taipei and Japanese believers were trying to assist the church in resolving these unreasonable demands, the Education Chief Takachiwa Yoshio (立川義男) sent by Imagawa Fuchi suddenly came to take over the two schools and forcibly assumed the role of acting principal. It became clear that the church, under Imagawa Fuchi's coercion, had no choice but to make concessions and transfer Danzhong Middle School to the Japanese government. From this point on, Danzhong Middle School marked the end of the missionary era and entered the era of Japanese governance.5

(2) Uemura Kazuto (上村一仁)

During the tenure of acting principal Leslie Singleton (沈毅敦) at Tainan Presbyterian Middle School in 1934, the "Shinto Shrine Worship Incident" occurred: Japanese academic director Uemura Kazuto (上村一仁) planned to lead students to worship at a shrine, which Singleton deemed a violation of the fundamental spirit of a Christian school, and thus intervened to prevent it. Uemura was dismissed and, in anger, incited right-wing Japanese individuals to write successive letters to the newspaper, accusing the school of lacking patriotism (Japanese).6

At that time, Japanese militarism was rampant, and the government sought to strengthen control over Christian schools, questioning Tainan Presbyterian Middle School's patriotism from various angles. A conflict ensued between the school's director Lin Maosheng (林茂生) and academic director Uemura Kazuto, which was reported by the media and manipulated by right-wing groups among Japanese residents in Taiwan, leading to an escalation of the dispute, with Lin becoming a target of attacks. Finally, Principal Wan Ronghua (萬榮華) was compelled to dissolve the support association, collaborate with the Japanese Christian Church, hire a Japanese principal, and step down to become a regular teacher in order to mitigate the crisis. Lin Maosheng resigned and announced the termination of all ties with the school.7

Anti-Christian Organizations

Kōmin Hōkōkai (皇民奉公會)

In April 1941, the Taiwan Governor-General's Office established the Kōmin Hōkōkai (皇民奉公會) in conjunction with Japan's "Taisei Yokusankai" (大政翼贊會), aiming to support the war effort by mobilizing all human and material resources. The Governor-General's Office utilized the existing administrative system to set up branch organizations step by step, with officials from the Governor-General's Office also serving as leaders of the society, and recruiting individuals from various sectors of society to participate. This was intended to rally the entire island of Taiwan to promote the "Kōmin Hōkō" (皇民奉公) movement, thereby training island residents to increase production in service of the nation, establishing a wartime living system, and transforming Taiwan into a military base. 8

The Kōmin Hōkō movement emphasized "Shindō Jitsugen" (臣道実践), initiated and executed by the Governor-General's Office, which was more clearly defined and directed by officials compared to the more "ambiguous" nature of the Kōminka movement. 9

To align with national policy, the Kōmin Hōkōkai (皇民奉公會) focused on promoting concepts such as "Kokutai Hon'i" (国体本義) and "Keishin Sōso" (敬神崇祖), with "keishin" referring to worshiping Shinto and the living god, the Emperor. The organization was structured with offices for administration, propaganda, training, culture, life, and economy under its central headquarters, forming various affiliated groups such as the Industry Hōkō Corps, Youth Hōkō Corps, Literature Hōkō Society, Kikyo Club, Hōkō Men’s Corps, and the Healthy Elderly Service Corps. The Kōmin Hōkōkai spread the idea of "fighting for His Majesty the Emperor" through lectures, exhibitions, and printed materials, with extensive outreach, such as publishing the "Kōmin Hōkō Series" in 1941 and 1942, and issuing the Kōmin Hōkōkai's official magazine "Shinkensetsu" (新建設) as propaganda tools. In simpler terms, the Kōmin Hōkōkai aimed to mobilize the entire organization across Taiwan to achieve effective propaganda, with various clans, committees, and tribal associations also required to adhere to the spirit of keishin sōso; for instance, rituals such as remote worship of the imperial palace and worshiping at shrines were necessary ceremonies.

The Kōmin Hōkōkai was integrated with the administrative structure of the Governor-General's Office, and its basic organization generally followed the model of Japan's Taisei Yokusankai (大政翼贊會). In addition to "regional organizations" formed according to administrative divisions, the Kōmin Hōkōkai also gradually incorporated various groups into "occupational organizations," including the Taiwan Industry Hōkō Corps, Business Hōkō Corps, Hōkō Men’s Corps, Taiwan Youth Corps, Kikyo Club, Greater Japan Women's Association, Patriotic Women's Association, Taiwan Drama Association, Taiwan Buddhist Hōkō Corps, Literature Hōkō Society, Fine Arts Hōkō Society, Music Hōkō Society, and other cultural organizations. Christian leader Lin Maosheng (林茂生) was appointed to serve as an officer of the Hōkōkai. 10

In early August 1942, the "Taiwan Christian Service Association," which had been established for five years in response to the North China Incident, began a series of reorganizations to expand its organization and correspond with the Kōmin Hōkōkai. Convened by Japanese officials, over 1,300 Japanese and Taiwanese Christians held a ceremony in Taipei's church to announce the establishment of the "Taiwan Christian Hōkōkai" (臺灣基督教奉公會), appointing 17 directors including Shang Yu and Er Lang along with local committee members. By the end of August, under the manipulation of the board, it was renamed "Taiwan Christian Hōkō Corps" (臺灣基督教奉公團), abbreviated as Hōkō Corps. They drafted regulations making clear their main purpose: "to uphold the imperial edict declaring war against America and Britain, to fulfill the duties of the subjects of the Empire, and to contribute to the nation through Christianity, in order to train the Kōmin." Their actual tasks included "organizing study sessions, lectures, and training sessions aimed at enhancing members' awareness, uplifting the national wartime spirit, guiding citizens in wartime life, assisting in the promotion of the national language for internal unity, and holding prayers for military victories."

In December 1942, the Tainan local branch of the Hōkō Corps gathered 400 believers for its inaugural ceremony; 11 in January of the following year (1943), the Kaohsiung local branch and Taitung local branch were established in succession. The former held its inaugural ceremony at the Kaohsiung City Public Hall, presided over by Chairman Shang Yu, with 300 believers in attendance; the latter held its inaugural ceremony at the Taitung Street Public Assembly Hall, also presided over by Pastor Shang, with 200 believers participating. 12 In summary, the Taiwan Christian Hōkō Corps serves as an auxiliary organization of the Kōmin Hōkōkai, driving the church to collaborate in the war effort. While it is not directly governed by the church, it provides the Japanese officials with the opportunity to direct the entire church, effectively uniting Taiwanese Christians into a broad wartime cooperative organization.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

Kominka Movement (皇民化運動)

With the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, Taiwan launched the "Kominka Movement" (皇民化運動) to align with Japan's domestic "National Spiritual Mobilization Movement," inheriting the connotations of social education movements. This can be seen as a series of "Japanization" measures implemented by Japan in colonial Taiwan. The Kominka Movement continued key aspects of social education, reinforcing the use of the Japanese language, mandating visits to shrines, promoting the home worship of "Jingū Ōma" (神宮大麻), and implementing Japanese lifestyle practices. Starting in 1940, it further encouraged "name changes" to strengthen Japanese spiritual cultivation. In terms of cultural education, it prohibited classical Chinese courses in schools, abolished classical Chinese columns in newspapers, banned traditional operas, carried out temple renovations, and improved the main halls of Taiwanese homes, with the aim of removing Han ethnic elements. Governor Kobayashi Jizō (小林躋造) clearly stated that "Japanization, industrialization, and the establishment of southern bases" were his three principles for governing Taiwan.13 The Kominka Movement significantly impacted various social strata, covering a wide range of details in colonial life,14 and the Second Sino-Japanese War put colonial Taiwan under a wartime system alongside the motherland. To secure Taiwan's strategic location, the governor's office attempted to assimilate Taiwanese people into true "imperial subjects," instilling in them a sense of patriotism and willingness to sacrifice for the Japanese nation.15 Clearly, the Kominka Movement had a significant influence on the daily lives of Taiwanese, including Christians.
