Taiwan: Agency (1930-1940)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Man Xiong Cai

Man Xiong Cai (滿雄才) (William E. Montgomery, 1875-1968), a native of Northern Ireland, graduated from Queen’s University Belfast (貝爾法斯特的皇后學院). He trained in theology at Princeton University (美國普林斯頓大學). Man Xiong Cai was well-respected for his knowledge and character. In 1909, he was sent to Taiwan by the Presbyterian Church of England to join the missionary efforts. His primary work involved managing Tainan Theological College, and he also acted as the principal of Chang Jung Senior High School. Man Xiong Cai served as the editor of the Church News of Tainan (府城教會報, 臺灣教會公報) and was a member of the first General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan. He left Taiwan due to the war but returned post-war to serve as dean of Tainan Theological College for a year and a half before resigning due to illness, ultimately passing away in Canada.1

  1. Sun Ya Ge

Sun Ya Ge (孫雅各) (James Ira Dickson, 1900-1967) graduated from Princeton Theological Seminary (普林斯頓大學神學院) in 1927. That same year, he married Sun Li Lian (孫理蓮). Influenced by the Student Volunteer Movement for Overseas Missions, he accepted a call from the Presbyterian Church in Canada, arriving in Taiwan in October. He served as acting principal of Tamkang Senior High School and later as dean of Taipei Theological Seminary (now Taiwan Theological College). In northern Taiwan, he assisted the younger generation of the church in promoting the church reform movement known as the New People Movement. In 1940, due to pressures from the Governor-General’s Office during World War II, Sun Ya Ge and Sun Li Lian left Taiwan to serve in British Guiana in South America. After the war, he returned to Taiwan and served again as the dean of Taiwan Theological College from 1948 to 1965. During his administration, he relocated the school from Shuanglian in Taipei City to the current site in Yangmingshan. Sun Ya Ge dedicated himself to missionary work among Indigenous peoples, founding 385 churches and facilitating the establishment of Yu Shan Theological College. Additionally, in 1947, he went to Shanghai to invite the Mennonite Central Committee to send a medical team to serve the Indigenous peoples in the mountains, which successfully brought the Mennonite Mountain Medical Team to Taiwan the following year. He, along with Sun Li Lian and others, developed medical and relief work in the mountainous areas. He continuously supported his wife Sun Li Lian’s social welfare work in Taiwan through resources from the Taiwan Presbyterian Church. In 1967, Sun Ya Ge passed away from a brain tumor and was buried at Taiwan Theological College. Sun Li Lian continued his legacy by establishing the Burnt Thorns Overseas Mission Society the following year, which first dispatched Indigenous Taiwanese missionaries to areas such as Sarawak, Malaysia.2

  1. Shang Yu Er Lang

Shang Yu Er Lang (上與二郎) (Yojiro Kami, 1884-1984) became the fourth pastor of the Taipei Church of the Japan Christian Church (now Jinan Presbyterian Church) in 1918. In 1933, in order to expand his missionary work, he established a mission center in Dadaocheng and invited Taiwanese people to participate, officially naming it the “Taipei Church of the Japan Christian Church” in 1935. Following the outbreak of World War II, the Japanese government rejected the influence of Britain and the United States. In 1937, with nine groups including Taiwanese and Japanese churches, he formed the “All Taiwan Christian Service Association for the North China Incident,” serving as president. When Japan declared war on Britain and the United States in 1940, foreign missionaries were forced to leave Taiwan, and the churches they led were taken over. Tainan Theological College was forced to close, and students moved to Taipei Theological College, where the principal, a Japanese named Oikawa Masaru, managed until his resignation in February 1944, after which Shang Yu Er Lang assumed the role. On April 29, 1944, the Japanese and Taiwanese churches across the island were merged by the guidance of the Japanese Governor-General’s cultural bureau to form the “Japan Christian Church Taiwan,” completely falling under a unified command system, with Shang Yu Er Lang as the leader, dedicating themselves to serve the Emperor under the active guidance of the Governor-General. Following Japan’s defeat in 1945, Pastor Shang was repatriated and founded the “Chitose Church” in Tokyo in 1947.3

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Northern Church’s Three-Year Movement

    During the Japanese occupation, the Northern Church gradually transitioned from its initial establishment phase into a period of organized pastoral care. The missionaries born during this era witnessed the great transformation of the Northern Church. In the 1930s, young missionaries were greatly influenced by Japanese Christian socialist Hekawa Toyohiko’s “Kingdom of God Movement” and learned from the Chinese Christians’ experience of promoting the “Five-Year Movement,” absorbing the concept of “Revival Evangelism.” The missionaries observed the practices of Japanese churches, learning rigorous procedures for deliberation to discuss issues related to revitalizing the church.

    Consequently, in 1934, during the 11th Northern Missionaries’ General Assembly held in Tamsui, young missionaries, motivated by the essence of faith and church development, proposed the concept of a “Three-Year Movement.” They focused on the responsibilities of mission work, deciding to rectify the church’s beliefs and establish the essence of Christianity. At the same time, considering that the Canadian parent church had assisted the Taiwanese for over 60 years, Christians were increasingly looking forward to self-governance. In 1935, the Canadian parent church successfully approved this plan, and the Northern Conference also officially sanctioned the proposal for the Three-Year Movement submitted by the missionaries’ general assembly.

    In November 1935, the “Northern Christian Three-Year Movement Department” was formally established, with missionary Ming Youde (明有德) serving as the head and Xiao Leshan (蕭樂善) as the executive (secretary-general), positioning this movement as a “total mobilization of the church,” specifically “reviving faith, promoting the spirit of Christ, guiding people to return to the Lord, and establishing a church that possesses the life of God,” clearly indicating the direction of their effort.

    In January 1936, the Three-Year Movement kicked off, dividing its work into three parts: the Revival Department, aimed at encouraging members to be zealously progressive, focusing on renewal within the church; the Salvation Department, hoping members would actively invite others to the faith, emphasizing expansion outside the church; and the Youth Department, aimed at invigorating Sunday schools and youth groups, targeting specific age groups to cultivate the most moldable young people, intending for them to become the foundation and pillars of the church.

    By 1937, entering the second phase of the Three-Year Movement, the northern Christians felt signs of revitalization in the church after a year of effort, anticipating continuous self-reflection to avoid jealousy, disputes, or disregard for the Bible. Xiao Leshan further encouraged believers to “speak the truth in love,” practicing justice and kindness to avoid falling short of God’s grace. Xiao Leshan believed that Christians needed to train themselves to be faithful in service to God, ensuring that this “Bible Movement” would spread the correct faith throughout Taiwan. He called on Christians to embrace the mission of evangelism, guiding and assisting one another so that both missionaries and lay believers could find their place to contribute.

    In 1938, entering the final phase of the Three-Year Movement, Christians still emphasized internal church development. Xiao Leshan pointed out that although the North had over 70 churches appearing robust, the actual spirit of evangelism needed strengthening, especially in the context of an increasingly clouded international situation. Particularly during the financial difficulties faced by the Canadian parent church, believers needed to remain attentive. Therefore, he encouraged everyone to take on the role of “pioneers,” asserting that progress and development were only possible through action, hoping Christians would grow in love for the Lord, live harmoniously, walk on the righteous path without fear of obstacles, making the Three-Year Movement a model for future generations.

    Looking at the overall implementation of the Three-Year Movement’s effects, the first year had more activities, such as the launch ceremony, lectures, and special meetings, divided into three main goals: Revival, Youth, and Salvation, executing affairs more systematically with considerable participation. In the second and third years, the focus shifted primarily to “literature evangelism.” Many written publications were issued throughout the three years, though the format of gatherings remained largely static.

    In fact, the setbacks of the Three-Year Movement were due to changes in the political environment. Affected by World War II, Christians could not wholeheartedly engage in the revival movement, nor could they hold public gatherings. In 1938, Xiao Leshan lamented that many believers were conscripted for “public service” to build defensive structures, leaving them unable to maintain church life.

    Clearly, “situational factors” limited large gatherings, and the events held mostly consisted of static lectures and Bible study meetings, resembling ordinary Sunday services. Christians passively listened and studied, lacking the initiative to “go out” for external evangelism, and regrettably missing opportunities to interact directly with non-Christians. However, Xiao Leshan always hoped that believers would build a stronger foundation in the Bible and enhance their Bible study through media publications. This idea is undoubtedly the foundation of faith.

    When Northern Christians reflect on the Three-Year Movement, they do not dedicate much space to discussion; some former leaders only briefly self-assess, stating, “Although several small publications were issued, the expected results could not be achieved due to the tense period of war.” In reality, the types of published materials are not few, but there remains room for improvement in their actual effects once these publications reach the hands of believers.

    However, it cannot be denied that under the promotion of Pastor Xiao Leshan (蕭樂善) and others, the Three-Year Movement indeed helped enhance the quality and quantity of Christians’ faith recognition. Through in-depth discussions of faith concepts and practices in newspapers and magazines, it benefited Christians’ self-reflection and motivation. In 1938, the Taipei Theological School and the Tamsui Women’s Study Institute officially established their boards, further solidifying educational institutions. The number of believers in 1938 increased to 9,667, leading to the Northern Conference being divided into three, forming the Taipei, Hsinchu, and Eastern Conferences, laying the foundation for the elevation of the “General Assembly.” The significance of the Three-Year Movement’s trajectory in the history of the Taiwan church is undeniable.

    1. The Closure of Tainan Theological School

      At the end of May 1940, a week after Tachikawa Masaharu (大川正) assumed the position of principal in Taipei, the Tainan Prefecture Education Department required the Tainan Theological School to appoint a Japanese principal. On June 11, a regular meeting of the Clerical Council was convened, where the acting principal, Hikari Chizu (希禮智), reported on the process of meeting with the education department head. The Clerical Council disagreed with the official request and decided to abandon the operation of the theological school, citing three main points: first, to arrange for male students to transfer to Taipei Theological School; second, for the female students of the Women’s Bible Society to transfer to Tamsui Bible School; and third, to notify Taiwanese church leaders of this decision and convey the Clerical Council’s position on the United Theological School.

      After achieving a preliminary consensus, to understand the views of key church leaders in the south, Yang Shiyang (楊士養) was privately asked to inquire about the thoughts of Taiwanese leaders. Many had held all-night discussions about the existence of the theological school, and in the end, they all agreed. The Taiwanese said:

      “However, if the Japanese insist on appointing the principal and that leads to closure, it poses a great danger given the current situation. So we must be very cautious.” As a result, it was communicated to Pastor Hikari that if the Clerical Council intended to close the institution and send the students to Taipei, we would agree to that.

      On June 21, 1940, the Southern Conference held a standing committee meeting, during which they learned that the Clerical Council would no longer continue operating the theological school. The Taiwanese were at first noncommittal but, feeling regretful, formed a ten-person group to convene a “Missionary Training Committee” to strategize. On July 7, Wan Ronghua (萬榮華) immediately wired the British parent church: “COLLEGE CLOSED STUDENTS GOING NORTH SEPTEMBER EDWARD BAND.” On the morning of July 17, the Missionary Training Committee met in Taiping, Tainan City, where Er Lang (二郎) and Banjiang Tiexiong (番匠鐵雄) both encouraged continuing the operation of the theological school.

      In the afternoon, the Southern Conference held a standing committee meeting, expressing a desire to continue operating the theological school but requiring that the annual funding of 10,000 yuan be equally shared by the four conferences, with a set deadline for response. If they could not bear the costs, students would be sent to Taipei for study, and they hoped that the assets of both schools would be provided for the establishment of a unified theological school.

      Looking at the church finances for 1940, the Kaohsiung Conference had total income of 5,613.55 yuan, total expenditure of 4,219.26 yuan, with a surplus of only 1,394.29 yuan; the Chiayi Conference had total income of 4,695.07 yuan, total expenditure of 3,771.80 yuan, with a surplus of only 923.27 yuan. Moreover, the total budget for the Southern Conference over two years was only 1,590 yuan. This financial condition was indeed insufficient to support the operational costs of the theological school. Consequently, on July 22, the Tainan Conference resolved: “This standing committee cannot bear the 2,500 yuan operational expenses for the theological school but hopes that the unified theological school centered around the Taiwan Conference can be realized.” On the 23rd, the Kaohsiung Conference resolved: “This conference cannot support the proposal due to the heavy financial burden, but fully agrees with the standing committee’s decision.” The replies from the Taichung and Chiayi Conferences were similar, clearly indicating that none of the four southern conferences could bear the significant expenses.

      The desire of the Taiwanese to independently manage the theological school was unachievable, leading them to begin handling the closure procedures with faculty and students, arranging for teachers to transfer and students to move schools, while applying to the governor’s office for disbandment. During the closure service, attendees felt a heavy and mournful atmosphere, with some church elders expressing a sense of “spiritual unrest.” This outcome left Miyauchi Akira (宮內彰) unemployed after being considered for the position of principal, which greatly displeased the recommender, Er Lang (二郎). Public opinion believed that the foreign powers withdrawing allowed local individuals to rise in prominence.

      The missionaries summarized, saying, “For a long time, we truly hoped that the Northern and Southern Theological Schools could unite, but unfortunately, this has not been successful. This time, the authorities want us to appoint a local person as principal, and luckily, Taipei Theological School has already invited Mr. Tachikawa, a very good candidate, to take the position, so it is quite appropriate for our students to go there. This is indeed the first step toward unity. Additionally, this way, we can save a lot of expenses, as traditionally, most of the funding for theological schools has come from British support. However, due to the current exceptional times, there are financial difficulties, and they are worried that they might not be able to sustain this funding each year.

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Imamura Fuyuzumi (今川淵)

Imamura Fuyuzumi (今川淵) (1886-1955), from Fukui Prefecture, Japan. He graduated from the political science department of the Tokyo Imperial University in 1912 and passed the higher civil service examination the following year, subsequently joining the Taiwan Governor’s Office. In July 1919, Imamura Fuyuzumi succeeded Motoharu Fudō as the head of Taipei City, overseeing administrative affairs in areas such as New Taipei, Yilan, and Keelung. He served as the governor of Tainan Prefecture from March 1932 to February 1936, later holding the positions of governor of Taipei Prefecture and director of the Monopoly Bureau. Additionally, the architect of the Taiwan Governor’s Office, Ikeda Kaoru (井手薫), was his brother-in-law.4

In 1931, Tainan Presbyterian Middle School faced public criticism for refusing to worship at Japanese shrines. At that time, Governor Imamura Fuyuzumi saw an opportunity to forcibly take over the male and female schools. With the aid of Japanese believers, he temporarily alleviated the crisis by hiring Japanese nationals as principals and board chairs of both schools. However, the two schools in Tamsui, Taipei, were mercilessly attacked in the media, especially by the “Kōseikai” (皇政会), which primarily consisted of the “Yamato” (大和) underworld group. They launched a campaign called the “Tamsui School Elimination Movement,” claiming that the land of Tamsui Junior High School was a “permanent leased site” from the unequal treaties of the Qing Dynasty and questioned why, in the “Holy Era of Shōwa” (昭和之聖代), such obsolete leased land was still allowed to remain on Japanese “imperial soil.” They aimed to force the church to give up the management of the schools.

In August 1936, the church managing Tamsui Junior High School, facing the increasingly difficult situation, made necessary compromises by replacing Principal Huang Chih-Lien with the Japanese-speaking Reverend Hideo Naka (明有德牧師) and hiring a retired Japanese army lieutenant as an instructor to implement military training courses. They hoped this reform would ensure the safety of both schools.

Unexpectedly, two years earlier, the key figure in the “Tainan Presbyterian Middle School Incident,” Governor Imamura Fuyuzumi, had been transferred to serve as the governor of Taipei Prefecture. At this juncture, Suzuki Isamu (鈴木勇), a Japanese instructor at Tamsui Junior High School, took advantage of his resignation to return to Japan and accused the church of promoting “unpatriotic sentiments” through the media. This controversy reignited concerns about the survival of Tamsui Junior High School and provided a pretext for Imamura Fuyuzumi, who was scheming all along. Imamura immediately pressured the church to transfer the management of Tamsui Junior High School and Tamsui Girls’ College to his organized group, implying that there would be severe consequences if the church did not comply. He also manipulated the newspapers to undermine Tamsui Junior High, distorting the narrative that students and Taiwanese believers were unhappy with the church’s management, leading to conflicts. Additionally, he sent military police to interrogate two teachers under the pretext of “instability and anti-national thoughts.”

In August 1936, while the church in Taipei and Taiwanese believers were trying to find a way to resolve this unreasonable demand, Takachiwa Yoshio (立川義男), the education official dispatched by Imamura Fuyuzumi, suddenly arrived to take over both schools and forcibly assumed the principal’s role. It became apparent that under Imamura Fuyuzumi’s coercion, the church had no choice but to concede and hand over both male and female Tamsui Junior High Schools to the Japanese government. Thus, Tamsui Junior High School ended its missionary era and entered the period of Japan’s management.5

Anti-Christian Organizations

Huangmin Fenggonghui (皇民奉公會)

In April 1941, the Taiwan Governor-General’s Office established the “Huangmin Fenggonghui” (皇民奉公會) in conjunction with Japan’s “Taisei Yokusankai” (大政翼贊會), aimed at cooperating in the war and mobilizing all human and material resources. The Governor-General’s Office utilized the existing administrative system to set up branches step by step, with officers from the Governor-General’s Office also acting as leaders of the organization, recruiting people from various sectors to participate. This was intended to call upon the entire island of Taiwan to promote the “Huangmin Fenggong” (皇民奉公) movement, training the islanders to increase production for the nation, establish a wartime living system, and transform Taiwan into a wartime base. 8

The Huangmin Fenggong movement emphasized “the practice of loyalty,” initiated and carried out by the Governor-General’s Office, which highlighted a more official-led character compared to the somewhat “ambiguous” nature of the Huangminization movement. 9

To align with national policies, the Huangmin Fenggonghui prioritized promoting the concepts of “the essence of national identity” and “reverence for gods and ancestors” (where reverence for gods refers to the worship of Shinto and the “living god” Emperor). It established departments under the central headquarters, such as General Affairs, Propaganda, Training, Culture, Life, and Economy, organizing subordinate groups like the Industrial Fenggong Team, the Volunteer Fenggong Team, the Literary Fenggong Association, the Kikyō Club, the Youth Fenggong Team, and the Elderly Volunteer Corps, among others. Through lectures, exhibitions, printed materials, and other publicity efforts, the Huangmin Fenggonghui instilled the concept of “fighting for His Majesty the Emperor” as a holy war, covering wide-ranging aspects, such as publishing a series of “Huangmin Fenggong Collections” in 1941 and 1942, and distributing the Huangmin Fenggonghui journal “New Construction” (新建設) as a propaganda tool. In simpler terms, the Huangmin Fenggonghui was a mobilization organization for all of Taiwan, attempting to exert the effects of propaganda, with various associations, committees, and local groups needing to adhere to the spirit of reverence for gods and ancestors, where rituals like worshipping the palace and honoring the spirit altar were considered necessary.

The Huangmin Fenggonghui was closely integrated with the administrative structure of the Governor-General’s Office, with its basic organization largely adopting the model of Japan’s Taisei Yokusankai. In addition to the “regional organizations” formed according to administrative divisions, the Huangmin Fenggonghui gradually incorporated various groups into “vocational organizations,” including the Taiwan Industrial Fenggong Team, Commercial Fenggong Team, Youth Fenggong Team, Kikyō Club, Greater Japan Women’s Association, Patriotic Women’s Association, Taiwan Drama Association, Taiwan Buddhist Fenggong Team, Literary Fenggong Association, Fine Arts Fenggong Association, Music Fenggong Association, and various other cultural and artistic groups. The Christian leader Lin Maosheng (林茂生) was appointed as a cadre of the Huangmin Fenggonghui. 10

In early August 1942, the “Taiwan Christian Volunteer Service Association,” which had been established for 5 years in response to expand the organization and in line with the Huangmin Fenggonghui, underwent a series of reorganizations. Under the encouragement of the Japanese, over 1,300 Japanese and Taiwanese Christians held a ceremony to establish the “Taiwan Christian Fenggonghui” (臺灣基督教奉公會) at the Taipei Church Hall, electing 17 directors including Zhang Yu (上与二郎) and local committee members. By the end of August, under the control of the board, it was renamed “Taiwan Christian Fenggong Team” (臺灣基督教奉公團), and it was decided that the main purpose was: “to uphold the sacred mission of the declaration of war against the United States and Britain, to fulfill the responsibilities of the Japanese people and to devote Christianity to the nation, with the goal of forming loyal citizens.” Its actual activities included “organizing workshops, lectures, and enrichment meetings to enhance members’ understanding, cultivate national wartime consciousness, guide the wartime lives of citizens, assist the implementation of the national language to promote unification, and hold victory prayer meetings,” etc.

In December 1942, the Tainan local chapter of the Fenggong Team (奉公團) gathered 400 believers for a founding ceremony; 11 in January of the following year (1943), the Kaohsiung local chapter and the Taitung local chapter were established in succession. The former held its founding ceremony at the Kaohsiung City Hall, presided over by Director Zhang Yu (上与二郎), with 300 believers participating; the latter held its founding ceremony at the local militia assembly hall in Taitung Street, also presided over by Pastor Zhang, with 200 believers attending. 12 In summary, the Taiwan Christian Fenggong Team serves as an outer organization of the Huangmin Fenggonghui (皇民奉公會), driving the church to cooperate in the war. Although it is not a group directly governed by the church, it provides the Japanese with the space to direct all churches, unifying Taiwanese Christians into a broad wartime cooperative entity.

The Kominka Movement

In 1937, with the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese War, Taiwan initiated the “Kominka Movement” (皇民化運動) to align with Japan’s “Total Mobilization for National Spirit” (國民精神總動員運動). This movement carried on the essence of social education and can be seen as a series of measures to promote “Imperial Subjectification” (皇國臣民化) in colonial Taiwan. The Kominka Movement continued the key aspects of social education, reinforcing the use of Japanese language, mandating visits to shrines, encouraging family worship of the “Jingu Daima” (神宮大麻), and promoting Japanese lifestyles. Starting in 1940, it further encouraged “name changes” as a means to strengthen Japanese spirit. In terms of education, it prohibited the teaching of Classical Chinese in schools, abolished Chinese language sections in newspapers, banned traditional opera, rearranged temples, and improved the main halls of Taiwanese families, all with the aim of eliminating Han ethnic characteristics. Governor Kobayashi Taiza (小林躋造) clearly stated that the three principles of his rule in Taiwan were “Imperialization, Industrialization, and Southern Expansion Base Establishment.” 13 The Kominka Movement affected nearly all social strata, covering a vast range of aspects in colonial life, 14 and the Sino-Japanese War drove colonial Taiwan into a wartime system, compelling the governor’s office to assimilate Taiwanese people into true “Imperial Subjects” (皇國臣民), instilling in them the patriotism and spirit of sacrifice of Japanese citizens. 15 Clearly, the Kominka Movement significantly impacted the daily lives of Taiwanese people, including Christians.

  1. Northern Church Three-Year Movement

    During the Japanese colonial period, the Northern Church gradually transitioned from its initial establishment phase to a structured pastoral phase.
    The missionaries born in this era witnessed significant changes within the Northern Church.
    In the 1930s, young missionaries were greatly influenced by Japanese Christian socialist He Chuan Feng (賀川豐彥)’s “Kingdom of God Movement” and learned from the experiences of Chinese Christians in promoting the “Five-Year Movement,” embracing the concept of “revival preaching.”
    The missionaries observed the experiences of Japanese Christian churches and learned about rigorous procedural governance to discuss the revitalization of the church.

    Therefore, in 1934, during the 11th Northern Missionaries’ General Assembly held in Tamsui, the young missionaries proposed the concept of the “Three-Year Movement,” focusing on the responsibility of evangelism and aiming to consolidate the church’s faith and establish the essence of Christianity.
    At the same time, considering that the Canadian mother church had assisted Taiwanese for over 60 years, Christians began to look forward to self-government.
    In 1935, the Canadian mother church successfully approved this plan, and the Northern Conference officially authorized the proposal for the three-year movement submitted by the General Assembly of missionaries.

    In November 1935, the “Northern Christian Three-Year Movement Department” was officially established, with missionary Ming Youde (明有德) serving as the director and Xiao Leshan (蕭樂善) as the administrator (general secretary), positioning this movement as a “full mobilization of the church,” aiming to “revive the faith, promote the spirit of Christ, guide people to return to the Lord, and establish churches with the life of God,” clearly marking the direction of effort.

    In January 1936, the Three-Year Movement was launched, with the ministry divided into three parts: one was the Revival Department, focusing on encouraging members to advance and improve, emphasizing internal renewal within the church; the second was the Soul-Saving Department, aiming for members to actively invite others to believe, stressing external expansion of the church; the third was the Youth Department, encouraging Sunday schools and youth groups to be active and progressive, specifically targeting specific age groups to cultivate the most malleable young people to become the foundation of the church.

    In 1937, the second phase of the Three-Year Movement began, and after a year of effort, Northern Christians felt signs of revitalization within the church, looking forward to continuous reflection and avoiding jealousy or contempt for the Bible.
    Xiao Leshan further encouraged believers to “speak the truth in love,” practicing justice and mercy so as not to fall short of God’s grace.
    He believed that Christians needed to train themselves to be loyal to God, spreading the correct faith throughout Taiwan, calling on Christians to accept the mission of evangelism and support one another so both missionaries and lay believers could find their place to contribute.

    In 1938, as the final phase of the Three-Year Movement commenced, Christians still focused more on internal developments within the church.
    Xiao Leshan pointed out that although the North had over 70 churches that appeared strong, the actual spirit of evangelism needed to be strengthened, especially amidst the looming international conflict and financial difficulties facing the Canadian mother church, Christians needed to be particularly careful.
    He encouraged everyone to take on the role of “trailblazers,” asserting that progress can only be made through action, expecting Christians to love the Lord more, harmonize, walk the right path without fear of obstacles, and make the Three-Year Movement a model for future generations.

    Looking at the implementation effects of the Three-Year Movement, the first year saw numerous activities such as launch ceremonies, lectures, and special gatherings, divided into three main objectives: Revival, Youth, and Soul-Saving, which made the execution of affairs more systematic, with considerable participation from attendees. The second and third years shifted the focus more towards “documentary preaching.” A significant number of publications were produced during these three years, but the majority of gathering formats leaned towards static events.

    In fact, the setbacks of the Three-Year Movement were due to changes in the political environment.
    Affected by World War II, Christians could not focus on the revival movement of faith, nor could they hold public gatherings. In 1938, Xiao Leshan expressed his regret, noting that many believers were conscripted for “labor service” to build defense works, thus unable to maintain church life.

    Clearly, “situational factors” restricted large gatherings, and the activities held mostly resembled static lecture meetings and Bible study sessions, similar in nature to regular Sunday worship.
    Christians passively listened and studied, lacking the “going out” aspect of external evangelism and opportunities for direct interactions with non-Christians, which naturally reduced the chances of conversions.
    However, Xiao Leshan always hoped that the congregation would have a stronger foundation in the Bible and strengthen Bible study through the promotion of journals and publications, a philosophy that undoubtedly forms the foundation of faith.

    When Northern Christians reflected on the Three-Year Movement, there was not much discussion on it.
    Some former executives merely self-evaluated briefly, stating, “Although we published several small periodicals, we did not achieve the expected results due to entering a period of war tension.”
    In reality, the variety of published materials was not small, yet the actual effects of each publication in the hands of believers still had room for improvement.

    However, it cannot be denied that under the promotion of Pastor Xiao Leshan and others, the Three-Year Movement indeed helped improve the quality and quantity of Christian faith identification, as discussions on faith concepts and practices through newspapers and magazines benefited Christians’ self-reflection and motivation.
    In 1938, the boards of directors for Taipei Theological School and Tamsui Women’s School were officially established, further solidifying educational institutions.
    The number of believers in 1938 increased to 9,667, facilitating the division of the Northern Conference into three separate conferences: Taipei, Hsinchu, and Eastern, laying the foundation for the elevation of the “General Assembly.”
    The significance of the Three-Year Movement’s direction in the history of the Church in Taiwan cannot be overlooked.

    1. Tainan Theological School Closure Incident

      At the end of May 1940, one week after Da Chuan Zheng (大川正) took office as president in Taipei, the Tainan Education Division demanded that Tainan Theological School also appoint a Japanese person as president.
      On June 11, the Clergy Council held a routine meeting, during which the acting president, Xi Li Zhi (希禮智), reported on the encounter with the Director of the Education Division.
      The Clergy Council disagreed with the official request and decided to abandon the management of the theological school, citing three main points: first, to arrange for male students to transfer to Taipei Theological School; second, for female students of the Women’s Bible Society to transfer to Tamsui Bible School; third, to notify Taiwanese church leaders of this resolution and convey the Clergy Council’s views on the united theological school.
      After reaching a preliminary consensus, the Clergy Council sought to understand the opinions of key figures in the southern churches by asking Yang Shiyang to privately inquire about the Taiwanese leaders’ views, many of whom often discussed the closure of the theological school overnight in private discussions, ultimately expressing agreement.
      Taiwanese leaders stated:

      “However, if the Japanese insist on having a president, then closing it poses a significant danger in light of the current situation. Hence, we must be very careful.”
      As a result, it was communicated to Pastor Xi that if the Clergy Council wanted to shut down the school, the students would be sent to Taipei, which they agreed to support.

      On June 21, 1940, a permanent committee was convened in the south, where the news of the Clergy Council’s decision to discontinue management of the theological school was heard.
      Taiwanese members were initially noncommittal but, feeling reluctant, a group of ten convened a “Missionary Training Division” to discuss countermeasures.
      On July 7, Wan Ronghua (萬榮華) immediately notified the British mother church, stating, “COLLEGE CLOSED, STUDENTS GOING NORTH SEPTEMBER EDWARD BAND” (學校關閉學生九月北上萬榮華).
      On the morning of July 17, the Missionary Training Division held a meeting in Tainan City at Taipingjing, where Shang Yu Er Lang and Ban Jiang Tie Xiong encouraged the continuation of managing the theological school.
      In the afternoon, the Southern Conference held a permanent committee meeting, expressing a desire to continue managing the theological school; however, each of the four conferences needed to share the annual funding of 10,000 Yuan and was given a deadline for response.
      If unable to afford it, students would be sent to Taipei to study, and both schools were encouraged to provide resources for the establishment of a united theological school.

      Reviewing the church finances for 1940, the total income for the Kaohsiung Conference was 5,613.55 Yuan, total expenditures were 4,219.26 Yuan, leaving a surplus of only 1,394.29 Yuan; the Chiayi Conference had total income of 4,695.07 Yuan, total expenditures of 3,771.80 Yuan, resulting in a surplus of only 923.27 Yuan.
      Moreover, the total budget for the Southern Conference over two years was only 1,590 Yuan.
      Such financial conditions were truly unable to support the expenses of the theological school.
      Thus, on July 22, the Tainan Conference resolved: “This permanent committee cannot bear the 2,500 Yuan operating cost for the theological school, but hopes that a united theological school, predominantly led by the Taiwan Conference, can be realized.”
      On the 23rd, the Kaohsiung Conference decided: “Our conference does not support this due to financial burdens; we unanimously support the decision of the executive committee.”

          Kōminka Society (皇民奉公會)

          In April 1941, the Taiwan Governor-General’s Office, in conjunction with Japan’s major political mobilization organization “Taisei Yokusankai” (大政翼贊會), established the “Kōminka Society” (皇民奉公會), aiming to assist in the war effort by mobilizing all human and material resources.
          The Governor’s Office utilized the existing administrative system to gradually set up branch organizations, where officials served as executives of the society while enlisting participation from various sectors of society to promote the “Kōminka” movement across the island.
          The goal was to train the local population to increase production in service to the nation and establish a wartime lifestyle, turning the island into a military base.8

          The Kōminka movement emphasized “the practice of loyalty,” with the Governor’s Office initiating and executing it, thus highlighting the official character of the movement more distinctly compared to the more “ambiguous” nature of the “Kōminka Movement.” 9

          To align with national policy, the Kōminka Society prioritized promoting the concepts of “National Identity” and “Reverence for God and Ancestors” (where reverence included honoring Shinto and the “living god” Emperor).
          Various departments were established under the central headquarters, including General Affairs, Propaganda, Training, Culture, Life, and Economy, organizing affiliated groups such as the Industry Volunteer Corps, Active Volunteer Corps, Literary Volunteer Society, Kikyō Club (桔梗俱樂部), Young Volunteers, and Senior Volunteer Services.
          Through lectures, exhibitions, and printed materials, the Kōminka Society promoted the idea of “fighting for His Majesty the Emperor,” covering a wide range of topics, such as the publication of the “Kōminka Society Series” in 1941 and 1942, as well as the release of the Kōminka Society’s organ “New Construction,” which served as a propaganda tool.
          In short, the Kōminka Society was a mobilizing organization throughout Taiwan, aiming to maximize its propaganda effects, with various councils and committees needing to adhere to the spirit of revering God and ancestors, engaging in necessary rituals such as remote worship of the imperial palace and shrine offerings.

          Integrated with the administrative structure of the Governor’s Office, the Kōminka Society largely followed the model of Japan’s Taisei Yokusankai.
          In addition to “regional organizations” formed according to administrative districts, the Kōminka Society also gradually incorporated various groups into “occupational organizations,” including the Taiwan Industry Volunteer Corps, Business Volunteer Corps, Young Men’s Volunteer Corps, Taiwan Youth Corps, Kikyō Club, Dai Nippon Women’s Association, Patriotic Women’s Association, Taiwan Drama Association, Taiwan Buddhist Volunteer Corps, Literary Volunteer Society, Art Volunteer Society, Music Volunteer Society, and other cultural organizations.
          Christian leader Lin Maosheng (林茂生) was appointed as a cadre for the Kōminka Society.10

          In early August 1942, the “All Taiwan Christian Service Association for the North China Incident,” which had been established for five years, underwent a series of reorganizations to expand its reach and respond to the Kōminka Society.
          Under the invitation of the Japanese, more than 1,300 Japanese and Taiwanese Christians gathered at the Taipei Church Hall to hold the founding ceremony for the “Taiwan Christian Service Association,” electing 17 directors, including Shang Yu Er Lang (上與二郎), and local committee members.
          By the end of August, manipulated by the board of directors, it was renamed the “Taiwan Christian Volunteer Corps” (referred to as the “Volunteer Corps”) and established regulations clearly stating its purpose: “to adhere to the imperial decree of war against the United States and Britain, to fulfill the responsibilities of the imperial people, and to commit Christianity to serve the nation, thus cultivating the imperial citizens as the objective.”
          Its practical operations included “organizing workshops, lectures, and spiritual retreats aimed at enhancing members’ understanding, uplifting national wartime consciousness, guiding the national wartime lifestyle, assisting in promoting the national language to achieve unification, and holding prayer meetings for victories in battle.”

          In December 1942, the Tainan division of the Volunteer Corps summoned 400 believers for its founding ceremony; in January the following year (1943), the Kaohsiung division and Taitung division were successively established, with the former holding its founding ceremony at the Kaohsiung City Church Hall presided over by director Shang Yu Er Lang, attended by 300 believers; the latter held its ceremony at the Taitung Street strongman gathering place, also presided over by Pastor Shang, with 200 believers attending.

          In summary, the Taiwan Christian Volunteer Corps was an auxiliary organization of the Kōminka Society, compelling churches to cooperate with the war effort.
          Although it was not a group directly governed by the church, it provided space for the Japanese to direct the entire church, effectively uniting Taiwanese Christians into a broad wartime cooperation group.
