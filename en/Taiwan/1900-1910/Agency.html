Taiwan: Agency (1900-1910)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

安彼得（1879-1910）

安彼得（Peter Anderson, 1847-1913）began his service in Tainan on January 14, 1879, and returned to England for vacation in 1885. After coming to Taiwan for the second time in October 1887, he planned to build a new medical hall. After several years of dealing with complex land disputes, the “New Building Medical Hall” he supervised was completed on April 17, 1900. It was equipped with the first manual elevator in Taiwan, making it quite modern compared to the “Old Building Medical Hall” run by the two elderly doctors. After 1901, the new building was managed by Dr. 小馬雅各 (James Laidlaw Maxwell Jr., 1873-1951), while 安彼得 moved to Takao.

Mrs. Peter Anderson (安夫人, 1847-1906), a Scottish woman, arrived in Taiwan on December 29, 1882. She left Taiwan on September 20, 1906, accompanied by her husband due to illness, and passed away in Edinburgh on December 27 of the same year. During her time in Taiwan, she assisted her husband, engaged in nursing work, taught patients to read and write in pe̍h-ōe-jī, and helped with the establishment of the new medical hall.

After his wife’s death, 安彼得 returned to Takao from England on April 2, 1907. In May 1908, when 小馬雅各 returned to England for vacation, he collaborated with 蘭大衛 (David Landsborough, 1870-1957) to help at the New Building Medical Hall. Due to health issues, 安彼得 went to Japan for recuperation in April 1909 and returned to Taiwan on May 7. He retired and returned to England in the autumn of 1910. 安彼得 spent a total of 31 years in Taiwan and passed away in his hometown of 珀斯 (Parthshire) on March 12, 1913, at the age of 66. (朱真一, 2012: 67-75)

According to a report by Yang Shufen of the 中華日報 on September 7, 2023, about the proposals for monuments at the 南山公墓幼兒墓園, 安彼得’s second daughter died young in 1891 and was buried in the 三分子公墓, later moved to the present 南山公墓 in 1934. The inscription reads, “The Grave of the Second Daughter of 大英 安. Departed this life on the second day of August 1891, Jesus called a child. See Matthew 18:2.” The biblical reference commemorates “Jesus called a little child and set him in the midst of them,” indicating that children are received in the kingdom of heaven.

In 2023, due to announcements regarding the relocation and planting preservation at the Tainan Christian Cemetery, on September 6, the public proposed the cultural asset “Taiwan Presbyterian Church Early Departed Members Cemetery (32 doors).” On January 17, 2024, a site inspection and registration tracking review meeting was conducted by the Tainan City Cultural Assets Management Office, and on February 26, it was stated that the case would not be registered for tracking at this time but that cultural asset investigations should continue and relocation operations deferred. The 安彼得 family can be said to have a close association with the history of the New Building Hospital and the development of Tainan City.

梅監務（1895-1931）

On October 16, 1895, 梅監務 departed from London with physician 蘭大衛 (David Landsborough) and pastor 廉德烈 (A. B. Nielson), arriving at 安平港 on December 18. Beginning in November 1896, 梅 and 蘭 established their main workplace in 彰化, an emerging and prosperous trading and administrative center, which could be considered the second “sub-mission center” after Tainan at that time. They also conducted medical mission work in 大社 through routine medical preaching, for instance, starting from April 1897, they engaged in four months of medical mission work in 大社. Additionally, while Dr. 蘭 worked in medical services, 梅監務 diligently preached in 彰化, 台中, 大社, 西螺, 南投, and 埔里. His usual method of preaching involved beating gongs, blowing trumpets, and loudly proclaiming, “The Son of God is missing!” During a visit to 澎湖 in 1898, he became good friends with Han Chinese preacher 林學恭 (林赤馬), later inviting him to join as a co-worker. He also led local resident 楊福春 to faith and worked together to develop 清水.

In July 1900, 梅 and 蘭 returned to England for vacation. By the end of that year, due to health issues (suffering from malaria), 梅監務 requested a transfer from the British Presbyterian Church’s overseas mission committee to work in Qing China. After discussions, 梅監務 was reassigned to Singapore, which had a large Chinese community, and he departed for that location in September 1901. However, his time in Singapore was not happy, and he continued to suffer from malaria. He longed for his colleagues in Taiwan and thought, “If malaria is bound to recur, he would rather fall ill in 福爾摩莎!”」On August 9, 1902, 梅監務 returned to Taiwan to work in 彰化, continuing to develop the ministry in 大社, 豐原, and 埔里. From May to August 1903, he worked with Dr. 蘭 on routine medical preaching in 大社. Starting in 1904, 梅監務 began leading Taiwanese preachers in studying the letters to the Corinthians, believing that Taiwan’s missionary situation bore similarities to the pagan world Paul faced.

Missionary Writings

In the autumn of 1906, 梅 and 蘭 returned to England for vacation. In 1907, 梅監務 published “The Heathen Heart,” a reflection on his missionary experiences that received significant attention. In early 1908, he married his mentor’s daughter, 瑪格麗特 (Margaret Rintoul Findlay), and in March of that year, he returned to 彰化 with his new bride, receiving a warm welcome. Their new home, supported by 12 brick columns, was also nicknamed “The Twelve Apostles.” 梅監務 also published “Commentary on Romans” written in Romanized characters. After Mrs. 梅 arrived in Taiwan, she actively learned Taiwanese from 林學恭 (Lin Chih-ma), but unfortunately, she had to go to Australia for recuperation early in the following year due to a severe lung infection, leading 梅監務 to resign from his work in Taiwan. Dr. 蘭 noted in his diary, “This was a sad farewell; 梅監務 deeply loved the Taiwanese people, and they loved him back… When we first arrived in 彰化 12 years ago, there were only four churches in central Taiwan. Through 梅監務’s diligent efforts, there are now 23 small churches. He should take pride in that.”

At the end of 1914, the couple returned to 彰化 and was welcomed with great enthusiasm. He published a work entitled “On Preaching,” firmly believing that the method for spreading the Gospel was through “evangelistic open-air preaching.” In April 1915, he and Mrs. 梅 went to Japan for treatment at 有馬, but they saw no improvement. Mrs. 梅 passed away in November of that year after returning to Taiwan, leaving them together for only eight years. Mrs. 梅’s funeral was presided over by Pastor 劉忠堅 (Duncan McLeod), and 梅監務 himself led the band, singing “Heaven Has No Suffering,” along with holding open-air preaching, drawing silent attention from the citizens of 彰化. In 1916, 梅監務 suffered from severe influenza, which, due to delayed treatment, resulted in a lung infection. After being cared for by Dr. 戴仁壽 (George Gushue-Taylor), he was advised to return to England for recuperation.

In September 1918, 梅監務 returned to Taiwan from England to continue his work and spent the summer of 1919 in 淡水 at the foot of 大屯山, preaching to nearby villages. In 1920, he published “The Mind of the Early Converts,” which received high praise. On November 29, 1921, he remarried, this time to nurse 洪伯祺 (Margaret Christian Arthur) from 彰化 Medical Hall. From the end of 1922, 梅監務 was assigned to Tainan Theological College to take over the work from Pastor 宋忠堅 and published the book “Ko-Cha e Kau-Hoe” written in Romanized characters.

In 1924, 梅監務 grew increasingly weak due to recurrent fevers and had to leave 福爾摩莎 in June to return to England for recuperation. He had hoped to return to Taiwan soon, but things did not go as planned, and he said farewell to Taiwan. During his time in England, he published “The Purpose of Jesus” (1929), “The King’s Guest” (1932), “Christ for Us and in Us” (1935), and “The Childhood of the Church” (1938). In 1928, he was awarded an honorary Doctor of Divinity (D.D.) degree by his alma mater, the University of Glasgow, and he retired to England in 1931 due to health issues. On February 28, 1940, 梅監務 passed away peacefully in Lennoxtown, Scotland, at the age of 75. A humble figure, 梅監務 was an evangelist, theologian, and sacred musician, often referred to as the “beggar of England” and “the Paul of Taiwan.” (鄭仰恩, 2000: 22-27)

費仁純（1900-1908在臺）

費仁純 (Mr. F. R.Johnson) arrived in Taiwan on January 12, 1901, succeeding 余饒理 as the second principal of the Presbyterian Middle School. During his tenure, he actively engaged in school affairs, incorporating instruction in Chinese, Japanese, and English, thus diversifying the educational approach. In 1906, he received official recognition from the Governor-General of Taiwan. He resigned on July 4, 1908, and returned to his home country.

Mrs. 費 began her time in Taiwan on November 17, 1901, and returned home on July 1, 1908. Her main work involved teaching “poetic meter” (aimed at helping students learn poetry) as part of the music curriculum. 林錦生 (Lin Ching-sheng, 1894-1980) was among those she nurtured. In 1901, she selected him from the Sunday school students at 太平境 to enter middle school, and in 1905, she chose him to learn to play the organ. At that time, the 太平境 chapel had a medium-sized organ, and Mrs. 費 served as the organist, wishing to cultivate local organists and pass on the duties. The only student who had learned to play the organ at that time was 林茂生 (Lin Mau-sheng). After graduating from middle school in 1907, he gained the recognition of Pastor 甘為霖 (Gan Weilin) to assist in dictionary compilation, also with Mrs. 費’s recommendation. (賴永祥, 1995)

According to the minutes of the Tainan Clergy Association’s 767th meeting on July 22, 1908, the contributions made by Mr. and Mrs. 費 during their seven years in 福爾摩莎 were significant; they were involved not only in supervising the middle school but also in valuable roles as the financial officer and library manager of the clergy association. This indicates that the couple had strong managerial and administrative skills. (Campbell, 1910: 767)

何希仁 (Hope Moncrieff, 1909-1915 in Taiwan)

Pastor 何希仁 succeeded the resigned pastor 梅監務 (Campbell Moody) in 1909 and continued the missionary work in central Taiwan. In 1910, the Tainan Clergy Association decided to establish 彰化 as a subordinate center for mission work, requesting the London headquarters to build missionary accommodations there, and he played a pivotal role in this initiative. In 1913, he led the establishment of a six-year “彰化小學” (Changhua Primary School) under the jurisdiction of the 彰化 church, which included a two-year preparatory middle school, hiring Pastor 黃俟命 (Huang Sihming) as the principal and teacher. Unfortunately, due to funding challenges and the pressures of public schooling, 何希仁 left Taiwan in 1915, and the primary school was discontinued in 1918.

From 1926 to 1929, Pastor 何 served as the principal of a theological school in 廈門, and from 1929 to 1934, he ministered in Edinburgh; during his retirement, he delivered speeches and conducted film tours and other matters. Detailed records of his gas projection and film lecture tours in the UK from 1933 to 1934 are kept at the University of London’s School of African and Oriental Studies, under the reference number GB 102 PCE/FMC/3/02/31c&d, covering the years 1926-1936 and 1938-1950.

Pastor 何 traveled extensively throughout Taiwan, reaching not only central Taiwan but also the outlying island of 澎湖 (Penghu). He published numerous articles in church bulletins between 1910 and 1915, focusing on three main categories: 1. Church news, 2. The Gospel and culture, 3. History, geography, and science.

He authored many works, including a translation of James S. Stewart’s “The Life and Teaching of Jesus Christ,” published by 廣學會 in 1940, which has been reprinted several times and remains in circulation. Another well-known translation is “Pi̍t Siâ Kui Chèng: 闢邪歸正,” published by 聚珍堂 in 1913. This material, translated by 何希仁 and others for evangelistic use, comprises 68 chapters: Chapters 1-12 discuss the attributes of God; Chapters 13-19 address the reasons against ancestor worship and other practices; Chapters 20-26 cover supernatural worldviews; Chapters 27-29 discuss the attributes of Jesus; Chapters 30-41 compare true and false religions; Chapters 42-63 analyze various folk religions; and Chapters 64-68 focus on improving social customs such as marriage and filial piety. Chapter 30, pertaining to the baptismal rites, was not translated.

In summary, 何希仁 can be viewed as someone who integrated reason with faith in his evangelistic efforts, continuing the legacy of 梅監務 and introducing contemporary Western scientific knowledge to Taiwan.

約美但 (Joy McDaniel) (1905-1917 in Taiwan)

約美但 (also known as “旦”) was born in Chateauguay, Quebec, Canada, to a grandfather who had immigrated from Scotland.His father, Robert Jack, was an advanced fruit farmer in Quebec, and his mother, Annie Linda, was a poet, writer, and horticulturist. The couple had 11 children, most of whom received their elementary education at home from their mother. 約美但 graduated first from the Presbyterian College of McGill University in 1905. After graduation, he continued his studies at the University of Chicago in the United States.

On November 3, 1905, 約美但, along with Pastors 宋雅各 (James) and Harriet Ferguson, and young women 金仁理 (Jane Kinney) and 高哈拿 (Hannah Connell), arrived in Taiwan as cross-cultural missionaries. After a period of language learning and understanding local customs, they engaged in medical work, women’s education, and theological education according to their individual passions, and established the 淡水女學校 (Tamsui Girls’ School). Soon, these young missionaries, together with the already established missionaries, Pastors 吳威廉 (William) and Margaret Gauld, formed the Canadian Mission Council, marking the beginning of the Canadian Presbyterian Church’s missionary collaboration team in northern Taiwan.

In 1906, 約美但 traveled to Hong Kong, where he married Catherine Winifred Bennett, the daughter of the pastor who baptized him (1882-1980). Their first child, Lilian, passed away three days after birth in Taiwan and was buried in the neighboring expatriate cemetery near 馬偕墓園. Subsequently, they had two sons and two daughters (Lawrence, Wilfred, Elsie, Marjorie) in Taiwan. Mrs. 約美但, Catherine, entered McGill University at the age of 15 and graduated first in her class at 19. Before marriage, she taught at a women’s college in Ontario; after marriage, in addition to caring for her children, she taught at 淡水女學校 and at the Christian Academy in Korea (now Yonsei University). Upon returning to Canada, she continued to teach to supplement the family income. Additionally, she was passionate about community service, held leadership roles, and served as president of the local United Church Women’s Missionary Society and Sunday School for over 20 years.

In 1907, 約美但 took over the administration of 牛津學堂 (Oxford School) from Pastor 吳威廉, preparing for its transformation into a theological school in Taipei. As a result, the admission requirements for 牛津學堂 were changed to require graduation from public schools designed specifically for Taiwanese Han people (equivalent to elementary schools), and an academic year system was established. This blended theoretical instruction with missionary teaching methods, allowing students to graduate after completing four to five academic years. In the first year of enrollment, three students completed the required courses and graduated. In addition to general subjects, biblical courses, and foundational theology, 約美但 introduced rational critical analysis and historical backgrounds in teaching to help students better understand and explain biblical messages.

Regarding teaching, 約美但 once wrote: “If foreign missionaries have no understanding of Japanese, they will be at a significant disadvantage in advising students on literature selection. The language of the younger generation is changing rapidly; even though some classical Chinese forms may be retained, it has effectively become more Japanese. Thus, for a group of Taiwanese students educated in Japanese primary or secondary schools, knowledge of Japanese is essential alongside Chinese for teaching any abstract subjects such as theology, to ensure clarity and improve teaching efficiency.”

Additionally, 約美但 aimed to cultivate local theological educators to be his successors, choosing several students to enhance their English capabilities with hopes that they would pursue further theological education abroad after graduation. Unfortunately, despite 約美但’s commitment to improving the quality of theological education, due to the limitations of the educational system at the time, graduates from 牛津學堂 or Taipei Theological School had to complete a regular secondary school curriculum before qualifying for admission to theological departments in Japanese universities.

Besides teaching, in February 1910, 約美但 was elected chairman at the 10th annual meeting of the Northern Taiwan Presbytery. He was also concerned about missionary work among the indigenous peoples in the east; in 1911, he accompanied Pastor 郭希信, who was serving the Klaman people in the Lanyang Plain, to travel from Keelung to the mountains by communication boat to understand the progress in the Amis mission area. Regarding this work, 約美但 wrote, “Every year, the Taiwan church (referring to the plain churches) dedicates offerings for the Amis mission in the Qilai Plain.”

Unfortunately, 約美但 later contracted malaria in Taiwan, and after treatment with quinine, he suffered hearing loss.In 1917, due to health issues, 約美但 requested a transfer to the cooler climate of Seoul, Korea, where he engaged in theological education at the newly established Korean Christian College. However, his hearing did not improve in Seoul, leading doctors to recommend outdoor work for him.

In 1920, 約美但 returned to Canada and resigned from his pastoral position to pursue his parents’ inspired interest—gardening. He established a floral nursery on the Mission Highlands facing the Fraser River Valley, working on the cultivation and sale of gladiolas, peonies, dahlias, irises, and daylilies. His products enjoyed a wide market across North America, the UK, Australia, and New Zealand. Due to his achievements in this field, a deep red variety of peony was named “Milton Jack” by his peers. (郭忠吉, 2022: 21)

宋雅各 (Dr. and Rev. J. Y. Ferguson, 1877-1965, often mistakenly referred to as George V. Ferguson) was a native of Toronto, Canada. He was dispatched by the Canadian Alliance Church to Taiwan to reopen the church hospital and became the second significant figure in the northern church’s medical mission history after 馬偕.

In 1905, at the request of the second Northern Presbytery of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church, Dr. 宋 was assigned by the Canadian Alliance Church to Taiwan to reopen the church hospital, arriving in 淡水 in November of the same year. Upon their arrival, the宋雅各 couple actively learned Taiwanese, and by the autumn of 1906, they reopened the hospital. Due to the lack of hospitals at that time and the doctor’s excellent skills and friendly demeanor, his reputation spread widely, attracting various patients from the northern regions. However, as 淡水’s prosperity declined and Japanese transport infrastructure centralized around Taipei, many people found the hospital’s location inconvenient.

On the other hand, Dr. 宋 hoped to meet contemporary standards and government requirements for a more equipped Christian hospital, as the hospital was becoming outdated. He collaborated with Pastor 吳威廉 (Rev. William Gauld) to plan a new hospital in Shuanglian and requested additional personnel from the Canadian headquarters to assist with medical work. Recognizing the potential of this endeavor, the Canadian headquarters allocated $25,000 for construction, and in 1911, nurse 烈姑娘 (Miss Isabel Elliot) arrived in 淡水. The following year, Dr. A. A. Gray and his wife were sent to Taiwan to assist with medical services.

As a result, Dr. 宋 relocated the medical center from 淡水 to Taiwan’s capital, Taipei, naming it 馬偕紀念醫院 (Mackay Memorial Hospital) in honor of Dr. 馬偕, the first missionary of the northern church. Consequently, Dr. 宋 became the second significant figure in the history of northern medical missions. As the founder of 馬偕紀念醫院, he significantly contributed to the hospital’s construction during his tenure as its first director. At one point, he took a year off for rest but returned to Taiwan to continue his service, fulfilling his mission at 馬偕醫院 for about five years.

In 1914, World War I broke out, impacting 馬偕醫院 as it faced a scarcity of medical supplies and personnel, forcing it to temporarily suspend operations in June 1918. Simultaneously, after years of dedicated service, Dr. 宋 devoted his most valuable fourteen years to Taiwan but ultimately left due to health issues that year, returning to Canada. He never returned to Taiwan afterward. Upon his return to Canada, he served as the head of surgery at Eastern General Hospital and continued to practice medicine independently until he passed away on February 23, 1965, in Toronto, Canada, at the age of 88.

Dr. 宋 had a deep affection for Taiwan, and although he had been away for decades, he often thought of the island, especially concerning the 馬偕紀念醫院 he founded. Whenever he met visiting clergy from Canada, he would inquire about the hospital’s news and often displayed photos of the hospital in his home. Even after the war, former patients would visit 馬偕醫院, seeking the doctor who had treated them and taught them to read the Bible and sing hymns.(1900-1923 in Taiwan)

Dr. 馬雅各二世 (James Laidlaw Maxwell Jr., 1873-1951) was a significant figure in Taiwan’s medical history. He was born into a family with a strong emphasis on education and community service. After completing his medical studies, he and his wife, 劉姆庭 (Margaret Rintoul Findlay), arrived in Taiwan as missionaries in 1900. They initially served with the Presbyterian Church, focusing on medical missions in the Tainan area.

Upon their arrival, the couple quickly became involved in the local community, providing medical care and organizing health education initiatives. Dr. 馬雅各 was known for his compassionate care and medical expertise, which earned him the trust and respect of the Taiwanese people. His approach to medicine was not just about treating illnesses; he believed in the importance of preventive care and community health education.

Throughout the years, he advocated for better health facilities and worked tirelessly to improve local healthcare services. Dr. 馬雅各 and his wife opened a small clinic and began training local medical staff, establishing a legacy that would enhance healthcare in Taiwan long after their departure.

In 1905, Dr. 馬雅各 played a crucial role in the founding of the new hospital in 香港, which became a center for medical training and service in the region. His efforts attracted attention and support from various churches and organizations, allowing for expansion in both patient care and teaching.

Dr. 馬雅各 was also deeply involved in the local church community, often preaching and providing spiritual guidance alongside his medical practice. He understood the interconnectedness of physical and spiritual health, regularly integrating this belief into his work.

Following a brief return to Scotland for health reasons, the couple returned to Taiwan in 1910, recommitting to their mission. They continued to develop medical services and church activities, solidifying their place in Taiwanese history as prominent figures in both healthcare and mission work.

Their tenure in Taiwan was marked by significant contributions to the education of future healthcare professionals, as they offered training and mentorship to local medical students. Dr. 馬雅各’s dedication to improving health outcomes in Taiwan left a lasting impact, and he is remembered as one of the key figures in establishing modern healthcare systems in the region.

The couple continued their service in Taiwan until 1923 when they returned to Canada to be with family. Their legacy in Taiwan remains as a testament to their hard work, dedication, and love for the people they served.Dr. 馬雅各二世 (James Laidlaw Maxwell Jr., 1873-1951) was a renowned physician specializing in leprosy. He arrived in Taiwan on February 24, 1901, at the age of 28, as a medical missionary assigned to the Tainan New Building Medical Hall, established by his father, Dr. 馬雅各, in 1865. On October 9 of the same year, he married nurse Millicent Bertha Saunders (1871-1961) in Hong Kong, and together they dedicated themselves to medical missionary work in Taiwan. They had four children: Millicent Hope (1902-1983), Louisa Grace (1905-1985), Mary Edith (1907-1925), and Hilda Elizabeth (1911-1991).

Dr. 馬雅各 served at the New Building Medical Hall for a total of 22 years, during which he modernized the medical facilities and, with the assistance of Mrs. Saunders, trained many local interns and nurses, becoming a pioneer in nursing education in southern Taiwan.

The couple frequently traveled around the island with assistants and medical supplies, engaging in medical missionary work, particularly focusing on indigenous groups, especially the 阿美族. Dr. 馬雅各 proposed a missionary movement aimed at the 阿美族 to the British headquarters, but due to the outbreak of the European war, the headquarters was unable to expand new projects, and thus the mission did not materialize.

During World War I, Dr. 馬雅各 was called to serve in a battlefield hospital. After the war ended, they returned to Taiwan, where the New Building Medical Hall was in a deteriorated state due to the war. Consequently, the couple immediately set about reorganizing the hospital for continued development. In 1923, Dr. 馬雅各 was invited by the China Medical Missionary Association to serve as its executive officer in Shanghai. In 1937, he became the secretary-general of the Chinese Red Cross Society. He returned to his home country in 1940 due to health issues related to his feet, but in 1944, he again went to a leprosy hospital near Hangzhou, using his specialized medical skills to treat patients. Unfortunately, he passed away from an acute infectious disease in Hangzhou in 1951 at the age of 78.

Throughout their time in Taiwan and later in China, Mrs. Saunders faithfully followed her husband, supporting him in their shared mission. After Dr. 馬雅各’s death, she returned to the UK, where she lived until she passed away in 1961 at the age of 90. (潘稀祺, 2018: 19-22)

金仁理 (Jane Kinney, 1907-1928 in Taiwan) and 高哈拿 (Hannah Connell, 1905-1931 in Taiwan)

After the death of Pastor 馬偕 in 1901 and at the urging of Pastor 吳威廉, female missionaries finally arrived in Taiwan in 1905. To improve the existing girls’ school and provide a more professional education for women, the Canadian Presbyterian Church sent two female missionaries (referred to as “姑娘” by the locals): Miss Jane Kinney, responsible for the Girls’ School, and Miss Hannah Connell, responsible for the Women’s School. In the first two years after their arrival in Taiwan, their focus was on learning the language while gradually understanding and adapting to the customs and culture of northern Taiwan.

Miss Kinney hailed from the eastern Canadian province of New Brunswick, the youngest of eight siblings from a farming family. She grew up attending Sunday school at the Greendale Presbyterian Church, where she later taught Sunday school. She had an impressive educational background, graduating from a normal school and obtaining her teaching license to teach in public schools for four years. Driven by ambition, she entered the University of New Brunswick, starting from the second year until graduation.

During her first two years in Taiwan, Miss Kinney focused on learning Taiwanese. Starting in her second year, she and Miss Connell meticulously planned the future of the girls’ school and worked together to realize its establishment. They traveled to Xiamen to investigate girls’ schools run by the British Presbyterian Church, gaining relevant experience as a model. Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Taipei Medical School YMCA

From the Japanese rule to the early post-war period, the Christian youth movement is indeed a continuous legacy. This period of “prehistory” nurtured a generation that was, on one hand, positively influenced by the international Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA) and on the other, characterized by the self-determination of young people. At that time, schools, churches, and business people successively organized youth groups, reflecting each other and developing in diversity. In 1898, the “Taiwan YMCA,” primarily made up of Japanese individuals, was established in Taipei, launching educational and service activities such as sports workshops, religious film screenings, concerts, dance classes, English Bible study classes, disaster relief for the poor, and publishing the periodical “Taiwan Youth.”

In November 1903, students of Taipei Medical School founded a Christian Youth Association, with participants including Zhou Fuquan, Liao Huanzhang, Wu Keji, and Zhao Dusheng. By 1904, there were more than 20 Christian students (including Japanese) at Taipei Medical School who wished to maintain their faith life on campus and promote fellowship. Zhou Fuquan borrowed a venue from the school to establish the “Taipei Medical School Christian Youth Association,” which had 12 articles of association and officers including Huang Huiwo, Liu Huanshang, Wu Keji, Li Shifang, and Zhao Dusheng, exhibiting a notable degree of autonomy. A larger student YMCA began in the 1910s, led by Zhou Zaifeng, forming the “Taipei Medical College YMCA.” In 1913, the “Gonglihui” from the Governor-General’s National Language School was merged in, gathering every Sunday at Mengjia Church.

During the Japanese rule, only the Japanese-dominated “Taiwan YMCA” officially joined the YMCA Japan Alliance and the World Association. As for the “Student YMCA,” despite claiming to be YMCA, due to potential national consciousness, it never joined the Japanese alliance, thus it belongs to a locally developed model of YMCA.

Creation of the Anglo-Japanese College Movement

In January 1904, church members in Tainan City initiated a movement to establish the Anglo-Japanese College and submitted a petition to the Mission Council. By February 1904, the Mission Council recognized “the hopes expressed by many brethren for the opening of the college” and decided to appoint missionaries Song Zhongjian (Duncan Ferguson) and Fei Renchun (Frederick R. Johnson) to discuss matters such as economic principles, board responsibilities, land acquisition, construction estimates, course offerings, faculty selection, annual costs, student regulations, and the naming of the college with local people. As a result, in April, the issue about establishing the Anglo-Japanese College, after discussions with believers in Tainan city, revealed that no clear opinions could currently be provided on the college’s prospects and many questions such as the blueprint, courses, management, and organization had not reached any agreement. Therefore, the Mission Council, considering the economic tension caused by the Russo-Japanese War and the difficulty in obtaining Western professors to manage the college, decided to temporarily shelve the matter.

The historical significance of this event lies in the fact that the essential “cross-cultural” contribution of Christianity is school education.

The “secondary school” established in 1885 found that local believers at its inception could not afford the costs, and there was a gap between the expectations of the “secondary school” and those of the Mission Council, as the local believers did not wish for it to be merely a preparatory school for the seminary. They aimed to emulate the Anglo-Chinese College in Fuzhou or Meiji Gakuin in Japan to create an “Anglo-Japanese College” focused on “the primary task of advancing education and civilizing society.” However, the Mission Council did not support this, and believers lacked funding. Some of their funds were directed towards Taichung Middle School, drawing attention from the Governor-General’s Office, leading to believers being forced to cease donations. Later, in 1915, after the school moved to outside Dongmen, local believers’ authoritative power increased, and a support association was organized to raise funds. However, under the premise that “worshiping at the shrine” was a recognized condition by the authorities, tensions arose between governing authority, religious education, and local education.

Key Theological Issues

Modern Education during the Japanese Occupation

In the early period of Japanese rule, the Governor’s Office suppressed the influence of Taiwanese and foreigners in politics, economy, and education, but intervened little in religious beliefs and foreign clergy. At the same time, the Governor’s Office viewed Christianity as a representative of Western culture, thus maintaining a favorable attitude towards the church, allowing missionary work to progress smoothly. On the other hand, foreign clergy mostly affirmed the governance of the Governor’s Office, believing that the “atmosphere of progress” greatly benefited the development of the church. As the power transitioned, a new generation of intellectuals grew up in Japanese-ruled Taiwan, not only receiving modern education but also many studying abroad. This so-called “Post-Battle of Yingwei New Generation,” both in background and ideology, differed from previous and subsequent generations. Christians in this period were no exception; they began to engage with Japanese culture, even being born as Japanese nationals. Church leaders believed that Christians had earlier and greater opportunities to receive education than ordinary Taiwanese, and thus Christians should become good citizens, displaying excellent conduct and adhering to disciplined lifestyles. In the early days of Japanese rule, most Taiwanese were deeply skeptical of Japanese education, fearing that their children would be conscripted after attending school, but many Christians quickly accepted education from the Mandarin Language Schools and aspired to study in Japan. At that time, the total number of Christians was less than one percent of the entire Taiwan population, yet, compared to ordinary Taiwanese, they were quite capable of adapting to and accepting the new Japanese education. For example, in the early medical schools, many students were Christians, accounting for a quarter of the total graduates between 1902 and 1906, a significant ratio. Furthermore, due to the proactive acceptance of Western education by Christians, they initiated the trend of Taiwanese studying overseas. This influence led Christians to long engage in a dilemma between “assimilation” and “modernization,” resulting in profound reflections on issues of identity and self-recognition.

Hymns

Taiwan’s first church hymn book, “聖詩歌,” was compiled by British Presbyterian missionary Rev. 甘為霖 in Tainan in 1900. This hymn book, which was lost for several decades, was finally rediscovered in 1988. The book includes 122 hymns. “聖詩歌” itself contains only one hymn with a melody; the other 121 consist only of lyrics written in Tainan dialect Romanization, without Chinese characters. Because these hymn books have lyrics without melodies or scores, the singing usually begins with the leader reciting the lyrics, then starting the melody with the first line, followed by the congregation joining in harmony. In addition, an organ was placed to select melodies, which was often the responsibility of the organist.

Formosa under the Dutch

“Formosa under the Dutch,” translated from Dutch historical documents, is a representative work of Taiwan’s missionary pioneer Rev. 甘為霖 (William Campbell). Since its publication in 1903, it has been widely regarded and remains a classic essential for studying Taiwan’s history during the Dutch colonial period. The English version of the book consists of three parts. The first part is selected from Francois Valentyn’s authoritative work “New and Old East Indies,” which includes descriptions of Taiwan’s geography, trade, and religion, and also features the famous report on Taiwanese indigenous people by the first Dutch pastor stationed in Taiwan, 干治士. The second part is selected from J.A. Grothe’s “Early Dutch Overseas Mission Archives,” primarily consisting of letters and resolutions from administrative personnel and church members, covering indigenous customs, historical grievances between various communities, the relationships between indigenous and Han people, competition over Taiwan’s sovereignty by the Japanese and Spaniards, as well as detailed first-hand accounts of the Dutch colonial government’s promotion of trade, agriculture, and missionary affairs in Taiwan, all of which are extraordinarily valuable. The third part is a translated excerpt from a book written by the last Dutch governor, naming “The Overlooked Taiwan,” which has a separate Chinese edition.

Governance of the Church

In 1893, Rev. Thomas Barclay read a thesis on “The Autonomy of Local Churches” (from “Church History Talks” 413) during a gathering of missionaries. Rev. William Campbell also published a brief article in the church newsletter in colloquial writing, titled “Governance of the Church” (from “Tainan Fort City Church Bulletin,” Volume 99, June of Guangxu 19, page 16).

In this article, Rev. Campbell pointed out that there are three main models for church governance: Episcopacy, Congregationalism, and Presbyterianism. The Presbyterian model often includes the establishment of a session (later referred to as “presbytery,” then changed to “small assembly”), a general assembly (later called “middle assembly”), and a general council (with the third level named “general assembly” and the highest level called “general council”). Under the Presbyterian system, elders govern the church, and the pastor is also considered an elder.Therefore, Rev. 甘為霖 also published the list of current ministers in the southern churches, known as the “Elder Register of Tainan Prefecture” (see “Church History Talks” 414).

The church in the south adopted the Presbyterian system. Thus, Rev. 甘為霖 later introduced the operational guidelines of the Presbyterian system in detail under the title “Governance of the Church.” The articles were published in the “Tainan Fort City Church Bulletin” across seven issues (issues 232-236, 240, and 146, from July 1904 to September 1905) and later compiled into a standalone pamphlet published in 1915.

His main reference was the system of the British Presbyterian Church, translating Session as “長老會,” presbytery as “大會,” and the highest level as “總會” (with the third level being “Synod” and the highest level being “Assembly”). Therefore, Rev. 甘’s writings are divided into four chapters: (1) congregation, (2) elders’ council, (3) presbytery, and (4) general assembly. The chairperson of the elders’ council, presbytery, and general assembly is referred to as “會正” (later changed to “議長”), while the record-keeper is called “紀事” (later changed to “書記”). Since the elders’ council later became known as the “小會” and the presbytery as the “中會,” for the sake of clarity, the terms “小會” and “中會” will be used instead of the original terms.

Under the Presbyterian system, the “小會” is composed of elders and the pastor (the pastor being a teaching elder) and is tasked with admitting members, handling transfers, maintaining a “membership register” (only those not subject to disciplinary action may partake in communion—referred to as “Chheng-khi-mia e;” it is further divided into adults and children), keeping a “record book,” convening meetings (referred to as “Leng hoe”), and striving for the spiritual progress of members. It is also stipulated that the number of members in a 小會 must be at least three. Guidelines for the deacons’ council are included; usually, the local pastor serves as the leader of both the 小會 and the deacons’ council. The 中會 is composed of the pastors in the region and elders elected from various congregations (referred to as delegate elders). Since the authority to handle the appointment, induction, and dismissal of pastors is given to the 中會, the writings detail topics such as “candidate pastors,” “vacancies for pastoral roles,” “protocol for appointing pastors,” “establishing pastors,” “transferring pastors,” and other regulations from the church or members submitted to the 中會.

Rev. 甘為霖’s “Governance of the Church” is an important document for understanding the organizational process of local churches!

Lim Teng-kui Incident

林登貴（Lim Teng-kui），also known as 丁貴, with the courtesy name 毓奇, was from the city of麻豆, Tainan. He enrolled in the predecessor of Tainan Theological College, “Capital College,” in 1894. In 1895, when the Japanese army occupied the southern part of Taiwan, he narrowly escaped death during the Ma Dou Incident, where a mob committed a massacre. On the eve of the Japanese army entering Tainan, he courageously volunteered to accompany missionaries to meet General Naoki Yoshifuru.

In September 1899,林登貴, who was working as a teacher at Tainan Tingzijiao Primary School, was accused of having an inappropriate relationship with a married woman, Mrs. Khim. This matter seemed to be manageable, but due to 林氏’s vigorous protests, he went from being a mere believer to becoming almost a figure of opposition against the church, causing quite a stir. This incident is considered the longest recorded event in the history of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church and led to a period of turmoil within the church.

At that time, the members of the Tainan Clerical Association included physician Peter Anderson, and pastors Duncan Ferguson, William Campbell, and Andrew B. Neilson, totaling four members. Records show that the first two believed there was room for discussion, while the latter two insisted on 林氏’s expulsion. The two sides reached an impasse, and even after asking pastors Campbell Moody from Changhua and physician David Landsborough III to mediate, there was no result. Ultimately, the English Presbyterian Church’s Overseas Missionary Society sent Pastor William McGregor from Xiamen to Taiwan to intervene.

The investigation committee held discussions regarding this incident on February 18, 1900, with Huang Yuede (Elder), Wu Hai (Deacon Wu Daoyuan), Xu Shang (Physician), Guo Mu, Yan Yong-shen, and Zhao Juexiang couple (the preacher, also known in historical materials as Bu-siong Wu Xiang or Zhu Chang), as well as Mrs. Khim herself. On March 1, 1900, 在教士会 members, local elders and deacons, and 林登貴, William McGregor read the Chinese verdict in front of them and recorded the English document in the minutes. The content was as follows:

“In the interactions between the two, there is no evidence of any criminal or evil intentions concealed. Therefore, we conclude that they have not committed such offenses.”

“Because the social interactions of young men and women can provoke suspicion from others, all defamation should be avoided as much as possible. This is an obligation of church members, which is even more important for those preparing to become preachers.”

“We conclude that 林登貴 did not pay full attention to avoiding rumors.”

“We conclude that 林登貴 frequently displayed a poor attitude during the investigation period, stating, ‘I forgot, go ask someone else,’ or disregarding William Campbell’s notification of expulsion, only responding with ‘I am busy,’ and even resorting to blackmail and abusive language, showing no humility toward superiors.”

“We conclude that 林登貴’s abusive behavior is a grave mistake, thus he should not hastily enter the theological school. However, we request the clerical association to still employ him for one year, teaching at an elementary school in another location, observing his words and actions, and then considering whether to allow him to enroll.”

Due to the suspicions regarding Mrs. Khim, 林登貴 was dismissed from the clerical association; however, he successfully re-entered “Capital College” in 1901, indicating that his character had been accepted. According to the first issue of the Alumni Association Magazine of the Presbyterian Theological School of Tainan (1928.1), 林氏 was listed in the “Alumni Directory,” confirming that he was indeed able to enroll successfully, although he did not become an ordained pastor.
