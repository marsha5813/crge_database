Taiwan: Agency (1910-1920)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

甘為霖 (Gān Wéilín)

Pastor Gān Wéilín (William Campbell, 1841-1921) was from Glasgow, Scotland, graduating from the University of Glasgow and the Free Church College. Although he was trained by the Scottish Presbyterian Church, like other Scots such as Mǎ Yǎgè (James L. Maxwell), Lǐ Yōu (Hugh Ritchie), and Bākèlǐ (Thomas Barclay), he joined the Church of Scotland and became a missionary there. Gān Wéilín worked in Taiwan from 1871 to 1917, spending the second-longest time in Taiwan after Barclay and making significant contributions to the Taiwanese church. Notably, the “Xùn Gǔ Táng” (訓瞽堂) he founded in October 1891 was the first educational institution for the blind in Taiwan.

Gān Wéilín arrived in Taiwan in December 1871 as a missionary. Aside from assisting established churches (primarily among the Sīlàyā (西拉雅) and Bázài (巴宰) tribes), he also dedicated himself to evangelizing in uncharted areas. Gān Wéilín may have been the first Westerner to thoroughly document Sun Moon Lake, naming it “Gān Zhìshì Hú” (甘治士湖) in honor of the first Dutch missionary to Taiwan.

Gān Wéilín was quite diligent in taking notes and writing among missionaries, producing numerous works, including “Taiwan Under Dutch Rule” (荷蘭統治下的臺灣), “The Success of Mission in Taiwan” (臺灣宣教之成功), and “Sketches of Formosa” (福爾摩沙素描), establishing himself as a respected scholar. In 1913, his edited work “Xiamen Yin Xin Zidian” (廈門音新字典) was published, known as the “Gān Dictionary,” an important reference book for Pe̍h-ōe-jī (白話字) and Chinese characters that remains widely used to this day. (Wáng Zhāowén 2013: 14-15)

吳威廉 (Wú Wēilián)

Pastor Wú Wēilián (William Gauld, 1861-1923) was born on February 25, 1861, in Westminster, London, Ontario, Canada. In 1880, after hearing Mǎjiē (George L. Mackay) speak about his missionary work in Taiwan during his return to report, he was deeply moved and resolved to go overseas for mission work. After graduating from Knox College, he applied to the Canadian Overseas Mission Board and was assigned to Taiwan in 1892, arriving in Tamsui on the night of October 22. Wú Wēilián was friendly and humble, diligent in his work with remarkable perseverance; his contributions to the missionary work in Taiwan were comparable to those of Mǎjiē. In his second year in Taiwan, he faced Mǎjiē’s return to report again, thereby taking on the responsibility of overseeing 60 churches and over 2,000 congregants, the Oxford Academy, a girls’ school, and the Mackay Memorial Hospital. During this time, Taiwan experienced turmoil due to the First Sino-Japanese War and the Boxer Rebellion, which only highlighted Pastor Wú’s leadership abilities. In May 1899, while on leave back home, he was recalled due to Mǎjiē’s illness; shortly thereafter, with Mǎjiē’s passing, Wú Wēilián took over the mission affairs in northern Taiwan. Unlike Mǎjiē’s autocratic leadership style, Wú Wēilián excelled in organization, linking various local congregations. In 1904, he established the North Presbytery and in 1905 formed the Mission Council, later communicating with the Southern Church of Scotland to create the “Taiwan Synod” (Synod of Formosa), laying the foundation for a systematic approach to mission work. In addition to his substantial contributions to church management, Wú Wēilián was also recognized for his architectural expertise. After arriving in Taiwan in 1892, he primarily took on the responsibility for church building designs. Documentation reveals that he designed and oversaw significant structures, including the Mǎjiē Tomb (1901), the Girls’ Dormitory (1906), the Pastors’ Residence (1909), as well as many churches in Beitou and Dadaocheng, Taipei Theological School (1918), and Mackay Memorial Hospital. Unlike the churches from Mǎjiē’s era, Wú Wēilián’s architectural designs exhibited refined construction techniques that generally reflected British colonial styles in Asia, bringing a new appearance to church architecture. (Adapted from Wáng Liúhóng’s article on the Open Museum, https://reurl.cc/eGnOg7, accessed 2025/01/12)

偕叡廉 (Xiè Ruìlián)

Xiè Ruìlián (George William MacKay, 1882-1963) was born into a missionary family, making his identity particularly noteworthy as he was of mixed heritage. His father, Xiè Ruìlǐ (Mǎjiē, George L. Mackay), was a missionary of the Canadian Presbyterian Church, while his mother, Zhāng Cōngmíng, was a local from Wugu in northern Taiwan.偕叡廉 (Xiè Ruìlián)

During Mǎjiē’s (George L. Mackay) return to report, Xiè Ruìlián (George William MacKay) was studying in Canada and, at a young age, was also sent by his father to attend the Victoria English School in Hong Kong. After Pastor Mǎjiē passed away, Xiè Ruìlián studied at St. Andrew’s College in Toronto, and later at the University of Toronto. He eventually earned a Master’s degree in Educational Psychology from Clark University in Massachusetts, following his father’s aspirations before applying to the Canadian Overseas Mission Board for a missionary posting. Together with his wife Xiè Rénlì (Jean Ross, 1887-1969), he went to northern Taiwan as a missionary.

Upon his return to Taiwan, Xiè Ruìlián was determined to establish a secondary school aimed at enhancing the educational opportunities for the church’s children and the youth of Taiwan. He fulfilled this ambition when Dànshuǐ Zhōngxuéxiào (淡水中學校) was officially established on April 4, 1914, making it Taiwan’s earliest five-year secondary school for local children. It became the sole pathway for higher education for young people in northern Taiwan during the Japanese occupation era and also served as a preparatory school for Taipei Theological School (now Taiwan Theology College). His abundant executive ability and vitality were believed to be influenced by his father; however, he also inherited Mǎjiē’s controversial traits, including a dictatorial and uncompromising nature.

吳希榮 (Wú Xīróng)

Pastor Wú Xīróng was born on June 2, 1881, and passed away on December 15, 1922, at the age of 41. The following day, a funeral was held at the Pingtung Chapel, which was packed with attendees. The eulogies, condolences, and narratives of the deceased’s life moved the congregation deeply. Additionally, the article “A Brief Biography of Pastor Wú Xīróng” published in Taiwan Church News, 455 (February 1923): 11-12, mentioned many anecdotes: Wú Xīróng was gifted and eager to learn from an early age, and he was passionate about missionary work. Following the example of his father Wú Gě (Wú Gē, an evangelist), he preached compellingly with a powerful voice that commanded attention. When told to speak more softly, he replied, “If I don’t preach loudly, I won’t wish to preach at all.” Consequently, he caused damage to his throat without concern. He taught people without discrimination, whether they were relatives, colleagues, friends, or elders, fearless and straightforward, like a fearless John the Baptist. At one point, someone informed him, “You have tuberculosis and will leave this world soon.” Instead of shrinking back, he became determined to go to Penghu for mission work. He exerted himself so much that he not only did not damage his health but instead became stronger, almost like the warrior David from Bethlehem. He often cared for God’s flock, intervening immediately when there were disputes among congregants, and sought to mend conflicts to bring about reconciliation. Jesus said, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the children of God.”

Seeing the financial difficulties facing the church and a lack of motivation among the congregants, he temporarily resigned from his service in Jiayi and returned to Pingtung to manage a pomelo orchard, hoping to make a profit to support the independence of the church. He truly worked hard, selling scallions and vegetables and managing an orchard, gradually facing signs of success. He can be regarded as a pioneer advocating for church autonomy.

During his time in Penghu, he partnered with Huáng Shēnhé to establish a society for lonely and widowed preachers; he also donated money to secretly assist a certain individual. The pastor’s wife, Lín Lùdé, continued the pastor’s will, preparing to take in that family, willing to assume their debts. The pastor’s wife was also remarkable! (Lài Yǒngxiáng 1995: 199)

In the record of the 50th meeting of the South Presbytery on March 20, 1923, a brief biography of Pastor Wú Xīróng was transcribed, stating: “Pastor Wú Xīróng was born on June 2, 1881, and passed away on December 15, 1922, at the age of forty-one. From a young age, he was intelligent and eager to learn, and as he grew, he was dedicated to preaching, fervently passionate and loud, evoking both admiration and awe. His spirit resembled that of his late father. When instructing the congregation, he spoke candidly and without reservation, regardless of whether they were family or close friends, treating colleagues and superiors equally. He exhibited a loyalty comparable to that of John the Baptist. He was concerned for his people, often mediating disputes, tenderly persuading both parties to reconcile to restore peace, earning a reputation as a paragon of resolution. Recognizing the financial difficulties of the southern churches and the hesitant spirit among the congregants, he resigned from his position, then tirelessly cultivated fruit, hoping to profit to ensure church autonomy. Despite great effort, he managed to plant a thousand pomelo trees, gradually seeing results. He was indeed a pioneer of autonomy in the church. The pastor continually practiced self-discipline, enduring hardship, assisting the poor, and had once helped a missionary by caring for their prolonged illness, which led to their recovery and return to service. If any preachers faced financial shortages, he would save to assist them. He once witnessed a deacon displaying loyalty, wearing a bamboo hat and straw sandals, helping carry a coffin to the grave; such deeds should be emulated by those of faith.”“Mr. (name) is survived by three sons and five daughters, all gifted and intelligent, achieving excellent academic results. It is especially hoped that they will carry on the legacy and aspirations of their ancestors, ensuring that the work of the pastor does not go in vain. We now take this opportunity to remember Mr. (name)’s great virtues and respectfully compose a few words in his honor.”

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Synod of Formosa

Although the southern and northern regions of Taiwan are divided into British and Canadian missionary districts, their beliefs and systems are identical, and the churches in the UK and Canada have deep-rooted connections and amicable relationships. As early as 1903, there were proposals for unification between the southern and northern churches; however, due to the significant differences in their respective strengths at that time, these proposals did not materialize. The southern church had established an autonomous organization called the “Tainan Presbytery” (臺南長老大會, Táinán zhǎnglǎo dàhuì) in 1896, while the northern church did not form the “Taipei Presbytery” (臺北長老中會, Táiběi zhǎnglǎo zhōnghuì) until 1904. In 1907, consensus was reached again to revisit the topic of cooperation between north and south, confirming that unification was necessary and that all local churches must fully understand this before moving forward.

Soon after, communication between the two sides became closer. In 1909, both parties agreed to delineate the church districts, with the western boundary marked by the Dajia River (大甲溪) and the eastern region north of Hualien Port (花蓮港) belonging to the northern church, while south of Guanyin Mountain (觀音山) belonged to the southern church. In 1911, representatives from both sides drafted the “Charter of the Southern and Northern Churches,” which was accepted the following year (1912), leading to the first meeting of the “Taiwan Presbyterian Church Assembly” (臺灣長老教會大會, Táiwān zhǎnglǎo jiàohuì dàhuì), officially held at the chapel on Ximen Street in Changhua. During this meeting, missionary Gan Weilin (甘為霖, Gān Wéilín) was elected as chairman, establishing a joint decision-making mechanism for both southern and northern churches.

Through discussions in the Taiwan Assembly, progress was made in the church system, gradually moving toward organized development. Organizationally, in 1919, a universally applicable liturgy was published for the entire island; in 1920, a “Teaching Examination Department” was established to coordinate the promotion of missionaries across Taiwan; in 1926, church music experts were commissioned to create a shared hymnal, compiling musical scores and localized works, giving rise to Taiwan’s “folk hymns” (民謠聖詩, mín yáo shèng sī). In terms of organizational structure, whether the southern or northern churches established “presbyteries” or elevated to “assemblies,” they sought the permission of the Taiwan Assembly. In religious education, a general secretary for Sunday School was appointed to enrich teaching content, renovate classrooms, strengthen statistical reporting, introduce Japanese educational materials, hold teachers’ spiritual retreats and home visits, and arrange travel lectures and adult Sunday Schools in various locations. Regarding social services, the Taiwan Assembly assisted the Bible Society, leprosy treatment associations, and promoted abstinence from smoking and alcohol, demonstrating a common consensus and broad outreach within the unification movement, and illustrating the influence of the Taiwan Assembly.

However, it must be acknowledged that due to human factors, there were times of disagreement between the north and south, causing resolutions to occasionally be unimplemented. Additionally, significant missionary institutions such as secondary schools, seminaries, youth associations, and medical institutions were not jointly operated, resulting in scattered efforts. Although the regulations aimed “for the unification of the Taiwan Assembly, with innovation as the fundamental spirit,” discussions often lacked continuity. At one point, the southern region even considered that the Taiwan Assembly was costly but of little use, suggesting that it could be abolished or reduced to a few people for “socializing.” Consequently, after 1935, the Taiwan Assembly transitioned to meeting every two years, and the format gradually turned into a “fellowship meeting.” In reality, numerous factors contributed to the ineffectiveness of the Taiwan Assembly, such as a ten-year development gap between the north and south, uncertain intentions of the parent churches, lack of direct contact among missionaries, absence of consensus in missionary work, and a tendency in both regions to isolate themselves, making it difficult to implement decisions.

The decision-making and execution authority of the Taiwan Assembly were insufficient; the actual power remained divided between the north and south. While the Taiwan Assembly reflected church unity, it lacked complete administrative and budgetary authority. Once the meeting ended, other times felt like “hollow shells,” evidently lacking in real power despite strong fellowship, keeping true unity still a distant goal. For example, in 1915, both churches responded to the universal unity movement, deciding to establish a joint seminary in Taipei; however, due to disagreements over admission qualifications, courses, and the “missionary examination” post-graduation, the two schools could not merge. Later, in 1925, the northern staff and students faced turmoil due to issues within the Canadian parent church, leading to funding shortages and personnel changes. Although there was a brief move south to study, later on, northern Taiwanese individuals opposed setting up a joint seminary in Taichung, and in 1927 students were recalled. The failure of the two schools to merge was not merely an issue of location; both churches bore responsibility regarding meeting procedures, regulatory compliance, and historical interpretation, exposing the weaknesses of the Taiwan Assembly’s ineffectiveness. (Lu Qiming (盧啓明) 2020: 89-127)

Taiwan Sunday School Association (全臺主日部會, Quántái zhǔrì bùhuì) (1917-1926)

Since the British Presbyterian Church (英國長老教會, Yīngguó zhǎnglǎo jiàohuì) began its missionary work in Taiwan in 1865, educational efforts have always been an important means of cultivating believers and expanding the church.Among them, the Children’s Sunday School (兒童主日學, Értóng zhǔrì xué), held every Sunday to teach children about the Gospel, is the foundation of Christian education and crucial for the future growth of the church. From the late Qing Dynasty to the Japanese colonial period, the British Presbyterian Church gradually developed an organized and systematic Sunday School education.

In April 1917, during the Taiwan Presbyterian Assembly, Pastor Guo Shuilong (郭水龍, Guō Shuǐlóng) proposed the establishment of a “Sunday School Department” to manage Sunday School affairs on a long-term basis. This proposal was approved by the assembly representatives, leading to the establishment of the department and the election of six pastors: Liu Zhongjian (劉忠堅, Liú Zhōngjiān), Xie Ruilian (偕叡廉, Xié Ruìlián), Ye Jinmu (葉金木, Yè Jīnmù), Song Zhongjian (宋忠堅, Sòng Zhōngjiān), Wu Xirong (吳希榮, Wú Xīróng), and Gao Jinsheng (高金聲, Gāo Jīnshēng) as committee members. On July 17 of the same year, members of the Sunday School Department gathered at the chapel in Changhua for discussions, with assistance from Pastor Huang Siling (黃俟命, Huáng Sǐmíng) and Pastor Song Zhongjian’s wife (宋忠堅, Sòng Zhōngjiān) (Elizabeth Christie Ferguson). This meeting decided to adopt the “Universal Sunday School Textbook” as the textbook for all Taiwan churches’ Sunday Schools and also publish a “Baihwa Character Sunday School Teacher’s Reference Book” for teachers to prepare lessons, to be issued quarterly. Furthermore, 11 teachers from the south and 6 from the north were appointed to guide Sunday Schools in various churches, aiming to establish sound Sunday Schools in every church. The main responsibilities of the Sunday School Department thereafter included:

  1. Surveying and compiling statistics on the number of teachers and students in Sunday Schools across Taiwan: Starting in November 1917, questionnaires were sent out for investigation. The assembly produced its first overall report on Sunday Schools in Taiwan the following year, and from then until 1926, annual statistics and reports became routine, except for the years 1923 and 1924.

  2. Editing and printing Sunday School textbooks and teacher reference books.

  3. Organizing “Teachers’ Workshops” to enhance teaching quality: From July 30 to August 14, 1918, the first workshop was held at a secondary school in Tainan, attended by 36 participants. Course content included New Testament studies, teaching methods and practical exercises, Sunday School organization management, child psychology, music theory, prenatal education and child health, extracurricular lectures, and studies on 1 and 2 Timothy.

  4. As of January 1919, special columns for “Correspondence Teaching” and “Voices of Sunday School” were opened in the “Taiwan Church News” to publish updates on local Sunday Schools and demonstrate teaching methods.

  5. Sending specialists to assist various local churches in establishing Sunday Schools.

On April 6, 1926, during an assembly meeting, it was decided that each of the southern and northern presbyteries would establish their own “Sunday School Departments.” However, the assembly still appointed pastors Gao Jinsheng, Ye Jinmu, Chen Xizhun (陳溪圳, Chén Xīzhùn), and Pan Daorong (潘道榮, Pān Dàoróng) to oversee the affairs of the Sunday School Department for the entire assembly until 1928, ensuring that the southern and northern Sunday School Departments became gradually familiar with their management until they could manage their respective Sunday School education independently.

In summary, the “Sunday School Department” established from 1917 to 1926 was responsible for improving educational affairs in the church, transitioning Sunday School from its founding period into an era of organization and expansion after 1926. (Zhang Miaojun (張妙娟) 2010: 79-103)

New Tower Hospital (新樓醫院, xīn lóu yīyuàn)

In 1865, the first medical missionary to Taiwan, James L. Maxwell (馬雅各, Mǎ Yǎgè), established the Kanhsi Street Medical Clinic, which later evolved into the Qihou Medical Clinic and then the Tainan Old Building (二老口) Medical Clinic. The “New Tower Hospital” was established in 1900, managed mostly by foreign medical personnel until 1935, and trained Taiwanese assistants and nurses as needed. The hospital charged low fees, but due to rising salaries and competition from private institutions, it still relied on subsidies from the British parent church and government grants. The hospital emphasized ventilation, lighting, and ward classification in its facilities, providing effective treatments for diseases such as tuberculosis and leprosy. As a result, hospitalized patients often converted to Christianity more easily than outpatient visitors, due to the careful attention they received. For a long time, the hospital and the church had to share limited resources, and due to the impact of religious policies, medical missionary activities gradually diminished after 1935.

In 1900, the new hospital, once opened, was named “New Tower Hospital,” featuring Taiwan’s first manual elevator to facilitate patients moving to the operating room located on the second floor. Various advanced medical equipment was introduced, and isolation wards were established. In 1901, Maxwell’s second son, Dr. James L. Maxwell, Jr. (馬雅各二世, Mǎ Yǎgè èrshì), along with his nurse-background wife, came to Tainan to continue his father’s medical missionary mission.He actively cultivated local medical personnel, utilizing steam sterilizers and X-ray machines, and promoted the cessation of opium and the prevention of sexually transmitted diseases, as well as caring for and treating leprosy patients. Between 1908 and 1909, Maxwell Jr. returned to the UK, with Dr. David Landsborough (蘭大衛, Lán Dàwèi) acting as the hospital director.

From 1914 to 1918, Dr. Dai Renshou (戴仁壽, Dài Rénshòu) served as the hospital director, focusing on nursing and hygiene. In 1917, he published “Internal and External Nursing” (內外科看護學, Nèi wàikē kànhùxué), written in colloquial characters, which became an important medical textbook. In 1919, Maxwell Jr. resumed the role of director and established the Rehabilitation Institute (鴉片戒毒所, Yāpiàn jièdú suǒ) and a leprosy treatment facility in 1922, marking a new chapter for the treatment of special diseases.

In summary, New Tower Hospital during the 1910s was in a developmental phase, showing significant progress compared to the unstable Mackay Hospital and the newly founded Changhua Hospital. Although finances were often in deficit due to low medical fees, the hospital started to boldly experiment with a self-sufficiency policy without relying on the parent church, albeit with mixed success. The personnel organization was also quite stable, as Maxwell Jr. spent a noticeably longer time than his father in Taiwan—6.5 years compared to his lifetime total of 23 years. Medical practices strengthened nursing in wards, categorizing male and female patients and establishing a tuberculosis ward. Overall, during its developmental period, New Tower Hospital achieved considerable success in medical education, healthcare services, and missionary work. (Tang Huiténg (湯惠婷) 2008: 181-200)

Mackay Hospital (馬偕醫院, Mǎjiē yīyuàn)

In 1872, Pastor George L. Mackay (馬偕, Mǎjiē) began his medical work after landing in Huwei (淡水, Dànshuǐ). In 1880, Mackay established the first Western-style hospital in northern Taiwan, “Mackay Hospital” (偕醫館, Xié yīguǎn), in Tamsui, meant to honor Captain Mackay, who shared the same surname. The hospital played a significant role during the Qing-French War in 1884, as medical staff rescued injured soldiers when a French warship bombarded Tamsui, marking the first Red Cross-like rescue operation in Taiwan. Mackay passed away in his residence in Tamsui in 1901, leading to a temporary closure of the hospital. In 1905, the Canadian parent church sent J. Y. Ferguson (宋雅各, Sòng Yǎgè), who held both physician and pastor roles, along with his wife, to Tamsui, reopening the hospital the following year. In 1911, Ferguson proposed relocating the medical center from Tamsui to Taipei and expanding the hospital in memory of Mackay, rebranding it as Mackay Memorial Hospital (馬偕紀念醫院, Mǎjiē jìniàn yīyuàn), which was completed at the end of 1912. In 1912, the parent church dispatched Miss Isabel Elliot (烈以利姑娘, Liè Yǐlì gūniáng) to Taiwan to establish a “Nursing School,” training early nurses in northern Taiwan. However, by 1918, due to the impact of the European War, the program was suspended for six years and only reopened on January 1, 1925. Throughout the 1910s, Mackay Hospital experienced significant upheaval, with the years 1911 to 1918 marked by a transformative development period, interrupted by the effects of the European War. (Xu Zhuāngzhuāng (許妝莊) 2010: 1-60)

Changhua Medical Clinic (彰化醫館, Zhānghuà yīguǎn)

Dr. David Landsborough (蘭大衛, Lán Dàwèi III, 1870-1957) arrived in Taiwan in 1895 during Japanese rule and founded the Changhua Medical Clinic in 1896, commencing his extensive 40-year engagement in medical care, preaching, education, and relief work.

In healthcare, aside from outpatient services at the clinic, he also offered touring medical services to Dasha from April to August and made home visits when requested by the public. The clinic’s outpatient services were divided into internal medicine, general surgery, and ophthalmology; Landsborough became well-known throughout the island for his surgical skills, particularly in eye surgeries, which often surpassed surgeries on other body parts. The touring medical service to Dasha culminated in 1906, with no further visits afterward; for home visits, he adhered to local customs, showing respect and discretion towards women. Fees were initially free but began charging approximately 10 cents per outpatient in 1904; after 1912, regardless of the size of the surgery, all procedures were charged at one yen (equivalent to two shillings). Additionally, he participated in epidemic prevention and smoking cessation initiatives, leading the way in treating leprosy at church hospitals. Statistics indicate he treated around 100,000 patients annually, averaging 300 patients daily, accounting for about 10% of the population in Taichung Prefecture and 70-80% in Changhua, making it the largest private hospital in central Taiwan during the Japanese colonial period.

In terms of preaching, Landsborough practiced a form of medical evangelism, spending more time on house calls before the hospital was established. After his marriage, his wife, Marjorie Landsborough (連瑪玉, Lián Mǎyù) strengthened the connection between the hospital and the church. By 1909, the number of churches in the central region grew from four in 1896 to 23, laying the foundation for Landsborough’s medical preaching endeavors.Education: Through examinations, intelligent youth were selected and taught medical skills. The instruction utilized Taiwanese Romanization (白話字, báihùa zì) and employed monkeys or cadavers as practical materials for anatomy studies. The teaching methods were quite similar to those of today; however, while the basics of medical science were relatively weak, clinical medicine had already become quite specific. Upon completing four or five years of training, the hospital issued graduation certificates; students would then pass the Japanese government’s qualification exams to obtain their official medical licenses. However, after 1930, the Japanese government strictly enforced the “Limited Local Physicians” policy, leading these students to live a life of displacement.

Relief: Free treatment was provided to the poor and sick, along with assistance in education and employment. Statistics indicated that the expenses for free patients constituted 30% to 50% of the costs incurred by paying patients, which was a notably high ratio. Consequently, the Japanese government recognized it as a charitable hospital, awarding it grants twelve times. The Empress of Japan bestowed “Imperial Songs” and “Imperial Plaques” during Crown Prince Hirohito’s visit to Taiwan and summoned Landsborough, presenting him with a medal—these instances all serve as evidence of his engagement in charitable work. Notably, Landsborough’s decision to graft skin from his wife’s thigh to treat Zhou Jinyao was particularly lauded as an example of “selfless love.”

In summary, during his 40 years of medical missionary work in Taiwan, Landsborough’s greatest achievement and impact came from the establishment of the Changhua Medical Clinic. In addition to treating patients and educating students in medical techniques, he was actively involved in epidemic prevention, smoking cessation, and leprosy treatment. He introduced modern medicine to central Taiwan, significantly improving public health conditions. (Li Xinfeng (李欣芬) 1989: 212-215)

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The Issue of Union between the Southern and Northern Theological Schools

The so-called “theological school union issue” was formally discussed at the Second Taiwan Conference in 1913. Pastor Gao Jinsheng (高金聲) from the south proposed that the two missionary societies negotiate whether the Southern and Northern theological schools could unite. A six-member group was immediately appointed to discuss this with the two societies. The following year (1914), Gao reported at the conference that he had already contacted the two societies and had discussions. Wu Weilian (吳威廉) argued that both schools should maintain their current status, and that the plan for Southern and Northern students to each study for two years was currently very difficult to implement. The conference subsequently resolved: 1. to ask the two missionary societies to continue to develop a plan for “temporarily studying for two years in the North and two years in the South.” 2. to establish a “theological school department” to discuss enrollment rules, admission qualifications, curricula, and other related matters. 3. that Taiwan should ideally have only one theological school. 4. to send representatives to negotiate with the societies on how to merge the two schools. Clearly, the benefits of cooperative theological education include saving costs, promoting collaboration, and even raising standards, but the premise is that consensus on the educational system and location must be reached.

In 1915, the conference resolved to select two places from both the North and the South, resulting in Taipei receiving 25 votes, Tainan 6 votes, Taichung 4 votes, and Tamsui 0 votes, confirming the location and promptly delegating the two societies to establish the theological school. In the summer of 1917, the cornerstone for the union theological school was laid in Niupuzhuang, Taipei, and it was completed the following spring. This newly built grand structure was designed by Wu Weilian according to the conference’s decisions, which indeed exceeded the needs of the North alone.

However, due to the significant disparity in student qualifications, the implementation of the union theological school became uncertain. In 1916, the Taiwan conference decided to discuss admission qualifications. Northern School Principal Yao Meidan (約美但) proposed that applicants should have at least graduated from high school and complete four years of study; required subjects included Old and New Testament Studies, Church History, Systematic Theology, Homiletics, Pastoral Theology, Ethics, Philosophy, Religious Pedagogy, Classical Chinese, English, and Japanese. Yao Meidan was a biblical scholar and educator, and during his management of school affairs from 1907 to 1916, he gradually introduced some advanced specialized knowledge into the curriculum and established the academic year system at Taipei Theological School in 1909, stipulating that all entrants must have completed primary school before studying six years in the theological school to graduate. Therefore, his proposal was merely an attempt to raise the academic level of the theological school.

At that time, however, the Southern school had no strict admission requirements; between 1903 and 1913, subjects aside from the Bible and the Four Books and Five Classics included some mathematics and science courses. It was not until 1914, after Koyano Masaki (河野政喜) joined the faculty team, that deeper subjects such as philosophy and systematic theology began to be taught. Even so, the level of incoming students remained uneven. For example, among the 13 entrants in 1926, only two had graduated from high school; three had equivalent education, while the others were primary school graduates, and some didn’t even know how to read Taiwan Romanization.

Additionally, Buckley considered that students needed practical training and that it was best not to leave the South, so he believed: “Due to several difficulties, we cannot determine the period for establishing the union school”; Yao Meidan proposed that “for various reasons, it would be better to delay further, and both sides still hope to wait for another two or three years before implementation.” The conference ultimately decided in favor of Yao Meidan’s opinion; however, he soon went to Korea, and leadership of the Northern school was handed over to Wu Weilian. Therefore, before the 1917 Taiwan Conference was held, no discussions had taken place regarding the system of the union theological school.

During the agenda of the 1918 Taiwan Conference, much discussion took place regarding the educational regulations. In the end, Wu Weilian presented a draft, and the conference decided to “temporarily use it for one year, then re-evaluate, but it must be recorded in the minutes,” stipulating that a minimum high school education was required, and four years of study were necessary to graduate. The curriculum included the national language (Japanese), classical Chinese, English, poetry metrics, biblical phonetics, church history, overviews of the Old/New Testaments, the life and epistles of Paul, homiletics, biblical exegesis, systematic theology, homiletics, pastoral theology, philosophy, psychology, and pedagogy.

From the above, it can be seen that the educational system had a considerable scale, covering the four major components of theological education: Scripture, History, Systematic Theology, and Practical Theology, nearly matching the required courses of modern theological seminaries. The following year, the Northern school took the lead in revising the curriculum, admitting high school graduates for four years of study, with levels based on graduation year. The Southern school did not make much progress.

From 1921 to 1926, there was no discussion regarding the school location; the Taiwan Conference turned to discuss the “quality” of the theological school. Internal factors were certainly considered to discuss merging. For example, in 1921, the theological school department discussed how to define the status of non-high school graduates who wanted to enter the theological school. The conference later decided that each of the Southern and Northern Missionary Boards should work it out on their own; if the original admission qualifications were to be changed, it would be reviewed by the theological school department for future meetings.

On the other hand, in 1922, the Governor-General’s Office issued a new “Taiwan Education Ordinance” to align with the extended policy for the mainland.In June, the government issued Ordinance No. 138, promulgating the “Regulations for Private Schools,” which consisted of six chapters and incorporated private schools into the overall educational system, applying public regulations accordingly. At the Taiwan Conference in 1922, discussions began to intensively focus on the status of students entering the theological school, deciding to categorize them into two types: students who graduated from high school would be termed as regular students, while those who had three years of incomplete high school education would be categorized as special students. The Southern Missionary Society further believed that the best way to resolve the current issues in theological education was to establish the union theological school in Taipei; however, until that was realized, the Tainan Theological School should still be strengthened, clearly indicating a very positive attitude. As for Southern high school students wishing to attend the Northern Theological School, they would need to apply for approval.

In summary, the discussions regarding the “Union Theological School” began at the Taiwan Conference in 1913. The proposal to study “two years in the North and two years in the South” remained in the negotiation phase without any resolution in 1914. By 1915, it was decided to locate the school in Taipei, and by 1917, the new campus was completed. However, from the conference discussions, it is evident that between 1915 and 1924, both sides lacked consensus on admission qualifications, course content, church internships, and the “pastoral examination” after graduation, resulting in the Southern and Northern schools continuing their separate practices with many issues outstanding. During this time, the Northern school gradually raised its admission standards and course level, while the Southern school made slow progress. When the Governor-General’s Office enacted the “Regulations for Private Schools” in 1922, although both the South and North received “establishment approval,” there was a significant gap in their essence. Thus, the inability to unify the educational system, compared to the selection of school location, was the primary reason for the failure to merge.

The Three Self-Movement

The so-called Three Self-Movement refers to self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation and is part of the indigenization of Christianity. In China, its aim was to encourage believers to take responsibility and eliminate prejudice against “foreign religion.” In 1877, at a nationwide missionary meeting in China, John Butler from the Ningbo Presbyterian Church was the first to propose that the work of local churches should be led by local people, as this was the best guarantee for Christianity to take root and could reduce criticism of the foreign religion by the populace. (Wu Xueming 1997: 83)

Notably, when the Chinese church began to advocate for the Three Self-Movement, it coincided with the time when Christianity was being introduced to Taiwan. In the south, it started with physician James L. Maxwell in 1865, while in the north, George Mackay led the way in 1872. After half a century of initial development, in the 1910s during the mid-Japanese colonial period, local voices began to rise, advocating for self-reliance.

By 1915, the Southern Church had been operating for 50 years. Although there had been discussions about church self-governance and self-support, no one had made significant efforts to promote it. After Wu Xirong (吳希榮) was hired as a pastor of the A-Qiu (屏東) Church in May 1915, he began to advocate vigorously for the church to be self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating; although his words might have been considered extreme at the time, they were also quite visionary. He stated that after 50 years, the Taiwanese church should not continue to rely on subsidies from the British mother church; relying on assistance from the mother church would be akin to a 50-year-old man still being nursed. Wu Xirong’s advocacy gained support from fellow pastors, including Lin Yancheng (林燕臣) from Donggang, Gao Duxing (高篤行) from Wandan, Cheng Xipan (鄭溪泮) from Ligang, and Liao Dezhi (廖得之) who was known for evangelistic work. They held meetings and conducted promotions to encourage church self-governance. (Yang Shiyang 1965: 123) This influence led to the establishment of the “Kaohsiung Bureau of Church Affairs” by the Southern Presbytery (at that time, Pingtung was under Kaohsiung’s jurisdiction), which operated during that period and inspired regions such as Tainan, Chiayi, and Taichung to delineate presbyteries in 1930.

The Northern Church also saw advancements. In February 1912, the 14th Northern Presbytery passed the regulations and officially established the “Missionary Bureau.” In September, it approved the “Constitution of the Taipei Presbyterian Church.” By June 1916, the Governor-General’s Office recognized the establishment of the “Northern Taiwan Christian Presbyterian Church Foundation,” further solidifying its organization. (Xu Qianxin 1955: 20-24)

Recognition for William Campbell

Missionary William Campbell frequently communicated with the Governor-General’s Office, not only negotiating support for believers in distress but also promoting official attention to blind education. In 1896, the blind school at Tainan’s Hong Gong Shrine was closed upon the expiration of its lease. While on vacation in Tokyo, Campbell visited former Governor Koshigaya Masaki to express the need for educational support for the blind in Taiwan. Later, Governor Kodama Gentarō accepted Campbell’s suggestion and set up a government-run school for the blind and deaf in Tainan, appointing Christian teacher Qiu Shan Yanzang (秋山衍藏) from Tainan Middle School as the first principal, allowing students to freely attend worship or listen to sermons during their spare time. In 1897, Campbell negotiated with Japanese officials to select three graduates from the Tainan Blind Training School, raising funds through a charity concert to cover their tuition for studying in Japan.On June 17, 1915 (Government Day), the Governor-General’s Office awarded William Campbell the “Fifth Class Order of the Rising Sun” for his works that helped promote the achievements of Taiwan’s governance to foreign audiences and correct misunderstandings among foreigners. In late April 1916, Campbell was invited by the Minister of Civil Administration to speak at the Mandarin School Auditorium in Taipei. He delivered speeches over four consecutive nights to more than 200 officials and gentlemen on topics including (1) Taiwan’s historical data, (2) the Dutch occupation of Taiwan, (3) China’s governance of Taiwan, and (4) Japan’s initial colonization of Taiwan, with translations provided by government officials. When Campbell retired in 1917, Governor Andō Kenzō sent a letter informing him that he would be awarded the “Fourth Class Order of the Sacred Treasures,” which was presented to him by the Japanese consul in Hong Kong. The governor even made a special trip to Tainan to express gratitude and recognize Campbell’s contributions in Taiwan, further demonstrating Campbell’s significance to the colonial authorities.

Key Theological Issues

A Dictionary of Amoy Vernacular (Xiamen Yin Xin Zidian)

The “Xiamen Yin Xin Zidian” (厦门音新字典) by Scottish missionary Gan Wei-lin (William Campbell) was published in 1913 and became quite popular, abbreviated as the “Gan Zidian” (甘字典). Its English title is A Dictionary of Amoy Vernacular spoken throughout the prefectures of Chin-chiu, Chiang-chiu and Formosa. The first edition was printed by the Yokohama Evangelical Printing Company and 1,000 copies were distributed by the Taiwan Church News Agency.

This dictionary contains approximately 15,000 entries, selected from the “Kangxi Dictionary” (康熙字典) and “Fifteen Sounds” (十五音), and also references Western compilers of Chinese character dictionaries, such as Morrison, Walter H. Medhurst, Williams, Giles, and Talmage, who collaborated on A Dictionary of Amoy Vernacular; George Mackay (馬偕) with his Chinese-English Dictionary, Father Couvreur and MacGowan with their English-Chinese Phrasebooks, and others.

The phonetics are drawn from the local dialects of Zhangzhou and Quanzhou, with a focus on the phonetic traits near Tainan due to assistants Lin Jin-sheng (林錦生) and Chen Da-luo (陳大鑼). Heavy reference is made to the Zhangzhou phonetics “Fifteen Sounds,” where the listed characters and their usages are adopted. They are arranged according to sounds, with single-syllable characters categorized accordingly. Similar to the vernacular Bible, “ch” and “ts” are used to distinguish the two accents. If a character has no original form, it is noted with a phonetic reading, using an additional mark to indicate the character is unavailable. Each character generally occupies one line with brief notes and usage. If a character has multiple pronunciations, it is found in several places; for example, “song” can appear as “ko” or “koa,” and “eye” as “ba̍k” or “bo̍k.”

Du Jiade (杜嘉德) edited the Comprehensive Xiamen-English Dictionary, which includes English annotations without Chinese character correspondence, while Bakeli (巴克禮) supplemented Du’s dictionary with Chinese characters but maintained English annotations, showing that the primary audience was Westerners. In contrast, the Gan Zidian’s annotations were all in Vernacular, allowing locals to learn characters using Xiamen sounds, which is why it became popular and loved by the residents of the island.

The second edition of the Gan Zidian was published in 1924. Due to Gan Wei-lin’s (甘為霖) death in 1921, the pastor Bakeli (Thomas Barclay) supervised the printing, hence the preface dated July 1923. The book was printed in Yokohama but was destroyed along with 1,500 copies in the Great Kanto Earthquake on September 1, 1923, necessitating reprinting in Shanghai. As demand continued, multiple editions were produced, and it is still available at the Taiwan Church News Agency today.

In addition to the dictionary section, there are appendices for common place names, personal names, biblical location names, family names, the sixty-year cycle, and other charts. Later editions included further additions of new characters at the end of the volumes.

The Romanization method of the Gan Zidian has been widely applied; for example, the Modern Minnan Dictionary edited by Murakami Yoshihiro (村上嘉英) in 1981 is largely based on this method. The Dictionary of Mandarin and Minnan Dialects, edited by the Chinese Language and Literature Research Institute of Xiamen University (厦门大学中国语言文学研究所), published by Fujian People’s Publishing House in 1980 and later issued overseas in 1982, adopted what is called the “Minnan Dialect Romanization Scheme,” showing that Gan’s method holds an undeniable status (Lai Yongxiang 1990: 95-96).

Nursing in Internal and External Medicine

The author of this book is Dr. Dai Ren-shou (George Gushue-Taylor M.B. B.S.F.R.C.S.), who came from Britain and spent nearly thirty years in Taiwan (1911-1940). Most of his life was spent in Taiwan, and like many missionaries, he dedicated his life to the people of Taiwan out of Christian love. He particularly focused on the early care of leprosy patients, and his story as the “Father of Leprosy Patients” has been passed down among many patients and their families. Those who were once isolated by society and abandoned by their families received careful diagnosis and treatment, and he traveled extensively to raise funds for their care, ultimately purchasing land in Bali, Taipei to establish “Le Shan Yuan,” a facility specifically for leprosy patients. His life was filled with legendary accounts and left behind many touching stories that people cherish fondly.Dai Ren-shou (戴仁壽) was born on what is now Newfoundland Island in Canada (then part of Britain, later divided into Canada). He graduated from the University of London Medical School and came to Taiwan in 1911 with his wife, who was a nurse. The couple initially served at Tainan’s Xinlou Hospital, and three years later, he succeeded the returning Ma Ya-ge Er (馬雅各二世) as the director. At that time, Western medicine was flourishing in Taiwan; besides a few public hospitals established by the Japanese government, church hospitals—Xinlou Hospital, Mackay Hospital, and Changhua Hospital—formed a triad that bore the primary medical burden on the island, all while striving to cultivate and educate local medical personnel.

While at Tainan’s Xinlou Hospital, Dr. Dai felt the need to elevate the nursing knowledge and standards among nursing staff and assistants, as well as the necessity to standardize nursing terminology. He referenced nursing manuals and textbooks published by 31 hospitals in Britain, Canada, and Japan, using the manual authored by Miss Oxford at Guy’s Hospital in London as the primary blueprint to adapt into the locally applicable “Nursing in Internal and External Medicine” (內外科看護學). The book spans a total of 675 pages, primarily in Romanized Xiamen dialect, with specialized terms interspersed with English, Japanese, and Chinese characters. It includes 503 illustrations to better help readers understand the material. The entire book is divided into four units: Anatomy and Physiology, General Nursing, Surgical Nursing, and Internal Medicine Nursing. In the end, he compiled medical terminology in three scripts: Chinese, English, and Romanized characters for user reference. The comprehensive coverage of the book is comparable to that of modern nursing texts; although theoretical nursing may be less emphasized, practical nursing techniques are extensive and richly detailed, making it undoubtedly an essential reference work for medical personnel of that era (Chen Meiling 2022: 22-24).

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

Jiaobanian Incident (Yujing Incident)

The Xilai’an Incident (西來庵事件) in 1915, along with the Jiaobanian Incident (噍吧哖事件), can be considered the largest armed anti-Japanese event re-emerging after the initial suppression of Han Chinese resistance in Taiwan by Japanese forces in 1902. The incident began when the central figure, Yu Qingfang (余清芳), gathered crowds at the Xilai’an temple in Tainan to raise the anti-Japanese banner of “Great Ming Compassionate Nation,” aiming to overthrow the Japanese Empire, which had ruled Taiwan for twenty years and was seen as nearing its end. Although religious superstition was one cause of the Xilai’an Incident, its “expansion” was not due to this superstition but rather the devastation of the “forests and fields” that the local populace relied on, caused by the “forest surveys” from 1910 to 1914 that led to the confiscation of land by the Governor-General’s Office. Consequently, the people faced dire hardships. (Cai Jintang (蔡錦堂) 2015: 20-21)

This incident can be described as “an anti-Japanese movement with nationalist revolutionary characteristics,” inspired in part by the success of the Xinhai Revolution in China. Over 2,500 participants were involved in the Xilai’an Incident, prompting the Governor-General’s Office to mobilize military and police forces for a strong crackdown, which took 10 months to fully quell. A total of 1,887 individuals were arrested, and among the 1,413 prosecuted, 866 were sentenced to death. Although a general amnesty was granted due to the enthronement of Emperor Taisho (大正天皇), 95 individuals, including Luo Jun (羅俊) and Yu Qingfang (余清芳), had already been executed. Additionally, 37 others, including Jiang Ding (江定), were not granted clemency and were also executed, resulting in a total of 132 individuals facing capital punishment, an immense sacrifice unmatched by previous events. (Wu Wenxing (吳文星) 2002: 173-174)

In church historical records, we find that Christians suffered as a result of the Xilai’an Incident. For instance, in “News from Jiaobanian,” Taiwan Church Report (臺灣教會報) Volume 371 (February 1916): 10-11, it states: “In the September report, there were mentions of news regarding Jiaobanian; at that time, the congregants were all safe, and only household goods were burned. Unexpectedly, when the church report was published, they encountered the crime of association. A total of 22 congregants were arrested, with 3 pardoned, 7 sentenced to death, and 12 receiving life sentences. Among those sentenced to life, there was a deacon with the surname Pan (潘), first name Dingwang (丁旺), who was fervent about the church. There were 2 new converts, and the rest were believers. Now, the Jiaobanian church has largely lost its strength. Currently, those who sought refuge there have recently been allowed to return to their original homes, constructing straw huts for temporary shelter, but they do not yet have a permanent abode. The atrocities they endured are beyond description. Moreover, the remaining congregants have no opportunities to gather for worship; our chapel has been borrowed by the police for several months, and later, it was borrowed again by the militia for a similar duration. It is only now, as things have become clearer, that we have the chance to gather there for worship again. We had previously been without a place for gathering for several months, holding services in temporary accommodations outside.”

Subsequently, in “Helping Jiaobanian,” Taiwan Church Report (臺灣教會報) Volume 380 (November 1916): 81, it notes, “We received money sent by friends to assist the suffering congregants of Jiaobanian, as recorded below (omitted). A total of 82.60 yuan was provided, and the ministers and current office holders have arranged to distribute these funds accordingly.”
