Taiwan: Agency (1910-1920)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

<p>甘為霖</p>
<p>牧師甘為霖（William Campbell, 1841-1921）是蘇格蘭格拉斯哥人，畢業於格拉斯哥大學與自由教會學院。雖然是蘇格蘭長老教會培養出來的人，但他和馬雅各（James L. Maxwell）、李庥（Hugh Ritchie）、巴克禮（Thomas Barclay）……等蘇格蘭人一樣，加入了英國長老教會，成為英國長老教會的宣教師。甘為霖於1871-1917年在臺灣工作，在臺生活的時間長度僅次於巴克禮，對臺灣教會有很大的貢獻。尤其他於1891年10月創辦的「訓瞽堂」，更是臺灣第一個盲人教育機構。</p>
<p>1871年12月甘為霖以宣教師身分抵達臺灣，除了協助已經成立的教會之外（當時最主要是西拉雅族和巴宰族的教會），更致力於福音未達之地的開拓事工。甘為霖可能是第一位踏查記述日月潭的西洋人，他以荷蘭來臺首位宣教師之名將其命名為「甘治士湖」。</p>
<p>甘為霖是宣教師當中相當勤於做筆記、寫作的一位，著作不少，包括《荷蘭統治下的臺灣》、《臺灣宣教之成功》、《福爾摩沙素描》等，是一位受人重視的學者。1913年他編輯的《廈門音新字典》（簡稱「甘字典」）出版，是白話字、漢字對照的重要工具書，至今仍非常通行。（王昭文2013：14-15）</p>
<p>吳威廉</p>
<p>牧師吳威廉（William Gauld，1861-1923），1861年2月25日出生於加拿大倫敦市西敏鎮（Westminster, London, Ontario）。1880年因聽到馬偕（George L. Mackay）初次返國述職時有關臺灣宣教的演講而深受感動，便立志前往海外宣教。因此，當他從諾克斯神學院（Knox College）畢業之後，便向加拿大海外宣教委員會申請，後1892年受派來臺馬偕，於該年10月22日夜間抵達淡水。吳威廉為人隨和謙遜，做事認真且毅力驚人，在臺灣宣教事業的發展上，不遜於馬偕。吳威廉抵臺的第二年，即面臨馬偕再度返國述職；因此，他必須擔起經營60間教會2000多位信徒、牛津學堂、女學堂、偕醫館等宣教事業的責任。期間臺灣又因甲午、乙未的戰亂，但動盪局勢下，更顯得吳牧師的領導才能。1899年5月吳威廉休假返國，不過因馬偕的病情又被召回，不久馬偕病逝，吳威廉便接下北臺灣宣教事務。有別於馬偕獨斷式的領導風格，吳威廉以組織見長，他連結各個地方堂會，1904年成立北部中會（North Presbytery），1905年組成北部教士會（Mission Council），後又與南部英國長老教會溝通，成立了臺灣大會（Synod of Formosa），奠定系統性運作宣教事業的基礎。除了教會經營的卓越貢獻外，吳威廉的建築專業也令人稱許。1892年抵臺後，教會建築設計的重任大部分落在吳威廉的身上。從文獻資料考證包括馬偕墓（1901）、姑娘樓（1906）、牧師樓（1909）、以及北投、大稻埕等多所教堂、臺北神學校（1918）、馬偕紀念醫院等建築，都為吳威廉所設計主導。有別於馬偕世代的建築，吳威廉規畫的建築營造技術洗鍊，大體呈現英屬亞洲殖民地的風格，為教會建築帶來新的面貌。（修改自王柳鋐撰於開放博物館https://reurl.cc/eGnOg7 2025/01/12瀏覽）</p>
<p>偕叡廉</p>
<p>偕叡廉（George William MacKay，1882-1963）出生在一個宣教家庭，身分格外引人注目，因為他是一位混血兒，父親偕叡理（通稱馬偕George L. Mackay）是加拿大長老教會的宣教師，母親張聰明則是北臺灣五股的本地人。</p>

<p>馬偕返國述職期間，偕叡廉在加拿大唸書以外；年少的他，也被父親送到香港的維多利亞英文學校就讀。馬偕牧師過世後，偕叡廉到加拿大多倫多聖安德烈學院讀書，爾後在多倫多大學，直到獲得美國麻省克拉克大學教育心理學碩士，才繼承父志，向加拿大海外宣道會申請宣教差派，與妻子偕仁利(Jean Ross，1887-1969)共赴北臺灣擔任宣教士。</p>

<p>偕叡廉返臺有一心志，就是要建立一所中學，這所中學要能提升教會子弟以及臺灣青年的知識程度。不負眾望，1914年4月4日，淡水中學校正式設立，這所臺灣最早的本地子弟五年制中學，成為在日治時期的北臺灣年輕子弟，唯一的升學管道，也成為臺北神學校(今為臺灣神學院)的預備學校。充沛的執行力與活動力，相信是受父親的影響所及；但同時，馬偕所為人爭議的特質，包括獨斷、不妥協的性格，也傳承在偕叡廉的身心。</p>

<p>吳希榮</p>

<p>牧師吳希榮，生於1881年6月2日，卒於1922年12月15日；享年41歲。次日就在屏東禮拜堂舉行葬禮，教堂坐得滿滿。弔詞、弔電、敘述故人略歷，會眾無不受感動。另於《臺灣教會報》455（1923.2），頁11-12載有〈吳希榮牧師小傳〉，述及其逸事多則：吳希榮天資高，從小好學，傳教熱心。效法其父吳葛傳道師，講道有力，聲音宏亮，聽者生畏。有人要他小聲一點，他說「不大聲來傳道理，我不願傳」。因此引起咽喉損傷也不顧。他教導人，不分高低，不管是親族、同僚、朋友或長輩，不畏懼，宛然如剛直的施洗者約翰。當時有人告訴他：「你患肺癆，不久會告別人世」。他聽了，不單不退縮，反而決心前往澎湖宣教。他盡力非常，而不但沒有損害身體，反而強壯起來，簡直像伯利恆的勇士大衛一般。他時常關心著上帝的羊群。會友間有失和，他立刻去排解，有親事難解決，他也會設法使其圓滿。耶穌說：「使人和睦的人有福了，因為他們必稱為上帝的兒子」。</p>

<p>他看到台灣教會財政困難而會友缺乏奮發之心，就暫辭嘉義的事奉，返回屏東經營柚園，希望獲利，能補充教會自立之費用。他真的挑蔥賣菜，掌起牛犛把，經營一所果園，逐漸有了成功之兆。他可稱為倡導教會自治的先鋒。</p>

<p>他在澎湖時與黃深河相約，創設一個傳道孤寡會；自己也捐錢，在暗中幫助某先生娘；牧師娘（林路得）也繼承牧師意志，準備接那一家同住，她們有債務也願意代清還。牧師娘亦甚了不起！（賴永祥1995：199）</p>

<p>在1923年3月20日的《南部中會議事錄》第50回紀錄中，撰錄了一篇〈吳希榮牧師小傳〉，全文如下：「吳希榮牧師，生於一八八一年六月二日，卒於一九二二年十二月十五日，享寿四十有一歲。憶自少時敏而好學，及長而任傳教，熱中如焚，講道之時大聲疾呼，令人動聽而生畏恍然。有令先君之氣概。其訓會眾也直言不諱，無分親朋與骨肉，無别同僚與師長。有若施洗約翰之忠直者。且彼関心於選民，盖某甲與某乙為爭端而將訴訟，彼即出為排難解紛，婉言切勸，幾至痛哭流涕，卒能感激其心，使雙方認罪，化爭訟而為和平，殊可稱帝子之美名，亦與魯仲連而並駕。且也視南部教會財政困難，亦知會友少有自治之心，彼乃辞職，旋竭力耕種隹菓，冀有所得利，以為自治經費，雖臥薪嘗胆，披星戴月，亦不辞其勞苦，卒竟種柚千株，漸見成効，其令內賢哲亦同心慘淡經營，彼誠自治之先鋒者也。牧師恆有刻苦之心，惡衣惡食，施濟貧人，曾扶助一傳道師，而為療養其久病，迄今已見康强，復出而為傳教者矣！倘有傳道師費用不足，乃克儉以補助之。嘗見一執事別卋，彼乃顯其忠誠，戴竹笠，著草履，相與舁棺至墳墓，是誠可為信者而取法也。」</p>

<p>先生現遺下三男五女，天資聰敏，讀書成績均得優等，尤深望其克紹箕裘，善継先人之志，俾牧師所作之工無徒勞也。茲不忘先生之大德，因敬撰數語以誌之。</p>

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Taiwan Synod (Synod of Formosa)

Although the southern and northern parts of Taiwan belong to the missionary districts of Britain and Canada respectively, their faith and system are not different, and the churches in the UK and Canada have a historical connection and friendly relations. As early as 1903, proposals for a union between the southern and northern churches emerged, but at that time the differences in church strength were too great to succeed. The southern church had already established an autonomous organization called “Tainan Presbyterian Synod” in 1896 (later renamed “Southern Synod”), while the northern church did not form the “Taipei Presbyterian Synod” until 1904. In 1907, a consensus was reached again to discuss the issue of cooperation between the south and north, confirming that a union was necessary and that it was essential for churches in various places to fully understand this before moving forward.

Soon, communications between the two sides became closer. In 1909, an agreement was reached to delineate the bishoprics, with the western boundary being the Dajia River; the eastern part north of Hualien Port belonged to the northern church, while south of Guanyin Mountain belonged to the southern church. In 1911, representatives from both sides drafted the “Constitution for the Union of Southern and Northern Churches,” which was accepted the following year (1912), and the first meeting was officially held at the Changhua Ximen Street Chapel, marking the first “Taiwan Presbyterian Church Assembly” (referred to as Taiwan Synod). During the meeting, missionary 甘為霖 was elected as the chairman, thereby establishing a mechanism for joint discussion between the southern and northern churches.

Through the discussions at the Taiwan Synod, progress was made in the church system, gradually moving towards organized development. In terms of systems, a liturgical text applicable throughout the island was published in 1919; in 1920, the “Teacher Examination Department” was established to coordinate the examination and promotion of missionaries across Taiwan; in 1926, church music experts were commissioned to formulate a shared hymnal, compiling music scores and local works, giving rise to Taiwan’s “Folk Hymns.” In terms of organizational structure, whether the southern or northern churches established a “Synod” or upgraded to a “General Assembly,” permission from the Taiwan Synod was required. In religious education, a General Secretary for Sunday Schools was established throughout Taiwan to enrich teaching content, renovate classrooms, strengthen statistical reports, introduce Japanese textbooks, hold staff retreats and home visits, and arrange itinerant lectures and adult Sunday Schools in various places. In terms of social services, the Taiwan Synod assisted the Bible Society, leprosy treatment organizations, encouraged abstinence from smoking and drinking, demonstrating that the unity movement had a certain level of consensus and a broad reach, and the Taiwan Synod had its influence.

However, it cannot be overlooked that due to human factors, there were differing opinions between the north and south at times, which led to resolutions being inadequately implemented. Additionally, significant missionary institutions such as secondary schools, seminaries, youth associations, and healthcare facilities were not co-organized, which resulted in a dispersion of effort. Although the regulations stated “the goal is to unify the Taiwan Synod, with reform as the fundamental spirit,” discussions always lacked continuity. At one point, the southern part even believed that the Taiwan Synod incurred significant costs with little practical use, proposing to abolish it or send a few people to “socialize” instead. Thus, after 1935, the Taiwan Synod changed to a biennial meeting, gradually taking the form of a “fellowship meeting.” In fact, there were many factors contributing to the ineffectiveness of the Taiwan Synod, such as a 10-year development gap between the north and south, unclear intentions from the mother churches, lack of direct communication among missionaries, a lack of consensus on missionary work, and a common mindset of isolationism in both north and south, all of which made decision-making difficult to implement.

The decision-making and executive powers of the Taiwan Synod were insufficient, and real power remained divided between the north and south. Although the Taiwan Synod embodied the unity of the church, it lacked complete administrative and budgetary authority. Once the meeting concluded, other times felt like “an empty shell,” with apparent excess in socializing and insufficient real power, making true unity still a distant goal. For example, in 1915, the southern and northern churches responded to the global unity movement and decided to establish a joint seminary in Taipei, but due to disagreements on admission qualifications, course offerings, and the “missionary examination” after graduation, the two schools could not merge. Later, in 1925, the northern faculty and students faced funding shortages and personnel changes due to turmoil in the Canadian mother church. Although some went south to study, northern Taiwanese still opposed establishing a joint seminary in Taichung and recalled the students in 1927. The failure of the merger between the two schools was not merely a matter of location; both the southern and northern churches bore responsibility in terms of meeting procedures, regulatory compliance, and historical interpretation, exposing the weaknesses of the Taiwan Synod’s functionality. (Lu Qiming 2020: 89-127)

General Sunday School Assembly of Taiwan (1917-1926)

Since the British Presbyterian Church (The Presbyterian Church of England) expanded its missionary work to Taiwan in 1865, education has always been an important means of cultivating believers and expanding the church.Among them, the Children’s Sunday School (a course held every Sunday to teach children about the gospel, referred to in English as Sunday School, SS) can be said to be the foundation of Christian education, closely related to the future growth of church attendance. From the late Qing Dynasty to the Japanese colonial period, the British Presbyterian Church gradually developed an organized and systematic Sunday School education.

In April 1917, during the Taiwan Presbyterian Assembly, Pastor 郭水龍 proposed the establishment of a “Sunday School Department” to handle Sunday School affairs in a long-term manner. This proposal was approved by the representatives of the Assembly, and six pastors were elected as committee members: 劉忠堅 (Duncan Macleod), 僑叡廉 (George W. Mackay), 葉金木, 宋忠堅 (Duncan Ferguson), 吳希榮, and 高金聲. On July 17 of the same year, members of the Sunday School Department gathered at the Changhua Chapel to begin discussions, and they sought assistance from Pastor 黃俟命 and Pastor 宋忠堅’s wife (Elizabeth Christie Ferguson). During this meeting, it was decided to adopt the “Universal Sunday School Textbook” as the textbook for Sunday Schools across Taiwan, and to compile a “Baihua Romanization Sunday School Teacher’s Reference Book” as a resource for teachers’ lesson preparations, published quarterly. In addition, 17 missionaries were assigned, with 11 from the south and 6 from the north, to provide district guidance for several churches’ Sunday Schools, aiming to establish robust Sunday Schools in every church. The responsibilities that the “Sunday School Department” undertook thereafter included:

  1. Investigating and compiling statistics on the number of Sunday School teachers and students across Taiwan: Starting in November 1917, questionnaires were distributed, and the following year’s Assembly was the first to present a report on Sunday School conditions in Taiwan. From then until 1926, with the exception of 1923 and 1924, annual statistics and reports became routine.

  2. Editing and printing Sunday School textbooks and reference books for teachers.

  3. Organizing “Teacher Workshops” to improve teaching quality: From July 30 to August 14, 1918, the first workshop was held at a secondary school in Tainan, with 36 participants. The curriculum included studies of the New Testament, teaching methods and practical exercises, Sunday School organization and management, child psychology, music theory, prenatal education and child health, extracurricular lectures, and lectures on 1 Timothy and 2 Timothy.

  4. Starting in January 1919, a column called “Communication Teaching” and “Sunday School Voice” was opened in the Taiwan Church News, featuring updates on local Sunday Schools and teaching method demonstrations.

  5. Dispatching specialists to each district to assist local churches in establishing Sunday Schools.

On April 6, 1926, it was decided in the Taiwan Assembly that each of the northern and southern synods would establish their own “Sunday School Departments.” However, the Assembly still appointed Pastors 高金聲, 葉金木, 陳溪圳, and 潘道榮 to oversee the Sunday School affairs of the entire Taiwan Assembly until 1928, to aid both northern and southern Sunday School Departments in gradually becoming familiar with their tasks until they could fully manage the Sunday School education in their jurisdictions independently.

In summary, the “Sunday School Department” established between 1917 and 1926 was dedicated to improving the educational affairs of the church, transitioning Sunday Schools from their founding phase into an era of organization and expansion following 1926. (Zhang Miaojuan 2010: 79-103)

XIN LO HOSPITAL

In 1865, the first medical missionary to Taiwan, James L. Maxwell, established a medical clinic in Kanjia Street, which later evolved into Qihou Hospital and then Tainan Old Building (Erlaokou) Hospital. “XIN LO HOSPITAL” was established in 1900, and before 1935, it was primarily managed by foreign medical personnel and trained local assistants and nurses based on actual needs. The hospital provided low-cost services, but due to rising salaries and competition from private institutions, it still relied on subsidies from the British mother church and grants from the governor’s office. The hospital particularly emphasized ventilation, lighting, and classification of wards, demonstrating good efficacy for diseases such as tuberculosis and leprosy, leading to hospitalized patients often finding it easier to convert to Christianity under careful care compared to outpatients. For a long time, the hospital and the church needed to share limited resources, and due to the impact of religious policies, medical missionary activities gradually diminished after 1935.

In 1900, after the new hospital was inaugurated and named “XIN LO HOSPITAL,” it was equipped with Taiwan’s first manual elevator, making it convenient for patients to access the operating room on the second floor. Various advanced medical equipment was introduced, and isolation wards were set up. In 1901, Maxwell’s second son, also a doctor, James L. Maxwell, Jr., came to Tainan with his wife, who had a nursing background, to carry on his father’s medical missionary mission.He actively cultivated local medical personnel, utilized steam sterilizers and X-ray machines, and promoted the cessation of opium use, prevention of sexually transmitted diseases, and care and treatment for leprosy patients. Between 1908 and 1909, 马雅各二世 returned to England, and during his absence, Dr. 兰大卫 served as the acting director of the Changhua Christian Hospital.

From 1914 to 1918, Dr. 戴仁壽 served as the director, placing emphasis on nursing and hygiene. In 1917, he published “Nursing for Internal and External Medicine,” written in Baihua, which became an important medical textbook. In 1919, 马雅各二世 returned to serve as director again, and in 1922, he established a rehabilitation center (opium detoxification center) and leprosy treatment facility, opening a new chapter for treating these special conditions.

In summary, during the 1910s, XIN LO HOSPITAL was in a developmental phase, demonstrating considerable progress compared to the unstable 馬偕醫院 and the nascent Changhua Hospital. Although finances were often in deficit due to low medical fees, the hospital began to brave attempts to implement a self-sustaining policy without relying on the mother church, despite not achieving complete success. The personnel organization was quite stable; for example, 马雅各二世 spent significantly more time in Taiwan than his father, totaling 23 years throughout his life. Medical practices strengthened ward nursing, differentiating between male and female wards as well as tuberculosis wards. The developmental phase of XIN LO HOSPITAL achieved significant results in medical education, medical services, and missionary work. (Tang Huiting 2008: 181-200)

Mackay Memorial Hospital

In 1872, Pastor 马偕 (George L. Mackay) landed in Huwei (Tamsui) and began his medical practice. In 1880, he established the first Western hospital in northern Taiwan, “Mackay Hospital,” in Tamsui, named in honor of another individual with the same surname, “Captain Mackay.” The hospital was instrumental during the Sino-French War in 1884, when medical personnel rushed to rescue soldiers as French warships bombarded Tamsui, marking the first Red Cross-like rescue effort in Taiwan. By 1901, after Mackay’s death at his residence in Tamsui, the hospital was temporarily closed. In 1905, the Canadian mother church sent a pastor and physician, 宋雅各 (J. Y. Ferguson), along with his wife, to Tamsui, and they reopened the hospital the following year. In 1911, 宋雅各 proposed moving the medical center from Tamsui to Taipei, and in memory of Pastor Mackay, expanded the hospital, renaming it Mackay Memorial Hospital, which was completed at the end of 1912. In 1912, the mother church sent Miss Isabel Elliot to Taiwan to establish a “Nursing School,” training early nurses in northern Taiwan at this time. However, due to the impacts of the European War, the school was closed for six years, reopening on January 1, 1925. The 1910s were a period of significant upheaval for Mackay Memorial Hospital; the years 1911-1918 marked a transformative phase, but operations were interrupted due to the effects of the European War. (Xu Zhuangzhuang 2010: 1-60)

Changhua Hospital

Dr. 兰大衛 (David Landsborough III, 1870-1957) arrived in Taiwan in 1895 during the Japanese rule; in 1896, he founded Changhua Hospital, marking the beginning of his 40-year commitment to medical care, ministry, education, and relief efforts.

Medical: In addition to outpatient services, the hospital included round trips to 大社 for clinical rounds from April and May to August, as well as home visits requested by the public. Outpatient services were divided into internal medicine, general surgery, and ophthalmology, and 兰大衛 was well-known for his surgical expertise, performing significantly more eye surgeries than on other body parts. The last visit to 大社 for medical rounds happened in 1906, and there were no further trips after that. For home visits, he adhered to Han customs, showing respect and sensitivity towards women. Initially, the consultation was free, but starting in 1904, the fee was approximately one dim of currency per outpatient; from 1912 onward, regardless of the surgery size, the fee was a flat rate of one yen (equivalent to two shillings). Additionally, he participated in epidemic prevention efforts and anti-smoking campaigns, pioneering leprosy treatment in church hospitals. It is estimated that around 100,000 patients received treatment annually, averaging 300 patients per day; this number accounted for about 10% of the Taichung region’s population and 70-80% of Changhua’s population, making it the largest private hospital in central Taiwan during the Japanese rule.

Ministry: 兰大衛 practiced typical medical evangelism; initially, when people were unaware of the hospital’s existence, he spent more time on home visits than in the hospital. After marriage, with the assistance of 连瑪玉 (Marjorie Landsborough), the connection between the hospital and the church became even closer. By 1909, the number of churches in the central region expanded from four in 1896 to 23, laying the foundation for 兰大衛’s medical evangelism.Education: Through examinations, intelligent youth were selected and taught medical skills. The instruction utilized Taiwanese Romanization (Baihua) and employed monkeys or cadavers as practical materials for anatomy training. The teaching methods were not much different from today’s practices, although foundational medical knowledge was relatively weak, while clinical medicine was already detailed and specific. After four or five years of training, graduates were issued certificates by the hospital; based on this, they could then pass the Japanese government’s examination to obtain a formal medical license. However, after 1930, the Japanese government strictly implemented the “limited physician” policy, leading students to lead a migratory and drifting life.

Relief: Free medical treatment was provided for impoverished and sick patients, and assistance was offered for their schooling and employment. Statistics indicated that the expenses incurred for free patients accounted for 30% to 50% of what self-paying patients contributed, which was a significant ratio. Consequently, the hospital was recognized as a charitable institution by the Japanese government, receiving “Imperial Grants” 12 times; the Empress of Japan granted an “Imperial Song” and a “Royal Plaque,” and when Prince Hirohito visited Taiwan, he also met with 兰大衛, awarding him a commendation. All these instances are evidence of his dedication to humanitarian efforts. Notably, 兰大衛’s act of taking four pieces of flesh from his wife’s thigh to perform a skin graft on 周金耀 was particularly praised as an example of “selfless love.”

In conclusion, 兰大衛 devoted 40 years to medical missions in Taiwan, with his greatest accomplishments and influences being the establishment of Changhua Hospital. In addition to treating patients and teaching students medical techniques, he contributed to epidemic prevention, smoking cessation, and leprosy treatment. In doing so, he introduced modern medicine to central Taiwan, significantly improving healthcare and hygiene conditions. (Li Xinfen 1989: 212-215)

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

South-North Theological School Union Issue

The so-called “Theological School Union Issue” was formally discussed during the second Taiwan conference in 1913. Pastor 高金聲 from the south proposed that the two missionary societies negotiate on whether the southern and northern theological schools could unite. A six-member team was immediately appointed during the meeting to discuss this with the two missionary societies. The following year (1914), 高氏 reported at the conference that he had corresponded with the two missionary societies and discussed this matter. 吳威廉 believed that the two schools should maintain the status quo, and the proposal for students to study two years in the north and two years in the south was currently very difficult to implement. Later, the conference decided: 1. to ask the two missionary societies to continue exploring the “temporary two years of study in the north and two years in the south” plan. 2. to establish a “Theological School Department” to discuss enrollment rules, admission qualifications, curriculum, and other related matters. 3. that Taiwan should ideally have only one theological school. 4. to send someone to negotiate with the missionary societies on how to unite the two schools. Clearly, the benefits of jointly running theological education include saving costs, promoting collaboration, and even raising standards, but the premise is that there must be a consensus on the educational system and campus location.

In 1915, the conference decided to select two locations from the north and south by vote, resulting in 25 votes for Taipei, 6 for Tainan, 4 for Taichung, and 0 for Danshui. The location was determined, and the two missionary societies were entrusted with the construction of the theological school. In the summer of 1917, the foundation for the united theological school was laid in Niu Pu Zhuang, Taipei, and it was completed the following spring. This newly built grand structure was designed by 吳威廉 according to the conference’s resolutions, and for the north, it indeed exceeded its needs.

However, due to significant differences in student proficiency, the implementation of the united theological school became indefinitely postponed. The Taiwan conference in 1916 decided to discuss admission qualifications. 北神校長 約美但 proposed that students should at least have completed high school and study for four years; required subjects included Old and New Testament scriptures, church history, systematic theology, homiletics, pastoral studies, ethics, philosophy, religious pedagogy, Chinese literature, English, and Japanese. 約美但, a biblical scholar and educator, gradually incorporated higher specialized knowledge into the curriculum during his tenure from 1907 to 1916, and in 1909 established the academic year system for Taipei Theological School, stipulating that all entrants must complete primary school before studying for six years at the theological school to graduate. Therefore, his proposal aimed to enhance the academic standards of the theological school.

At that time, the southern school had no strict admission requirements. Between 1903 and 1913, besides the Bible and Four Books and Five Classics, some subjects in mathematics and science were taught. It wasn’t until 河野政喜 joined the faculty in 1914 that deeper subjects such as philosophy and systematic theology were introduced. Even so, the proficiency level of the entrants remained uneven. For example, among the 13 students admitted in 1926, only two had graduated from high school; three had equivalent qualifications, while the others were primary school graduates, including some who did not even know the Romanization of Taiwanese.

Additionally, 巴克禮 considered that students needed to have practical training and should preferably not leave the south, thus stating: “Given the existing difficulties, it cannot determine when to open the united school”; 約美但 suggested, “Due to some inconvenient aspects, it would be appropriate to postpone, as both sides still hope to wait another two to three years before implementation.” The conference finally resolved to follow 約美但’s opinion; however, he soon went to Korea and passed the baton of 北神 to 吳威廉, so before the Taiwan conference convened in 1917, there was no further discussion on the system of the united theological school.

In 1918, the agenda of the Taiwan conference included much discussion on academic regulations. Ultimately, 吳威廉 proposed a draft, and the conference resolved to “temporarily use it for one year, to be reconsidered later, but it must be recorded,” stipulating that at least a high school level was required, with a four-year study to graduate. The curriculum included national language (Japanese), Chinese literature, English, poetics, explanation of scripture phonetics, the history of Christianity, church history, general outlines of Old/New Testament books, the life of Paul and his letters, homiletics, interpretation of Old/New Testament books, systematic theology, homiletics, pastoral studies, philosophy, psychology, and pedagogy.

From this, it is clear that the system had considerable scale, covering the four major parts of theological education: Scripture, History, Systematic, and Practical, almost comparable to the core curriculum of modern theological seminaries. The following year, 北神 took the lead in revising the academic system to accept high school graduates, requiring four years of study, and changing the ranking to be based on the year of graduation. 南神 did not make significant progress.

From 1921 to 1926, there were no discussions regarding the location, and the Taiwan conference turned to discuss the “level” issue of the theological school. Internal factors certainly included discussions about merging, for instance, in 1921, the Theological School Department discussed how to set the identity of non-high school graduates seeking admission to the theological school. Later, the conference decided that the southern and northern missionary societies should find ways on their own, and if the original admission qualifications were to be changed, it would be determined by the Theological School Department for future consideration.

On the other hand, the Governor-General’s Office issued a new “Taiwan Education Order” in 1922 to align with the extension policy of the mainland.In June, the order No. 138 was issued, promulgating the “Private School Regulations,” which includes six chapters and incorporates private schools into the entire education system, applying public regulations. In 1922, the Taiwan conference began actively discussing the identities for entering theological school, deciding to classify them into two categories: high school graduates as “undergraduate students” and those with three years of high school education as “selected students.” The southern missionary society further believed that the best solution to the current theological education problems was to establish the united theological school in Taipei; however, before that could be realized, strengthening the Tainan Theological School should still be prioritized, indicating a generally positive attitude. As for southern high school students wishing to attend the northern theological school, they would need to apply for approval.

In summary, the discussion on the “united theological school” began at the Taiwan conference in 1913. The proposal to “study for two years separately in the north and the south” only remained in the negotiation phase without resolution in 1914. In 1915, the site was decided to be in Taipei, and a new campus was completed in 1917. However, according to the discussions at the conference, between 1915 and 1924, both sides lacked consensus on admission qualifications, curriculum content, church internships, and the “Ministerial Examination” after graduation, leading to the southern and northern schools continuing their separate paths with many unresolved issues. During this time, the northern school gradually raised admission standards and course levels, while the southern school progressed slowly. When the Governor-General’s Office issued the “Private School Regulations” in 1922, although both the north and south obtained “establishment approval,” significant differences had already emerged. Thus, compared to the selection of the campus location, the inability to unify the academic system remains the primary reason for the failure to merge.

Three Self Movement

The so-called Three Self Movement refers to self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation, which is part of the indigenization of Christianity. In China, the goal of promoting this was to encourage believers to take responsibility and eliminate the prejudice against “foreign religions.” In 1877, at a national missionary conference in China, John Butler of the Ningbo Presbyterian Church was the first to propose that the work of local churches should primarily be in the hands of local people as the greatest guarantee for Christianity to take root and to reduce public criticism of foreign religions (吳學明 1997: 83).

It is worth noting that when the Chinese church began to advocate for the Three Self Movement, it coincided with the introduction of Christianity into Taiwan. In the south, this began with physician 馬雅各 in 1865, while in the north, it was pioneered by 馬偕 in 1872. After half a century of foundational development, by the mid-1910s during the Japanese colonial period, local voices began to advocate for the self-reliance movement.

By 1915, the southern church reached its 50th anniversary. Although there had been discussions about church autonomy and self-support in the past, there had been no serious advocacy. After 吳希榮 was appointed as the pastor of the 阿緱 (Pingtung) church in May 1915, he began to vigorously promote the ideas of church self-government, self-support, and self-propagation; his words were somewhat radical for the time but showed considerable foresight. He stated that the Taiwanese church had been established for 50 years and should no longer rely on subsidies from the British mother church; continuing to do so was akin to a 50-year-old man still being breastfed. 吳希榮’s advocacy received support from his fellow pastors at the time, including Lin Yanchen from Donggang, Gao Duxing from Wandan, Zheng Xipan from Ligang, and Liao Dezhi, who was known for his evangelistic efforts. As a result, they either held meetings for discussions or engaged in public advocacy to strongly encourage church autonomy (楊士養 1965: 123). The influence of this movement led to the establishment of the “Kaohsiung Prefectural Church Affairs Bureau” by the southern church association, which inspired places like Tainan, Chiayi, and Taichung, eventually leading to the delineation of the church association in 1930.

The northern church also made progress. In February 1912, the 14th Northern Church Association convened and passed regulations to officially establish a “Missionary Bureau.” In September, they reviewed and approved the “Constitution of the Taipei Presbyterian Church.” By June 1916, the Governor-General’s Office recognized the organizational establishment of the “Northern Taiwan Christian Presbyterian Church,” further solidifying its status (徐謙信 1955: 20-24).

Recognition of 甘為霖

Missionary 甘為霖 (William Campbell) frequently interacted with the Governor-General’s Office, not only negotiating on behalf of believers facing adversities but also encouraging official attention to the education of the blind. In 1896, the blind school at 洪公祠 in Tainan closed due to the expiration of its lease. During his vacation in Tokyo, 甘氏 visited former governor 樺山資紀 to express the needs for blind education in Taiwan. Subsequently, Governor 兒玉源太郎 adopted 甘為霖’s recommendation and established a government-run blind and deaf school in Tainan, appointing the Christian teacher 秋山衍藏 from Tainan Middle School as its first principal, allowing students to freely participate in worship or attend sermons after class. In 1897, 甘氏 negotiated with the Japanese to select three graduates from the Tainan Blind Training School, raising funds for their study expenses in Japan through a charity concert.On June 17, 1915 (the anniversary of the beginning of governance), the Governor-General’s Office awarded 甘為霖 (William Campbell) the Fifth Class Order of the Rising Sun for his contributions in promoting Taiwan’s governance and correcting foreign misunderstandings through his writings. In late April 1916, upon the invitation of the civil governor, 甘為霖 delivered a series of lectures at the national language school auditorium in Taipei, covering topics such as (1) Taiwan historical data, (2) the Dutch occupation of Taiwan, (3) China’s governance over Taiwan, and (4) Japan’s initial colonization of Taiwan. These lectures were translated by officials from the Governor-General’s Office and took place over four consecutive nights, attended by over 200 officials and dignitaries. When 甘為霖 retired in 1917, Governor 安東貞美 sent a letter informing him that he had been awarded the Fourth Class Order of the Sacred Treasure by the Japanese consul in Hong Kong. The governor even made a special trip to Tainan to express his gratitude to 甘為霖 for his achievements in Taiwan, which underscores 甘為霖’s significance to the colonial authorities.

Key Theological Issues

Xiamen Pronunciation New Dictionary (甘字典)

After the publication of William Campbell’s《厦門音新字典》 in 1913, it became quite popular, commonly referred to as the “甘字典”. The English title is A Dictionary of Amoy Vernacular spoken throughout the prefectures of Chin-chiu, Chiang-chiu and Formosa. The first edition was printed by the Yokohama Gospel Printing Company and a thousand copies were published by the Taiwan Church News Agency.

The book includes approximately 15,000 characters selected from the “Kangxi Dictionary” and also refers to dictionaries compiled by Westerners, such as those by Morrison, Walter H. Medhurst, St. Williams, Giles, Talmage’s 《廈門音的字典》, George Mackay’s 《中西字典》, and works by Father Couvreur, MacGowan, and Hare.

This pronunciation is derived from the local dialect spoken in the Zhang-Jiang-Tai region, with emphasis added to the accent around Tainan, as the assistants were Lin Jincheng and Chen Daluo. It makes extensive references to the Zhangzhou pronunciation “Fifteen Sounds,” retaining the original character forms and usages listed. Organized by single sounds according to the pronunciation, it uses “ch” and “ts” to distinguish between the two accents, similar to the vernacular Bible. If a character has no original form, a phonetic reading is provided, with a dash indicating the absence of a suitable character. Each character generally occupies one line, with simple annotations and usages. If a character has multiple pronunciations, it appears in several places, for example, the word for song appears as ko and koa; the word for to see appears as ba̍k and bo̍k.

The《廈英大辭典》compiled by杜嘉德 includes English annotations without corresponding Chinese characters, while Bakley’s supplementary edition includes Chinese characters but retains English annotations, indicating its primary audience was Westerners. In contrast, the annotations in the 甘字典 are entirely in vernacular, allowing Chinese readers to learn characters using Xiamen pronunciation, which is why it is beloved and widely used by the islanders.

The second edition of the 甘字典 was published in 1924. Since 甘為霖 passed away in 1921, the printing was overseen by Pastor Thomas Barclay, hence the preface dated July 1923. The book was printed in Yokohama but was largely destroyed in the Great Kanto Earthquake on September 1, 1923, with 1,500 copies lost, necessitating a reprint in Shanghai. Due to ongoing demand, it has seen several reprints, and copies are still available through the Taiwan Church News Agency.

Beyond the dictionary section, there are appendices for common geographical names, personal names, biblical place names and personal names, surnames, the sixty Jiazi, and seasonal tables. Subsequent editions have included additional new character supplements.

The romanization method of the 甘字典 has been widely applied; for example, the 1981 edition of《現代閩南語辭典》edited by 村上嘉英 is largely based on this method. The《普通話閩南方言詞典》compiled by Xiamen University’s Research Institute of Chinese Language and Literature used the so-called “Min Nan Dialect Spelling Plan, Romanization from the 甘字典, and a simplified chart for International Phonetic Alphabet.” Thus, the significance of the 甘法 cannot be overlooked. (賴永祥 1990: 95-96)

Nursing in Internal Medicine and Surgery

The author of this book is Dr. George Gushue-Taylor M.B. B.S. F.R.C.S., who came from the UK and spent nearly thirty years in Taiwan (1911-1940). He spent most of his life in Taiwan, and like many missionaries, devoted his life to the people of Taiwan with a heart full of love for humanity. He dedicated his prime years entirely to the care of leprosy patients. His renowned story as the “Father of Leprosy Patients” is well-known among many patients and their families. At that time, these patients, who were isolated by society and abandoned by their families, not only received meticulous medical treatment from him, but he also traveled around the world multiple times to raise funds for them. He purchased land in Bali, Taipei, to build a facility called “樂山園” specifically for leprosy patients. His life was filled with legends, leaving behind many touching stories that people cherish and remember.Dr. George Gushue-Taylor was born on the island of Newfoundland in present-day Canada (then part of the British Empire). He graduated from the medical school of the University of London in England and came to Taiwan in 1911 with his wife, who was a nurse. The couple initially served at the Tainan Xinlou Hospital, and three years later, he succeeded the returning Dr. Ma Yajue II as the hospital director. At that time, Western medicine in Taiwan was flourishing, with a few public hospitals set up by the Japanese government, and church hospitals such as Xinlou Hospital, Mackay Hospital, and Changhua Hospital forming a triad that bore the main burden of healthcare on the island while also striving to cultivate and educate local medical personnel.

While at Tainan Xinlou Hospital, Dr. Gushue-Taylor recognized the need to improve the medical knowledge and standards of nursing staff and assistants, as well as the need to standardize nursing terminology. He referred to nursing manuals and textbooks published by 31 hospitals in the UK, Canada, and Japan, using the manual written by Miss Oxford of Guys Hospital as the primary model, adapting it to meet local medical needs in the form of《內外科看護學》. The book consists of 675 pages, primarily written in romanized Xiamen dialect, with specialized terminology interspersed with English, Japanese, and Chinese characters. The book includes 503 illustrations to help readers better understand its content. It is divided into four units: Anatomy and Physiology, General Nursing, Surgical Nursing, and Internal Medicine Nursing. Finally, he compiled medical vocabulary in Chinese, English, and romanization for reference convenience. The breadth of the book is comparable to that of modern nursing studies; while theoretical nursing may be less prevalent, practical nursing techniques are diverse and comprehensive, making it undoubtedly an essential work for medical personnel of that era. (陳美玲 2022: 22-24)

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

噍吧哖 Incident (Yu Jing Incident)

The events of the Xilai’an Incident in 1915 (the first part) and the噍吧哖 Incident (the latter part) can be considered the largest armed anti-Japanese incident re-emerging after the Japanese military thoroughly suppressed the armed resistance of Han Chinese people in Taiwan in 1902. The incident was initiated by the central figure 余清芳, who used the Zhaitang Xilai’an in Tainan County to gather people to raise the anti-Japanese banner of “Great Ming Compassionate Nation,” intending to overthrow the Japanese Empire which had ruled Taiwan for twenty years. Although religious superstition was one of the main reasons for the occurrence of the Xilai’an Incident, its “expansion” was not due to religious superstition but rather the “forest land” that the residents of 噍吧哖 relied on for their livelihood was confiscated by the Governor’s Office as a result of the “Forest Survey” from 1910 to 1914, leading to dire living conditions for the people. (蔡錦堂2015:20-21)

This incident can be described as “an anti-Japanese action with revolutionary national character,” and it was undoubtedly inspired by the success of the Xinhai Revolution in China. Over 2,500 participants were involved in the Xilai’an Incident, prompting the Governor’s Office to deploy military and police forces for a strong crackdown, which lasted for 10 months before being completely suppressed. A total of 1,887 people were arrested and investigated in connection with the case, among whom 1,413 were prosecuted; 866 were sentenced to death. Although many received a commutation of sentence due to the enthronement of Emperor Taisho, aside from the 95 individuals already executed, including 羅俊 and 余清芳, there were still 37 individuals, such as 江定, who did not receive commutation and were executed, resulting in a total of 132 individuals facing the death penalty, marking an unprecedented level of sacrifice. (吳文星2002:173-174)

In church historical materials, it can be seen that some Christians suffered due to the Xilai’an Incident. For example, in the article “News from 噍吧哖,” published in Volume 371 of the Taiwan Church News (February 1916), pages 10-11, it notes: “In the September report, there was news about 噍吧哖; at that time, the congregation members were safe, with only household goods being burned. Unexpectedly, when the church report was published, they faced collective punishment. There were a total of 22 members arrested, 3 exempted, 7 sentenced to death, and 12 sentenced to life imprisonment. Among those sentenced to life, one deacon named 潘丁旺 was very enthusiastic about the church. There were 2 converts, and the rest were believers. Now, the church in 噍吧哖 has mostly lost its strength. Currently, the homes of the sheltering members there have recently been approved by the government for them to return to their original residences, living temporarily in thatched huts, and they have not yet built proper shelters. The tragedy among them is beyond words. Furthermore, the other members have no chance to gather for worship; our chapel has been borrowed by government police officials for several months, and then lent again to strongmen for a similar duration. Now that things have become clearer, we finally have the opportunity to gather there for worship again. Previously, for several months, there was no place to gather, and we had been worshipping in dormitories outside.”

Next, in “Support for 噍吧哖,” published in Volume 380 of the Taiwan Church News (November 1916), page 81, it states: “We have received monetary assistance sent by friends to support the suffering members of 噍吧哖, as noted below (excerpted), totaling 82.60 Taiwanese dollars; the preachers and officials at the current congregation are trying to distribute this amount.”
