China: Context (1990-2000)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

Relevant Regulations on Religion

  1. Circular on Some Problems Concerning the Further Improvement of Work on Religion, Document No. 6, 1991 (中共中央文獻研究室綜合研究組、國務院宗教事務局政策法規司1995: 213-221; 邢福增2006: 9; Chinese Law and Government 2000a: 56-63)

In December 1990, the National Religious Work Conference was held in Beijing. The conference discussed and passed the Circular on Some Problems Concerning the Further Improvement of Work on Religion (referred to as Document No. 6), which clarified the direction and tasks of religious work going forward. Document No. 6 affirmed that “the overall situation of religious work is good,” but also pointed out that “foreign hostile forces have continuously taken advantage of religion to infiltrate and carry out destructive activities against us,” and indicated that there are “a few hostile elements that are active, establishing illegal organizations to contest leadership in temples, monasteries, and churches,” as well as “illegally running scripture schools, seminaries, and theological colleges to compete for the youth.” Therefore, it is essential to recognize the complexity and seriousness of religious issues and to pay great attention to religious work.

The document covers six key points: (1) to be conducive to “uniting the vast masses of believers and to the stability of the nation and society,” comprehensive and correct implementation of the policy of freedom of religious belief must be executed; (2) religious affairs must be managed according to law; (3) fully leverage the role of patriotic religious groups; (4) resolutely combat criminal activities utilizing religion; (5) improve the organizational structure of religious work and strengthen the construction of religious work cadres; (6) enhance the leadership of the party in religious work.

Among them, the management of religious affairs according to law can be said to be the focus of Document No. 6. It mentions that religious activities should be included within the scope of laws, regulations, and policies, clearly stating that “all places of religious activity must be registered according to law,” and firmly prohibiting the illegal preaching activities of “self-appointed preachers.” At the same time, foreign religious groups and individuals are not allowed to interfere in China’s religious affairs, including establishing offices, building temples, monasteries, and churches, or conducting missionary activities. Projects with foreign cooperation “must not carry conditions related to missionary work, establishment of religious institutions, or building of temples, monasteries, and churches.” It also indicates that “we must accelerate religious legislation,” which involves administrative regulations concerning religious affairs and allows provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities to formulate local religious administrative regulations. This initiated a new phase in the religious legislative work of China in the 1990s.

In April 1991, the State Council’s Bureau of Religious Affairs submitted a report titled “Ideas on the Religious Legislative System and Religious Legislative Projects During the ‘Eighth Five-Year Plan’” and proposed four levels of legislative ideas: (1) laws promulgated by the National People’s Congress or its Standing Committee that adjust the relationship between religion and other aspects of society as a whole, ensure citizens’ rights to freedom of religious belief, protect the legitimate rights and interests of religious organizations, and ensure the normalization of religious activities, i.e., to formulate a basic law concerning religion. At that time, three proposals were envisioned: (i) to formulate a Religious Law; (ii) to separately formulate laws regarding freedom of religious belief and religious organizations; (iii) to formulate general principles for religious laws. (2) Administrative regulations promulgated by the State Council adjusting issues common to various religions that can be resolved within government jurisdiction, such as management of religious venues, management of religious personnel, management of foreigners’ religious activities in China, and management of religious audiovisual products. (3) Departmental regulations that adjust issues with a smaller scope, strong policy orientation, and fall within the realm of internal departmental management. (4) Local religious regulations or local government regulations formulated by local people’s congresses or governments based on local conditions. Additionally, the promotion and support for religious groups and temples to combine their characteristics to formulate internal management systems and regulations according to law (not part of legislation). From subsequent developments, it can be seen that, apart from the first proposal, the others have been implemented to varying degrees.

This is the second policy document concerning religious issues and work from the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China since Document No. 19 in 1982. The next similar central document was the Decision of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Strengthening Religious Work in 2002 (Document No. 3).

National Religious Administrative Regulations 1994

  1. (國家宗教事務局政策法規司2000: 2-6, 10-14; 中國法制出版社2002: 4-5, 15; 上海市委統戰部課題組2004: 28-29; Chinese Law and Government 2000a: 64-74)

Administrative regulations are a general term for normative legal documents related to administrative management and administration of administrative matters promulgated by the highest national authority, the State Council. Their status is lower than that of the constitution and laws, but higher than that of general local regulations.

On January 31, 1994, Premier Li Peng signed State Council Orders No. 144 and 145, promulgating the Regulations on the Supervision of the Religious Activities of Foreigners in China (Order No. 144) and the Regulations Regarding the Management of Places of Religious Activities (Order No. 145).

Order No. 144 consists of a total of 13 articles and is the first regulation specifically addressing the religious activities of foreigners in China. Subsequently, the State Administration for Religious Affairs’ Interpretation of Certain Provisions of the Regulations on the Supervision of the Religious Activities of Foreigners in China was issued in April 1994, followed by the Specific Rules for Implementing the Regulations on the Management of Religious Activities of Foreigners in China in September 2000 (revised in November 2010).

The term “foreigners” as referred to in Order No. 144 also includes “Chinese citizens who reside abroad” and residents of Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau (Article 11). There is a clause stating “respect for the religious beliefs of foreigners” (Article 2), which the authorities interpret as “regardless of which religion they believe in, and whether this religion exists in China or not, the Chinese government shows respect.” At the same time, it stipulated that foreigners conducting religious activities in China “must not establish religious organizations, set up religious offices, establish places for religious activities, or run religious schools in China, nor develop believers among Chinese citizens, appoint religious personnel, and carry out other missionary activities” (Article 8). This reflects the principle of “differentiated treatment” adopted by the Chinese government—on one hand, respecting the religious activities of foreigners of different faiths in China (excluding preaching to Chinese people and developing believers), and on the other hand, limiting the “legitimate religions” available for Chinese people to the “five major religions.”

Order No. 144 stipulates that “foreigners may participate in religious activities at temples, monasteries, mosques, churches, and other religious venues in China. Invited by provincial, autonomous region, or direct-controlled municipality religious organizations, foreigners may preach and give sermons in Chinese religious venues” (Article 3). Additionally, religious activities “with participation from foreigners” can be held in locations approved by the religious affairs department of the county-level people’s government (Article 4). This refers to the “designated religious venues” approved by the State Administration for Religious Affairs for foreigners in China. These designated venues are not only for the five major religions recognized by the Chinese government (for instance, the Beijing International Mandarin Church was approved for establishment on August 12, 2007) but also include religions outside the five major religions (such as Mormonism). In 2000, the State Administration for Religious Affairs stated in the Specific Rules for Implementing the Regulations on the Management of Religious Activities of Foreigners in China that “foreign religious organizations and their members without corresponding legitimate Chinese religious organizations in China” may engage in interaction with relevant departments of the Chinese government or religious communities, with the consent of the provincial religious affairs department and subsequent approval from the national authorities (Article 9).

The issuance of Order No. 144 reflects the continuous increase in the number of foreigners working and studying in China as part of the reform and opening-up. Taking Shanghai as an example, official estimates show that the number of foreigners living in Shanghai increased from 3 in 1998.

Religious Activity Regulations and Management

In 2019, the number of foreign residents in Shanghai increased from 50,000 in 1998 to 180,000. Among the foreign individuals in China, there are many who practice various religions. In addition to the five major religions recognized by the Chinese government (Buddhism, Taoism, Islam, Christianity, and Catholicism), there are also various other religions (such as Judaism, Mormonism, Baha’i Faith, etc.). Meeting the religious activity needs of these foreigners has become a challenge for China’s religious work. It has been reported that consulates from countries such as the United States, Canada, South Korea, France, Italy, and Australia in Shanghai have repeatedly demanded that the Shanghai Municipal Government and relevant departments arrange for the religious lives of their citizens in Shanghai. Other foreigners and individuals from Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan, and overseas have also continuously made new religious requests.

Order No. 145 consists of a total of 20 articles and outlines management measures for places of religious activities, introducing specific requirements and procedures for registration. Its promulgation was in accordance with the requirements of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China’s Document No. 6 (1991): “All places of religious activities must be registered in accordance with the law (detailed methods will be specified separately).”

The purpose of the regulations is “to protect normal religious activities, maintain the legitimate rights and interests of places of religious activities, and facilitate the management of places of religious activities” (Article 1). It stipulates that “temples, monasteries, mosques, churches, and other designated places for conducting religious activities” “must register. Registration methods will be formulated by the State Council’s Bureau of Religious Affairs” (Article 2). Furthermore, “no one may use places of religious activities to conduct activities that undermine national unity, ethnic solidarity, social stability, harm citizens’ health, and interfere with the national education system,” and that “they are not subject to the control of foreign organizations and individuals” (Article 4). “The religious affairs departments of the people’s governments at county level and above shall provide guidance and supervision on the implementation of these regulations” (Article 13), and the interpretation of these regulations falls under the responsibility of the State Administration for Religious Affairs (Article 19).

In April, the State Administration for Religious Affairs issued the Registration Procedures for Venues for Religious Activities. To establish a place for religious activities, the following conditions must be met: (1) a fixed location and name; (2) citizens who regularly participate in religious activities; (3) an organizational management structure composed of believing citizens; (4) religious personnel or individuals who meet the religious qualifications to host religious activities; (5) management regulations; (6) legal economic income (Article 2). Regarding registration procedures, the responsible person of the management organization of such a venue must submit an application for establishment registration to the religious affairs department of the people’s government at county level or above (Article 4). Those that meet the requirements will be registered, while those that are “not fully compliant” will be subject to “temporary registration or deferred registration” based on circumstances, and those that do not meet the requirements “will not be registered” (Article 6).

In July 1996, the State Administration for Religious Affairs issued the Method for the Annual Inspection of Places of Religious Activity (21 articles), requiring registered places of religious activity to undergo annual inspections. The annual inspection content includes: (1) compliance with national laws, regulations, and policies; (2) the formulation and implementation of management regulations; (3) major religious activities and foreign-related activities; (4) major financial management and income and expenditure situation; (5) changes in registered items; (6) changes and management of affiliated enterprises, institutions, and existing real estate; (7) other relevant matters (Article 4). The management organization of religious activity venues must fill out the annual inspection form and submit it to the competent authority (Article 6), which will conduct the inspection and sign off on the findings (Article 7). If the annual inspection results are “unsatisfactory,” they must be rectified within a specified time (Article 12). If the venue fails to accept the annual inspection or does not rectify within the specified time, the person in charge will be held accountable, and penalties will be applied to the venue, including warnings, cessation of activities, or cancellation of registration based on the severity of the situation. In particularly serious cases, it will be referred to the same-level people’s court for closure (Article 14). The venue management organization may apply for administrative reconsideration or file an administrative lawsuit against the administrative actions of the competent authority (Article 15).

In November 2004, Premier Wen Jiabao signed State Council Order No. 426, promulgating the Regulations on Religious Affairs, which officially came into effect on March 1, 2005. The drafting of the Regulations on Religious Affairs took six years and is the first comprehensive administrative regulation concerning religion in China, marking a new milestone in China’s religious legislative work.

Departmental Regulations of the State Council

  1. (國家宗教事務局政策法規司2000: 8-22; Chinese Law and Government 2000a: 69-74)

Departmental regulations refer to various administrative normative legal documents published by ministries and commissions under the State Council based on laws and administrative regulations of the State Council, also known as ministerial regulations.

In the 1990s, the departmental regulations related to religious affairs included: (1) Rules for the Implementation of Registration and Administration of Religious Social Bodies, issued by the State Administration for Religious Affairs and the Ministry of Civil Affairs in May 1991; (2) Registration Procedures for Venues for Religious Activities, issued by the State Administration for Religious Affairs in April 1994; (3) Method for the Annual Inspection of Places of Religious Activity, issued by the State Administration for Religious Affairs in July 1996; (4) Method for Employing Foreign Specialists to Religious Schools, issued by the State Administration for Religious Affairs, the State Foreign Expert Bureau, and the Public Security Bureau in November 1998.

Among them, the Rules for the Implementation of Registration and Administration of Religious Social Bodies were formulated based on the administrative regulations Regulations on the Registration and Administration of Social Organizations, issued in October 1989. The relevant regulations stipulate that social organizations accept “guidance from the relevant business management departments,” and all organizations must obtain “approval from the relevant business management departments” before applying for registration with the “registration management authority” (i.e., the Ministry of Civil Affairs at all levels). The “business management departments” and the “registration management authority” constitute the dual management system of the Chinese government over social organizations. For religious social organizations, the Bureau of Religious Affairs and the Ministry of Civil Affairs formulated corresponding registration management methods, applying the aforementioned dual management system to religious social organizations, meaning that religious organizations at all levels must first secure approval from the corresponding religious affairs departments (business management departments) before applying for registration with the Civil Affairs Department (registration management authority) (Articles 2 and 3). The establishment conditions include: (1) a group name, office address, and person in charge; (2) statutes that do not violate the constitution, laws, or regulations; (3) legal sources of income; (4) verifiable classics, doctrines, and regulations that conform to China’s existing religious history and do not contradict the organization’s statutes; (5) broad representation among organizing members (Article 4). Additionally, it stipulates: “No identical or similar religious social organization may be established within the same administrative area” (Article 7). In other words, registered religious organizations have a special monopolistic position, excluding competition from other similar organizations.

The Registration Procedures for Venues for Religious Activities and the Method for the Annual Inspection of Places of Religious Activity were issued primarily based on the provisions of Order No. 145, Registration Procedures for Venues for Religious Activities.

As for the Method for Employing Foreign Specialists to Religious Schools, it was formulated based on Order No. 144, Regulations on the Supervision of the Religious Activities of Foreigners in China, and the Interim Provisions on the Work of Foreign Cultural and Educational Experts, consisting of a total of 8 chapters and 36 articles: (1) General Principles; (2) Employment Principles; (3) Application and Approval for Employment Qualifications; (4) Conditions for Hired Personnel; (5) Reporting and Approval of Employed Personnel; (6) Management of Teaching Work of Foreign Specialists; (7) Legal Responsibilities; and (8) Supplementary Provisions. The provisions primarily target religious schools, stipulating that the hiring of long-term or short-term foreign specialists must follow prescribed application and reporting processes. These regulations also apply to professionals from Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan who give lectures (Article 33).

Comprehensive Local Religious Regulations

  1. (國家宗教事務局政策法規司2000: 24-121; 葉小文1998: 1; 2005: 7; Chinese Law and Government 2000b: 17-26)

    Local Religious Regulations

  1. 51-58)

Local regulations refer to the laws formulated and promulgated by the people’s congresses and their standing committees of provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities directly under the Central Government, based on the specific conditions and practical needs of their administrative regions, under the premise that they do not contradict different constitutions, laws, and administrative regulations. The regional people’s congresses and their standing committees in cities where provincial or autonomous region governments are located, or in larger cities approved by the State Council, may also formulate local regulations based on local conditions and practical needs, as long as they do not conflict with the constitution, laws, administrative regulations, and local regulations of the province or autonomous region.

In the 1990s, a total of 11 provincial and municipal people’s congresses passed local “Regulations on Religious Affairs (Management).” After the implementation of the administrative regulations “Regulations on Religious Affairs” in 2005, these regulations were amended.

The earliest local religious affairs regulations were issued in Shanghai, which defines “religious affairs” in Article 4 as “the public social affairs related to religion and its interactions with the state, society, and the masses.” In 1997, Hainan Province changed “the masses” to “citizens.” In the same year, Ye Xiaowen, the Director of the Bureau of Religious Affairs, defined “religious affairs” as follows: “Religion is not just a personal belief issue; it is a social entity involving social organizations (religious groups), social facilities (temples, monasteries, and churches), and social activities (religious activities involving large numbers of believers or other activities). Inevitably, this social entity will generate various religious affairs or social matters in relation to the entire society.” Therefore, “religious affairs” have become a form of “public social affairs,” referring to various relationships, behaviors, or activities concerning public interests that arise from religion as a social entity. Ye specifically pointed out that “because they involve public interests, these relationships, behaviors, or activities regarding religion must be regulated by law (that is, the rules of behavior guaranteed by the power of the state); because they involve public interests, the government must conduct administrative management of these relationships, behaviors, or activities in accordance with the law; and because they involve public interests, the management of religious affairs is not about interfering in normal religious activities and the internal affairs of religious organizations, nor is it about ‘separation of church and state’ or ‘officially-sponsored religions.’

It is noteworthy that among the 11 regions, over half (6) retained the term “Religious Affairs Management,” reflecting a strong “management-oriented” approach (even if the term “management” is not explicitly present in the name, it is essentially centered on management).

In terms of content, the regulations generally include: (1) General Principles; (2) Religious Organizations; (3) Religious Personnel; (4) Places of Religious Activities; (5) Religious Activities; (6) Religious Schools; (7) Religious Property; (8) Foreign Exchanges; (9) Legal Responsibilities; and (10) Supplementary Provisions.

Among them, except for Xinjiang, where no explicit definition is provided, the local “Regulations on Religious Affairs (Management)” mainly define “religion” as the five major religions: Buddhism, Taoism, Islam, Catholicism, and Christianity. Concerning “religious organizations,” this also points to patriotic religious organizations corresponding to the five major religions. The Heilongjiang Provincial regulations also include “Orthodoxy” and the “Harbin Church of the Chinese Orthodox Church.” This differs from the handling in Orders No. 144 and 145, which do not enumerate the “five major religions.”

Human Rights in China

  1. Human Rights in China, 1991 (中共中央文獻研究室綜合研究組、國務院宗教事務局政策法規司1995: 289-292)

In October 1991, the State Council Information Office released the white paper Human Rights in China, with the sixth section addressing “Citizens’ Freedom of Religious Belief.” The white paper stated that in China, it is difficult to estimate the number of people who believe in Buddhism and Taoism, while the total population practicing Islam is over 17 million. There are approximately 3.5 million and 4.5 million believers in Catholicism and Christianity, respectively. The Chinese constitution stipulates that citizens have freedom of religious belief, and the state protects normal religious activities and the legitimate rights and interests of religious organizations. The white paper reviewed the damage done to religious policies during the Cultural Revolution, but noted that as reforms and openings progressed, by the end of 1989, over 40,000 temples and places of worship had been opened. Since 1980, the central government has provided 140 million yuan in maintenance subsidies for temples and places of worship, and local governments at all levels have also allocated funds for the maintenance of these sites.

The white paper mentions that there are 8 national religious organizations, 164 provincial-level religious organizations, and over 2,000 county-level religious organizations in China. Since 1980, various religions have established 47 religious schools, graduating over 2,000 students and sending over 100 religious students abroad to 12 countries and regions. The total number of religious personnel is 200,000, of which nearly 9,000 are elected to various levels of the people’s congresses and political consultative conferences.

Regarding criminal acts utilizing religion, all those arrested were involved in subverting state power, destruction, endangering national security, inciting the masses to resist the enforcement of national laws and regulations, or severely disturbing social order. Others have been caught defrauding money under the guise of religion, endangering others’ physical and mental health, or enticing women; “not a single one was arrested for their religious beliefs.”

China’s religious policy promotes independence and self-administration, opposing foreign forces controlling and interfering in China’s internal religious affairs. The white paper specifically mentions that before 1949, Catholicism and Christianity were historically under the control of foreign religious powers. It further states that based on equality and mutual respect, the Chinese religious community has initiated international exchanges and relations. From 1955 to 1990, more than 11,000 Chinese Muslims made the pilgrimage to Mecca.

Tibet - Its Ownership and Human Rights Situation

  1. White Paper 1992: Tibet - Its Ownership and Human Rights Situation (中共中央文獻研究室綜合研究組、國務院宗教事務局政策法規司1995: 293-296)

In September 1992, the State Council Information Office published the white paper Tibet - Its Ownership and Human Rights Situation, in which the eighth section addresses “Freedom of Religious Belief.” The white paper emphasizes that Tibet has historically been a part of China, and under the governance of the Chinese government, the religious freedom of the Tibetan people is fully protected. The majority of people in Tibet practice Tibetan Buddhism, while there are approximately 2,000 practitioners of Islam and nearly 600 Catholics.

In Tibet, the government legally protects normal religious activities and customs, maintaining and restoring historical temples such as the Potala Palace, Drepung Monastery, and Sera Monastery. Furthermore, the government emphasizes that “freedom of religious belief” is a basic right protected by the Chinese constitution, allowing anyone to freely choose to believe in or not believe in a religion without state coercion. At the same time, the government opposes any activities aimed at division under the guise of religion, believing that the Dalai Lama and his supporters exploit religion to interfere with politics and undermine national unity.

The white paper also notes that before democratic reforms, religious beliefs in Tibet were distorted by the old system of theocratic integration, linking religion closely with the feudal serf system, with monks and nobles controlling significant social resources. After the democratic reforms in 1959, the serf system was abolished, and religion became a personal belief, allowing people to freely choose their beliefs without oppression. The government has also promoted the reform of temple management systems, normalizing the lives of monks and religious activities. In recent years, there have been illegal activities exploiting religion, yet “not a single one has been arrested or convicted due to their religious beliefs.”

Moreover, Tibet’s religious culture has been preserved, with Tibetan Buddhist scriptures protected and published. Academic research institutions are committed to the organization and development of Buddhist culture. The government also supports hosting Buddhism academic exchanges, allowing Tibetan Buddhism to interact with other religious cultures and promoting diverse development.

  1. The Progress of Human Rights in China, 1995 (國務院新聞辦公室1995)

In December 1995, the State Council Information Office published the white paper The Progress of Human Rights in China, which elaborates on the new advancements in China’s human rights endeavors over the four years since the release of Human Rights in China in November 1991.

In the second section, “Citizen’s Political Rights,” it discusses the state of freedom of religious belief: “Freedom of religious belief is protected by law in China. Various religions in China carry out normal activities according to the rights specified in the constitution and laws. Buddhism and Taoism are widely practiced in China, but the number of believers is not formally counted. Currently, there are over 9,500 Buddhist temples in the country, with around 170,000 monks; there are over 6,000 Taoist priests and priestesses residing in temples, with over 600 open Taoist temples. Approximately 17 million people practice Islam, with over 26,000 mosques; there are nearly 4 million Catholics, with 2,700 clergy and over 4,000 churches; about 6.5 million Christians with 18,000 pastoral staff, over 8,000 churches, and more than 20,000 informal meeting points. Religious organizations and religious affairs are not subject to foreign control, which is intended to safeguard the true rights of Chinese citizens to enjoy freedom of religious belief.

Freedom of Religious Belief in China

  1. White Paper: Freedom of Religious Belief in China, 1997 (國務院新聞辦公室1997; Chinese Law and Government 2003b: 7-20)

In October 1997, the State Council Information Office released the White Paper on the Freedom of Religious Belief in China, marking the first time the Chinese government publicly articulated its position and the current status of religious freedom to the international community. The document, comprising 10,000 words, is divided into five sections: (1) The Current State of Religion in China; (2) Legal Protection of Freedom of Religious Belief; (3) Judicial and Administrative Safeguards and Supervision of Religious Freedom; (4) Support for Independent and Self-Managed Religious Affairs; (5) Protection of Minority Groups’ Rights to Religious Freedom.

Firstly, regarding the current state of religion in China, the white paper describes China as a “religious country,” primarily adhering to Buddhism, Taoism, Islam, Catholicism, and Christianity. There are currently over 100 million adherents of various religions, with more than 85,000 places of religious activities and approximately 300,000 religious personnel. There are over 3,000 religious organizations, which also operate 74 religious schools to train religious personnel. In Christianity, there are around 4 million Catholics with about 4,000 clergy members, over 4,600 churches and meeting places; approximately 10 million Protestants with over 18,000 clergy and over 12,000 churches, in addition to more than 25,000 informal meeting points. It states that “all normal religious activities at places of worship and those conducted in believers’ homes according to religious customs” are managed by religious organizations and adherents, protected by law, and no one may interfere. All religions enjoy equal status and coexist harmoniously, with no religious conflicts reported; believers and non-believers show mutual respect and live in unity and harmony.

Secondly, the rights of Chinese citizens to freedom of religious belief are protected by the constitution and laws. In addition to the constitution, various laws (such as the Ethnic Regional Autonomy Law, Civil Code, Education Law, Labor Law, Compulsory Education Law, Electoral Law of the People’s Congress, Regulations on Village Committees, Advertising Law, etc.) establish that citizens are not to be discriminated against based on their religious beliefs. It also mentions Orders No. 144 and 145. The white paper asserts that the legal protection of citizens’ rights to freedom of religious belief in China is generally consistent with the major contents of relevant international instruments and conventions (such as the United Nations Charter, Universal Declaration of Human Rights, International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and the United Nations Declaration on the Elimination of All Forms of Intolerance and of Discrimination Based on Religion or Belief as well as the Vienna Declaration and Programme of Action). While citizens enjoy the right to freedom of religious belief, they must also meet the obligations stipulated by law and uphold the interests of the people, respect legal authority, maintain ethnic unity, promote national unity, and strive to adapt to socialist society in China. The Chinese government punishes cult organizations conducting illegal activities under the guise of religion and reaffirms that “no one in China is punished for their religious beliefs.”

Furthermore, regarding judicial safeguards, China has clear penal provisions for violations of citizens’ rights to freedom of religious belief. In terms of administrative safeguards, various levels of government in China have set up religious affairs departments, which do not interfere in the internal affairs of religious organizations and activity venues. Currently, religious organizations and places of religious activity must register with the government according to the law. “Christian believers conducting prayer and Bible study activities (known as ‘family meetings’ in Chinese) at home with family and friends according to religious customs do not require registration.”

The white paper reiterates China’s policy of independent and self-managed religion and points out that 19th-century Western Christianity and Catholicism served as tools of colonialism and imperialist aggression against China. The Chinese church became a dependency of Western missionary societies. It was not until after 1949, with the end of the semi-colonial and semi-feudal society, that historical conditions for achieving independence and self-management for Chinese Catholicism and Christianity were provided. Regarding Sino-Vatican relations, the white paper states that the Vatican must sever diplomatic ties with Taiwan and recognize the government of the People’s Republic of China as the sole legitimate government of China, and it must not interfere in China’s internal affairs in the name of religious matters.

Finally, the white paper affirms the Chinese government’s protection of the rights to religious freedom for ethnic minorities, specifically mentioning the situations in Tibet and Xinjiang. It reiterates a firm opposition to the use of religious fanaticism to divide the people, disrupt the nation, and harm the unity among ethnic groups, while resolutely opposing illegal activities and terrorism related to religion, thereby maintaining national unity and social stability in ethnic minority areas, as well as protecting the normal religious activities of minority believers.

Therefore, the white paper states: since the founding of the new China, especially in the nearly 20 years of reform and opening up, the human rights situation of the Chinese people has greatly improved, and the right to freedom of religious belief has also been fully respected and protected. The Chinese government will continue to make greater efforts in maintaining human rights, including the protection of religious freedom.

Legal Cases or Incidents Related to Religious Leaders or Organizations

  1. The Dama Station Church Incident (1990-1991) (羅旭旋 n.d.)

On February 22, 1990, at 11:30 PM, after an evangelical meeting at the Dama Station Family Church in Guangzhou, dozens of public security officers, armed with a search warrant, conducted a raid on the meeting place, confiscating microphones, tape recorders, Bibles, and hymnals used for the gathering, and taking the responsible person, Lin Xian’gao (Samuel Lamb, 1924-2013), away. According to the announcement from the government of the Yuexiu District of Guangzhou City, the church was cited for violating the Administrative Regulations for Religious Activity Venues in Guangdong Province by failing to register with the religious authorities, and Lin was accused of illegal preaching. The Dama Station was declared an illegal preaching site and shut down. Lin was released after being detained for 21 hours, during which he was primarily questioned about the unregistered status of the meeting site and accused of violating religious policies; not cooperating with the religious management imposed by the Christian “Two Associations”; and relying on foreign church funding, technology, and personnel, in violation of the principle of “Three-Self” (self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation). Lin defended himself by stating that the church abided by the law and did not violate national laws and regulations; the constitution provides for citizens’ freedom of religious belief, and there is no legal requirement to join “Three-Self” organizations. The church is capable of self-support without intervention from outsiders in its “autonomy.” Consequently, Lin refused to register and to cease gatherings. After his detention, the Dama Station conducted only prayer meetings until normal gatherings resumed in March 1991. From March 1990 to January 1991, he was interrogated multiple times. Subsequent interrogations were also numerous, focusing mainly on urging registration, studying documents, ideological education, and inquiries into interactions with the outside world and church activities.

  1. The Case of Pastor Gao Liangyi from the Dongguan Church in Shenyang (1994) (趙天恩、莊婉芳1997: 660-661; 林瑞琪1994: 2-3; Ngai & Chan 1994; Kwan 1994a, 1994b, 1994c, 1994d)

In 1994, Gao Liangyi (高良義) and Gao Peiman (高沛滿), pastors of the Dongguan Christian Church (Three-Self Church) in Shenyang, Liaoning Province, along with church members, sought to reclaim church property that had been occupied for many years. They discovered discrepancies in the church’s accounts and unexplained missing funds. In January, a group of believers from the church and students from Northeast Theological Seminary distributed flyers criticizing local Three-Self Association leader Lv Zhibin as a “false pastor.” They also implicated Lv and another senior pastor in the misuse of church funds alongside a local religious bureau official. On March 14 and 16, 7,688 church members voted to establish a temporary management committee for the Dongguan Church to audit the accounts. Because two of the involved people were members of the municipal political consultative conference, the public security authorities launched mass arrests, detaining 29 church members, including the two pastors on April 7. The 29 church members were released after 12 hours, but the two pastors were held for several months. It was reported that Bishop Ding Guangxun also attempted to intervene in the matter.

The incident drew significant media attention abroad, with extensive reporting from the Hong Kong newspaper South China Morning Post. The leftist Hong Kong newspaper Ta Kung Pao reported on October 7, 1994, that from October 1993 to February 1994, Gao Liangyi and Gao Peiman “exploited the discord among different religious sects within the Dongguan Church and inconsistencies regarding church property to incite and organize uninformed individuals to engage in illegal activities, personally attack and assault different old pastors and church workers, and infringe upon the legitimate rights and interests of religious activity venues, severely disrupting social order and causing a very bad impact.” Consequently, local judicial authorities processed Gao Liangyi and Gao Peiman according to the Regulations on the Management of Places of Religious Activities.

Finally, Gao Peiman was released on bail after the review period expired due to his status as a collaborator, while Gao Liangyi faced charges under Article 159 of the Criminal Law for obstructing official duties, with judicial authorities stating further investigations would follow. Article 159 of the Criminal Law states, “Disrupting public order in other public places…”.

Crackdown on ‘The Anointed King’ — Leader Wu Yangming Arrested (1995)

  1. (趙誌恩1996: 25-29; 梁家麟1999: 175-176)

‘The Anointed King’ was established in 1988 by Wu Yangming (吴扬明, 1944-1995) from Anhui. He became a Christian in 1979 and was scrutinized by the public security bureau in 1983 for participating in the ‘Shouting Sect’. In 1986, he underwent re-education and was later sentenced to one year in prison. The Wenzhou Church Demolition and Sealing Incident (1996-2000)

  1. (舍禾2015: 639-647)

In February 1996, the Zhejiang Province Spiritual Civilization Construction Committee issued a notification on anti-superstition efforts to halt the illegal construction of temples and churches and to manage unauthorized burial sites (浙文明〔1996〕1号), which was distributed by the Zhejiang Provincial Office and the Zhejiang Provincial Government. On February 28, the provincial party committee and government held a conference in Hangzhou, calling for strong opposition to superstition and the illegal construction of religious buildings.

In April, they convened a follow-up meeting in Hangzhou to receive reports on the special management efforts from various regions and to plan the next phase of these efforts, setting the stage for the church demolition events.

The Wenzhou municipal government actively cooperated with the provincial committee’s work, declaring a crackdown on cults, illegal gatherings, and illegal constructions that affected city planning. They pressured unregistered churches in Wenzhou to join the Three-Self Patriotic Movement while threatening to demolish churches. In April, Yongjia County proceeded to demolish several prayer sites, including Biliand Mountain and Qiaotou Prayer Mountain. Dongtou County also demolished a private house that served as a gathering place. The Zhenhai District initiated special “anti-illegality” efforts, demolishing a number of illegal and unauthorized churches. In Leqing County, numerous churches (such as Baishi Church) were inspected and closed. However, after being sealed for three months, congregants resumed their gatherings.

In March 1997, a report from the Zhenhai District government noted that under the special governance efforts in 1996, a total of 246 small temples and shrines had been demolished, along with three illegally constructed churches and 26,000 illegal tombs. In 1997, Wenzhou’s religious bureau urged the Shangdimen Church (Wenzhou’s largest family church) to join the Three-Self movement, but this attempt failed, and the church was subsequently demolished. The congregants then dispersed into five different locations for worship, which had grown to over 16 meeting points by 2010. In the same year, the Bilian Fengche House Church in Yongjia was destroyed.

At the end of 1999, Wenzhou initiated another round of “anti-illegality” measures. The Zhenhai District Religious Affairs Bureau described that “anti-illegality” was aimed at areas previously demolished that had resurfaced and required strong consolidation of results. In November 2000, Wenzhou launched a new round of “rectification of illegal religious activities”, aimed at addressing issues such as “an excess of religious activity sites and unreasonable layouts” and “illegal preaching,” targeting unregistered religious venues for sealing and demolition.

As a result, the Chengjiao Bridge Church in the suburbs, along with properties associated with the churches in the South Gate area, West Mountain area, City Center, and Shuomen area, were destroyed. Six churches were bombed from Ximen’s Ping Tian Mountain Church to the foot of Dongfeng Mountain, many of which were prayer mountains. In Yongjia County, the Lingtou Church, Xialong Church, Diaopian Church, Jincang Church, Gaoshan Church, Tangshang Church, and Minglong Church in the Bilian area were all destroyed. Additionally, some churches were repurposed for public use; for example, 22 churches and superstitious sites in Yongjia County were converted into administrative village offices and rural community cultural activity centers.

According to a December 2000 report by the Hong Kong-based China Democracy Movement Human Rights Information and News Summary, Wenzhou (which includes two cities, six counties, and three districts) has over 8,000 religious sites, of which only 3,200 were registered (1,200 Christian churches, 120 Catholic churches, and the rest being temples and Taoist temples), leaving more than 5,000 unregistered, including over 400 Christian and Catholic churches. Since the end of 1999, Pingyang County had closed and demolished 110 sites in November, reaching a total of 454 by December. By the end of December, at least 3,011 “illegal religious sites” had been demolished in Wenzhou (611 in Yongjia County, 540 in Cangnan County, 470 in Leqing County, 454 in Pingyang County, 356 in Rui’an County, 262 in Zhenhai District, 158 in Lucheng District, 85 in Longwan District, and 76 in Dongtou County).

The number of churches demolished in Wenzhou from 1996 to 2001 represented the most severe and large-scale occurrences since the Cultural Revolution.

Since the reform and opening up, folk beliefs that were once labeled as “feudal superstitions” by the Chinese government have gradually revived. In the 1980s, the Chinese government still attempted to ban various forms of “feudal superstitions,” but after the 1990s, the “folk belief boom” became an unavoidable social reality in both rural and urban areas. With the “rebirth” of folk beliefs, the contradictions and conflicts between them and Christianity have become a significant phenomenon of concern in rural society.

In a master’s thesis from the Central Party School of the Communist Party of China, using Zhangzhuang Village in Dongming County, Lushi Nan as a case study, it was pointed out that in the 1990s, the Landlord God Temple and the Guandi Temple, which had been demolished during the Cultural Revolution, were successively rebuilt. Apart from folk beliefs and ancestor worship, there was also a Christian church in the village with 260 believers and a Catholic church with 60 believers. As folk beliefs developed in the village, villagers who practiced Christianity faced discrimination from other villagers for not worshiping ancestors or visiting graves, being labeled as “disrespectful to ancestors and unfilial.” The thesis reflects the contradictions between Christian doctrine and traditional customs (the “ritual dispute”).

The aforementioned “ritual dispute” also occurred in Henan. A scholar conducting research in Y County pointed out a very important part of local traditional funerals—shrouding the corpse. Filial children, relatives, and friends within five degrees of the deceased would participate in this ritual, with “washing the face” and “opening the path” being the most significant. The “washing the face” ritual involves the deceased’s daughter cleaning the seven orifices of the deceased, aiming to ensure the deceased can see and hear clearly in the afterlife and articulate well, while praying for the deceased during the cleaning. For instance, while cleaning the eyes, one might say: “Dad (Mom), I’m making your eyes bright, the road in the afterlife is dark and hard to navigate, you need to open your eyes wide to see the path clearly and avoid tripping.” “Opening the path” involves the daughter combing her hair with a peach wood comb and saying: “Dad (Mom), I’m opening the path for you, you should walk in the middle of the three roads, don’t take the wrong one!” Only after these two rituals can the coffin be sealed. When an elderly Christian believer with a son and a daughter passed away, the non-Christian son and relatives insisted on holding a traditional funeral, requiring the Christian daughter to perform “washing the face” and “opening the path,” or else they would not seal the coffin. At this moment, the tension between Christianity and Chinese traditional culture reached its peak. Ultimately, it resolved with the compromise of the Christian daughter. However, while performing the traditional rituals, the daughter secretly incorporated Christian elements. She prayed: “Thank you, Lord, in the name of the Lord, Dad, I’m opening the path for you, you should walk on the road to heaven. Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. Post-Tiananmen: From Containment of China to Engagement Policy (Garver 2016: 528-556, 634-673; Lampton 2001: 15-49, 111-155; 蔡瑋 1994: 28-38)

After the “June Fourth” massacre, Western countries adopted a hardline sanctions policy towards China, particularly the United States and the European Community, which implemented economic, military, and diplomatic restrictions on China. The United States immediately suspended high-level military exchanges with China, froze military sales, and passed the Tiananmen Sanctions Act at the end of 1989, imposing strict controls on military technology imports and exports to China. Additionally, the U.S. Congress began annual reviews of China’s Most Favored Nation (MFN) status in early 1990, attaching human rights conditions in an attempt to force Beijing to improve its human rights situation through trade policy. On the European side, the European Community announced an arms embargo against China. These measures reflected the Western countries’ attempts to pressure China into improving its human rights situation through containment and sanctions.

However, with the end of the Cold War and changes in the global economic landscape, the United States and other Western countries gradually shifted their policies towards China, moving from initial containment and sanctions to what is termed an engagement policy. The primary reason for the U.S. government’s adjustment of its China policy was the belief that maintaining economic ties with China and promoting its integration into the international system would better facilitate long-term political reform and economic liberalization in China. In 1993, President Bill Clinton temporarily tied improvements in China’s human rights record to the continuation of China’s MFN status, but the following year decided to uncouple human rights from trade, advocating instead for promoting internal reforms in China through economic relations. This decision was partly influenced by pressure from the American business community, as many large multinational companies believed that sustaining economic cooperation with China was more aligned with U.S. long-term interests than sanctions.

Moreover, geopolitical considerations were also an important factor in the U.S. changing its policy towards China. After the end of the Cold War, the U.S. began to view China as an indispensable part of the global economic system, rather than merely as an ideological opponent. In 1997, the Clinton administration officially proposed a Strategic Partnership, seeking to establish a stable cooperative framework with China and encouraging it to further open its markets and participate in international rule-making. This policy ultimately led to the U.S. Congress passing the U.S.-China Relations Act in 2000, granting China Permanent Normal Trade Relations (PNTR) and paving the way for China’s accession to the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001.

On the other hand, in 1992, 邓小平’s southern tour had a profound impact on China’s economic reform process and indirectly facilitated the shift of Western countries from the containment and sanctions policy following June Fourth to an engagement policy. After the tour, China’s market-oriented reforms deepened further, and its rapid economic growth made the U.S. business community recognize the potential of the Chinese market. Multinational corporations, chambers of commerce, and lobbying groups (such as the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the National Committee on U.S.-China Relations) pressured Washington to maintain stable trade relations, ultimately influencing the Clinton administration’s decision in 1994 to unlink human rights from MFN status. In the face of the rising importance of the Chinese economy, the U.S. and other Western countries, considering diplomatic and economic interests, gradually relaxed trade restrictions on China in the mid-1990s, transitioning to encouraging China’s integration into the global economic system.

The transition from Western containment of China to an engagement policy reflects changes in the international political and economic environment. Although Western countries attempted to force China to make concessions through hardline measures in the years following June Fourth, over time, economic realities, corporate interests, and China’s growing global influence led the U.S. and other countries to adopt a more pragmatic approach. This engagement policy brought about China’s economic rise but also sparked a long-term debate on whether economic openness genuinely promoted China’s political liberalization.

After 1989, the upheaval in Eastern Europe and the dissolution of the Soviet Union posed a significant challenge to the rule of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). The CCP placed great importance on the changes in Eastern Europe, viewing them as a severe political crisis and warning, and conducted in-depth analysis to ensure the stability of its own regime.

The CCP’s fundamental understanding was that the changes in Eastern Europe did not signify a failure of socialism itself, but rather were due to improper economic reforms, political laxity, Western infiltration, and ideological confusion. Especially regarding the Soviet Union, the CCP believed that Gorbachev’s “new thinking” and political openness led to a split within the Communist Party and ultimately a loss of control over the state. Therefore, the CCP emphasized “never following the Soviet path,” adhering to the “Four Stresses,” and politically underscoring “the party’s leadership,” while strengthening the party’s control over the military, ideology, and society to prevent the growth of dissenting forces. Concurrently, patriotic education was reinforced, and the Western strategy of “peaceful evolution” was criticized to maintain social stability.

The CCP also drew economic lessons from the failures in Eastern Europe, believing that ensuring economic growth and improving people’s living standards were crucial for consolidating power. Thus, it further promoted “reform and opening up,” developing a market economy while maintaining party control. In 1992, Deng Xiaoping’s southern tour emphasized that “development is the hard truth,” establishing a socialist market economy with Chinese characteristics that attracted foreign investment while preserving political stability.

In September 1990, Jiang Zemin pointed out that after the upheaval in Eastern Europe, many communists around the world began to doubt the future of socialism, with some even losing confidence. However, he firmly believed that socialism was currently at a “low tide” but was not “collapsing.” He argued that by focusing on speeding up economic development, the superiority of the socialist system could be demonstrated. In 1991, he stated that ideological work would directly affect the success or failure of socialism, necessitating “political struggle” and “theoretical struggle” while adhering to the leadership of the Communist Party and the socialist path.

Additionally, the role of religion in the fall of communist regimes in Eastern Europe was also a point of attention.In April 1990, Chinese Communist Party elder Chen Yun wrote a letter to Jiang Zemin expressing his deep concern about the recent increasingly serious issue of “religious infiltration,” particularly the rampant “counter-revolutionary activities disguised under the cloak of religion in the new situation.” He pointed out that “using religion to compete for the masses, especially the youth,” was “a painful lesson from the loss of power by certain communist-led countries.” He urged the central leadership to seriously “address this major issue” so that it would not become “a new factor of instability.”

In 1992, Xu Ruilei from Jinling Union Theological Seminary, in an article titled “How to Correctly Recognize the Role of Religion in the Dramatic Changes in Eastern Europe,” stated that the main causes of the upheaval were “internal factors,” namely “the decline in the quality of the party” and “long-standing unresolved economic and ethnic issues.” He argued that the relationship between religion and government in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union had been “relatively coordinated,” and thus, religion was not the “foundation for liberalization” or the “breeding ground for peaceful evolution.” Of course, he also acknowledged that religious institutions played certain roles in this transformation; for example, the Nikolaikirche in Leipzig became a “catalyst” for subsequent protests, but that could not be used to claim that religion was “the mother of revolution,” at most it was just a “midwife.”

In December 2001, at the National Religious Work Conference, Jiang Zemin delivered a speech titled “On Religious Issues,” stating that “during the process of the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the upheaval in Eastern Europe, international hostile forces have utilized religion. After the Cold War, these international hostile forces have intensified their use of ethnic and religious issues to implement a political strategy of Westernization and fragmentation against our country.” Therefore, “in the current domestic and international situation, the Party’s leadership over religious work and the government’s management of religious affairs can only be strengthened, not weakened.” Jiang believed that “one cannot use religious belief freedom and separation of religion and state as an excuse to abandon or shirk the state’s management of religious affairs,” and that “using religion to oppose the Party’s leadership and the socialist system, undermining national unity and the solidarity of various ethnic groups, is absolutely not allowed. Using religion to harm the interests of the state and society and obstructing the legitimate rights of other citizens is also absolutely not allowed.” “In these years of international struggle, hostile forces often use religious issues to attack us. They have intensified their infiltration and destructive activities through religion, trying to undermine the leadership of the Communist Party of China and our socialist state.” “Some overseas Christian groups have increased their efforts to evangelize in our country. Now the methods and means of religious infiltration have diversified; many religious organizations use radio broadcasts and the Internet for propaganda and covertly send religious books and audiovisual materials by smuggling and mailing, disguising the dispatch of missionaries under the guise of tourism, investment, economic cooperation, and cultural exchange while supporting the establishment of secret religious organizations and underground churches, among other activities. We must maintain a high level of vigilance regarding these trends and take practical preventive measures to ensure we do not be swayed by minor interests and forget greater principles.”

Overall, the upheaval in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union prompted the CCP to be alert to the crisis of its regime and to respond to challenges by strengthening the leadership of the party, promoting economic development, and strictly controlling ideology, thus avoiding the pitfalls faced by Eastern Europe and ensuring the long-term rule of the Communist Party. At the same time, the CCP’s tightening control over religious affairs in the 1990s cannot be separated from the backdrop of the Eastern European upheaval.

In the Xi Jinping era, the relationship between the events in Eastern Europe and religion has again drawn China’s attention. In 2015, Duan Dezhiz published the book “Research on Overseas Religious Infiltration and the Upheaval in Eastern Europe,” indicating that the “sacred alliance” between the United States and the Vatican was an important reason for religious infiltration in Eastern Europe, thereby necessitating that China strengthen its defenses against overseas religious infiltration.

  1. The Handover of Sovereignty over Hong Kong and Macao and “One Country, Two Systems” (王宏志 2000)

On July 1, 1997, and December 20, 1999, the People’s Republic of China reclaimed sovereignty over Hong Kong and Macao, respectively, establishing the Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions and ending years of colonial rule by the United Kingdom and Portugal. This marked an important milestone in China’s realization of national unity at the end of the 20th century. The handover of sovereignty had a profound impact on the construction of Chinese nationalism, particularly in aspects such as the narrative of “great unity” and the practice of “One Country, Two Systems.”

Since the 1842 Treaty of Nanking, which ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain, there have been multiple treaties changing the status of the area, including the Treaty of Beijing in 1860 and the Hong Kong Convention for the Extension of Territory in 1898, which divided Hong Kong into the permanently ceded Hong Kong Island, Kowloon Peninsula, and the New Territories on a 99-year lease. In 1984, China and the United Kingdom signed the Sino-British Joint Declaration, confirming that Hong Kong would “return” to China in 1997 and implement “One Country, Two Systems” without change for fifty years.In Macao, the Treaty of Friendship, Trade, and Navigation signed between China and Portugal in 1887 granted Portugal “permanent administrative rights” over Macao. However, after the establishment of diplomatic relations between China and Portugal in 1979, discussions regarding the transfer of Macao’s sovereignty began, ultimately leading to the signing of the Sino-Portuguese Joint Declaration in 1987, confirming that Macao would “return” to China in 1999.

The handover of sovereignty over Hong Kong and Macao has been shaped by the official Chinese narrative as a significant event that has “cleansed” the “national humiliation” of the modern era, aligning with the nationalist discourse of “great unity.” Since the Opium War, the invasions by Western powers and the unequal treaties have led to significant territorial losses for China, and since the founding of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, the CCP has continuously emphasized the historical mission of “achieving national unity.” The “return” of Hong Kong and Macao is regarded as an important achievement in this process, allowing Chinese nationalists to emphasize the historical legitimacy of the CCP regime in realizing national rejuvenation.

“One Country, Two Systems” is the political framework proposed by China during the reclamation of sovereignty over Hong Kong and Macao, allowing both regions to retain their existing capitalist systems for 50 years without change. This policy is not only aimed at ensuring the economic stability of Hong Kong and Macao but also forms part of Chinese nationalism—showcasing China’s ability to embrace diverse systems while achieving national unity. The concept of “One Country, Two Systems” is also seen as a model for China’s “peaceful reunification” with Taiwan. For the CCP, Taiwan, which awaits “returning to the motherland,” becomes the final goal in realizing the “great unity” of the Chinese nation.

Regarding Hong Kong, in April 1990, the Third Session of the Seventh National People’s Congress promulgated the Basic Law, proposing the “Three Principles” of “mutual independence, non-interference, and mutual respect” as the basis for exchanges between religious organizations in Hong Kong and the mainland. According to the Basic Law, “Hong Kong residents enjoy freedom of religious belief, the freedom to preach publicly, and the freedom to participate in religious activities.” Additionally, “religious organizations may continue to operate religious schools, other schools, hospitals, and welfare institutions as well as provide other social services according to existing methods,” and they “may maintain and develop relations with religious organizations and believers from other regions,” while “schools operated by religious organizations may continue to provide religious education, including offering religious courses.”

  1. Patriotism and Nationalism (人民日报 1994; 张江明 1994: 18; Dikötter 2022: 229-232)

The 1990s were a period of deepening economic reform in China, with a gradual shift from a planned economy to a market economy, resulting in significant changes to the social structure. This also brought ideological challenges, such as the weakening of socialist beliefs, increased social differentiation, and the impact of Western culture and values. After June Fourth, faced with the upheaval in Eastern Europe and the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the CCP realized the need to stabilize the domestic political environment, consolidate the position of official ideology, and shape national consciousness through patriotic education and the cultivation of socialist spiritual civilization.

In August 1994, the Central Committee of the CCP issued the “Outline for the Implementation of Patriotic Education” drafted by the Central Propaganda Department, requiring localities to earnestly implement it. The “Outline” pointed out that “patriotism is a historical category,” thus “in contemporary China, patriotism and socialism are essentially the same.” The 21st issue of the journal Qiushi in 1994 further quoted Deng Xiaoping, stating, “Some say that not loving socialism does not mean not loving the country. Is the motherland abstract? If one does not love the socialist New China led by the Communist Party, what can one love?”

At the Sixth Plenary Session of the 14th Central Committee held in October 1996, a resolution on several important issues regarding the construction of socialist spiritual civilization was passed. Within this, patriotism again became the focus of spiritual civilization construction: “Patriotism has always been a banner under which the Chinese people unite and struggle. In contemporary China, patriotism is organically unified with socialism in the great practice of building socialism with Chinese characteristics, serving as a powerful motivation to inspire the entire nation to achieve national rejuvenation.” The core of patriotic education is to help people realize that “only socialism can save China, and only socialism can develop China’s truth,” and to build and defend the “socialist motherland” with national spirit. National interests, national dignity, ethnic unity, national reunification, and national security became the main contents of patriotic education in the new era.

Against this backdrop, in May 1999, NATO aircraft mistakenly bombed the Chinese embassy in Yugoslavia, resulting in the deaths of three Chinese journalists and injuring dozens more. The incident sparked intense outrage in Chinese society and became one of the most fervent expressions of nationalist sentiment in the 1990s. Following the incident, massive protests erupted in cities such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, with tens of thousands of students and citizens surrounding the U.S. embassy, shouting anti-American slogans, and throwing stones while burning items.The Chinese government initially allowed, and even encouraged, the protests, but later emphasized “rational patriotism” and called for calm among the public in order to manage the situation. As for Chinese state media, they characterized the incident as a “deliberate provocation by the United States,” claiming that the bombing was a meticulously planned act of war aimed at hindering China’s economic development and suppressing its rise. Subsequently, U.S. President Bill Clinton apologized to the Chinese government and expressed “deep condolences” to the victims and their families; however, the official narrative continued to accuse the United States, using this to stir public dissatisfaction and reinforce the narrative of “Western hostile forces suppressing China.” Jiang Zemin even proclaimed, “The Chinese nation cannot be bullied!”

Under the construction of the official narrative, the incident became framed as a concerted effort by the hostile capitalist camp to destroy the rising socialist China and realize ambitions of “fragmentation” and “Westernization” of the country. The fact that the U.S. president personally called Jiang Zemin to apologize was depicted by Chinese media as “a heavy blow to the arrogance of American hegemony.” Officials affirmed that the incident “further strengthened national cohesion,” with “a vast number of cadres and the masses gaining a clearer understanding of the hypocrisy of the ‘human rights,’ ‘humanitarianism,’ ‘freedom,’ and ‘democracy’ that the United States and the West proclaim.”

Undoubtedly, the incident made nationalism a more significant political force within Chinese society. After 1999, the Chinese government placed greater emphasis on safeguarding national sovereignty and dignity, further combining nationalism with patriotism to form the mainstream ideology of “sovereignty supremacy and national rejuvenation.”
