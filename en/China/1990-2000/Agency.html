China: Agency (1990-2000)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Han Wenzao (Wang Rongwei 2006: 12-14; Shen De Rong 2006: 19-20; Zhao Zhi En 2006: 18-21; Amity Foundation 2006: 13-16)

Han Wenzao (韓文藻, 1923-2006), born in Shanghai, attended the Huadong Public School run by the Shanghai Municipal Council. In 1940, he enrolled in the Civil Engineering Department of Zhejiang University and was baptized as a Christian the same year. After the Pacific War, he transferred to St. John’s University in Shanghai. After graduating in 1944, he served as a training officer in the Student Department of the Shanghai YMCA, and after a year of training, was assigned to work at the YMCA in Nanjing. In 1951, he became a member of the Christian Three-Self Reform Committee and also served as the General Secretary of the Nanjing Three-Self Reform Movement Promotion Association, later becoming the General Secretary and Chairman of the Nanjing Three-Self Patriotic Association, playing a key role in the Nanjing Three-Self Movement. Additionally, he served as the Vice Chairman and Secretary-General of the Jiangsu Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee and from 1961, was the Chairman of the Board of the Jinling Union Theological Seminary and the Office Director.

In 1980, he was elected as the Deputy Secretary-General of the National Three-Self and Deputy General Secretary of the National Christian Association during the Third National Christian Conference, as well as the Chairman of the Jiangsu Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee and President of the Jiangsu Christian Association. In 1991, he became the Vice President of the China Christian Association and Vice Chairman of the Jiangsu Political Consultative Conference. From 1997 to 2002, he succeeded the retired Bishop Ding Guangxun as President of the China Christian Association and was also a member of the Standing Committee of the National Political Consultative Conference and Vice Chairman of the Jiangsu Political Consultative Conference.

In 1985, Han Wenzao assisted Bishop Ding Guangxun in founding China’s first non-governmental organization initiated by Christians, the Amity Foundation, and served as Vice Chairman and Secretary-General for a long time, leading the Amity Foundation to actively introduce resources from overseas churches for charitable causes in China, such as education, healthcare, social welfare, and rural development. Later, Han Wenzao, along with Bishop Ding Guangxun, collaborated with the United Bible Societies to establish Amity Printing Co., Ltd., prioritizing the printing of Bibles for Chinese churches.

After the reform and opening-up, Han Wenzao was actively involved in and responsible for the foreign affairs work of the National Two Sessions of Christianity, adhering to the principle of independent management of churches and summarizing the six-character principle of “legal, open, and honest” for foreign affairs work. He worked hard to facilitate visits to the World Council of Churches by the National Two Sessions of Christianity in 1983 and 1989, and he helped the China Christian Association become a member of the World Council of Churches in 1991. Han defended the situation of religious freedom in China, citing the significant increase in the number of Chinese Christians since the end of the Cultural Revolution. He criticized the U.S. State Department’s 1999 International Religious Freedom Report for inconsistencies with facts regarding China and, during the visit of the Chinese religious leaders’ delegation to the U.S. in 2000, he stated that foreign believers who come to China to preach without an invitation from the China Christian Association or provincial associations are violating Chinese law.

Han Wenzao actively participated in activities promoting world peace and served as the Vice Chairman and Secretary-General of the Chinese Religious Peace Committee, Executive Committee member of the Asian Religious Peace Conference, and Council member of the International Council of the World Religious Peace Conference, actively engaging in international activities that promote world peace, such as the Asian Religious Peace Conference and the United Nations Millennium Peace Conference for Religious and Spiritual Leaders.

He passed away on February 3, 2006, at the age of 83.

  1. Luo Guanzong (Fu Xianwei 2011: 6-7; Li Pingye 2015: 48-50; Shen De Rong 2011: 19-21; Amity Foundation 2011: 20)

Luo Guanzong (羅冠宗, K. Z. Loh, 1920-2011), from Gao Ming, Guangdong, attended the affiliated high school of Guanghua University in Shanghai and graduated from the Political Science Department of Fudan University in Shanghai in 1943. During his time at school, he participated in the Christian Student Fellowship and accepted the Christian faith, being baptized at the Shanghai Anglican Church of the Savior in 1942. After graduation, he joined the Shanghai YMCA, serving in various positions including Student Officer, Chief Officer, Deputy General Secretary, and General Secretary. In 1945, he initiated student relief work at the Shanghai YMCA, serving as the Executive Secretary of the Shanghai Student United Relief Committee, establishing student economic canteens in 14 colleges and universities utilizing UN disaster relief supplies, providing free lunches to underprivileged students. During the Chinese Civil War, he engaged in the students’ “Anti-Hunger, Anti-Civil War” democratic movement, working among students and young professionals.

After the initiation of the Three-Self Movement, he became a key participant. In 1951, he was appointed as a member of the Christian Three-Self Reform Committee.From 1953 to 1954, Shanghai held two sessions of the “Three-Self Reform Cadre Study Class,” where he served as the Head of the Education Group. In 1954, he became a standing committee member of the China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee. In 1956, he was appointed Secretary-General of the Shanghai Three-Self Organization. During the Cultural Revolution, Luo Guanzong was labeled as a “power faction” by the rebels and was subjected to concentrated study sessions.

After the Cultural Revolution, Luo Guanzong participated in the “Policy Implementation Review” of the Shanghai Political Consultative Conference, serving as the Deputy Leader of the Religious Group. From 1981 to 1992, he was the Chairman of the Shanghai Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee, later becoming Honorary Chairman. He served as the Vice Chairman of the China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee from 1980 to 1997 (also serving as Secretary-General from 1992 to 1997), and as Chairman from 1997 to 2002. From 2002 to 2008, he was the Director of the Advisory Committee of the China Christian Two Associations, and after 2008, he served as the Honorary Director of the Advisory Committee.

During his chairmanship of the national Three-Self organization, he proposed the “Four Musts” to address the internal connection between “adhering to the Three-Self principle” and “running the church well”: (1) must adhere to independence and autonomy; (2) must be patriotic and love the faith; (3) must strengthen unity; (4) must implement the Three Goods (self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation).

In addition to the Three-Self Organization, Luo Guanzong represented the Shanghai YMCA in various committees such as the Democratic Youth Federation of Shanghai after the founding of the People’s Republic of China, and in 1953 was elected as the Deputy Chairman and Secretary-General. In 1966, he was appointed Vice Chairman of the All-China Youth Federation. He served on the standing committees of Shanghai’s Municipal Political Consultative Conference from its first to fifth sessions, as well as in vice-secretary roles from the third to seventh sessions and Director of the Office during the sixth to eighth sessions. Beginning in the 1970s, he was a member of the National Political Consultative Conference for the fifth and sixth sessions and a standing committee member for the seventh to ninth sessions. Furthermore, he held positions as a director of the China International Exchange Association and a board member of the Amity Foundation.

Luo Guanzong authored and edited two volumes of “Selected Works of the China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement” (1993, 2006) to discuss the Three-Self Movement. To explain how imperialism used Christianity to invade China, he edited “Lessons from the Past: A Review of the Historical Facts of Imperialism’s Use of Christianity to Invade China” (2003).

He passed away on February 13, 2011, at the age of 91.

  1. Yang Yidong (Yang Yidong 2002; Li Yading n.d.; UPI 1994; Kwan 1994; Kohut 1994; Associated Press 1994)

Yang Yidong (楊毓東, 1920-1999) was born into a Christian family in Liaoyang, Liaoning. Influenced by his cousin at the age of 17, he began to fervently pursue his faith. He enrolled at Shenyang Theological Seminary in 1938 and graduated in 1941, later serving as a pastor at a church in Lishu County, Siping. A year later, he entered the Northeast Theological Seminary run by the Scottish Presbyterian Church for further studies. After the Pacific War, foreign teachers and the dean of the seminary were detained in camps, and the school was maintained by Chinese teachers. After the war, Yang had to suspend his studies due to tuberculosis and graduated in June 1948.

During the Chinese Civil War, Yang Yidong was assigned to a church in Fushun. Before the “liberation” of Northeast China, he was sent in August 1948 to the American North Presbyterian Church in Beiping (Peking), but was soon dismissed due to the changing situation. After the founding of the People’s Republic of China, Yang Yidong served as a pastor at the New Street Church of the China Christian Church, where under his leadership, the congregation grew from 30 to 130 members. The church faced difficulties due to ongoing political movements in the 1950s. In July 1958, Yang Yidong was labeled as a “rightist” and was arrested that year, sentenced to three years of labor re-education. He was taken to “Beidahuang” (Northeast reclamation area) and built a labor camp farm in Baichengzi, Jilin, only returning to Beijing on Christmas of the same year.

After the Cultural Revolution began in 1966, Yang Yidong was frequently criticized and soon was sent back to his hometown in rural Liaoyang as a “counter-revolutionary element,” where he farmed for over a decade until 1979 when he returned to Beijing to reunite with his family. In July 1980, the church in Gangwashi was reopened; in 1982, Yang was fully rehabilitated. In April 1986, he was ordained as a pastor at Gangwashi Church by the two associations in Beijing, and a year later he became the senior pastor. In 1988, Yang supported Ding Guangxun’s reforms to streamline relations, but he divergent with others in the Beijing Two Associations who opposed the abolition of the Three-Self policy. After the June Fourth Incident, youth gatherings at Gangwashi Church flourished, with many expressing a desire to serve.

In 1991, the Beijing Two Associations planned to end the Korean ethnic group service at Gangwashi Church and relocate it to Chongwenmen Church, but they faced opposition from the Korean congregants. The Religious Affairs Bureau requested Yang to comply, and in September he was briefly asked to resign. Subsequently, another pastor (Yu Xinli) was assigned to Gangwashi Church to constrain Yang.Yang’s conflicts with the municipal two associations and the Religious Affairs Bureau also involved issues of church governance and autonomy. In April 1993, the Beijing two associations decided to remove Yang from his position as senior pastor due to him reaching the age of 70, replacing him with Yu Xinli. However, with the support of the church council and congregants, Yang firmly refused and fought for his position alongside them. In August 1993, the management committee of Gangwashi Church submitted a letter of support addressed to the Beijing Christian Two Associations. As a result, Yang was accused of “anti-government” and “anti-Three-Self” offenses. Ultimately, in December 1994, during a church gathering, officials from the Religious Affairs Bureau and Three-Self leaders, protected by police, took action to seize control, leading to Yang Yidong’s removal from authority, with Yu Xinli taking over. The struggle of Gangwashi Church’s congregants received extensive coverage from overseas media.

After being forced to leave Gangwashi Church, Yang took a three-month break before deciding to establish a house church and an underground seminary, focusing on preaching and training young believers. He was diagnosed with liver cancer in July 1998, passing away on June 20, 1999, at the age of 80.

  1. Cui Quan (Yin Yingqiao 2014: 227-235; Vala 2017: 159-166)

Cui Quan (崔權, 1965-), of Korean ethnicity, was born in Yanji City, Jilin, and is a fourth-generation Christian; his mother was a pastor in a Three-Self church. However, Cui did not accept Christianity until after the June Fourth Incident in 1989, when he began actively seeking God. Yet, after attending church for over three months, he still had not truly encountered God. In August 1993, following a traffic accident that resulted in a double fracture of his spine, his mother prayed for him in the hospital, saying, “It’s okay, this is a good thing; through this, you can return to God.” At that moment, Cui’s legs felt numb and painless. That day he experienced God, prompting him to contemplate life: “If that accident had been a head-on collision, I would have died. What happens after death? In that moment, where would I go after dying? After 29 years, has my life held any meaning? Is my life a success or a failure?” Confined to bed for two months, he pondered these questions, began listening to sermons and hymns, and found comfort.

In November 1993, he met a Korean-American missionary and officially made his commitment. In early 1994, Cui enrolled in a “Pastor Training Center” for a year of equiping. In 1995, he engaged in campus ministry in Changchun. Six months later, he passed the entrance exam for the Northeast Theological Seminary in Shenyang. While studying, he felt that what he learned differed from what he had previously encountered with missionaries. He later discovered officials from the Religious Affairs Bureau teaching at the seminary, emphasizing the need to build large churches under government guidance and cooperate with the government, leading him to feel discontent with the Three-Self churches. During his time at seminary, he visited different churches and came to understand the distinctions between Three-Self churches and house churches. Upon graduation in 1999, he felt compelled to leave traditional churches and the Three-Self system to serve in house churches. Thus, he arrived in Shanghai to pastor a fellowship of 17 members, all North Korean migrants working in the city, deciding to transform the fellowship into a church named “Shanghai Korean Church.”

The church initially held meetings in a factory in Pudong and faced relocation a year later. Realizing that there were more Korean people in Puxi than in Pudong, he decided to move the church there. About six months later, he found an old, closed hotel on Qixin Road and renovated its former discotheque into a meeting hall. The church began with over 90 attendees and later noticed Han Chinese participating in the gatherings. Though they did not understand Korean, they were attracted by the worship atmosphere, so Cui decided to start Chinese meetings. Each Sunday, there would be separate services for Korean and Han congregants, and the church was renamed “Shanghai Wanbang Missionary Church (萬邦宣教教會, Universal Mission Church, abbreviated as Wanbang Church).”

Two years later, the church faced another relocation due to demolition, so they rented land on Wuzhai Road to build a new church, where they began holding services in June 2003, with attendance increasing to 300 people. In July 2005, Cui participated in a “Building Healthy Churches” pastor training at the Busan Suwang Church in South Korea, followed by a conference in August. After the speakers laid hands on him, Pastor Cui began to receive a baptism of the Holy Spirit. By the end of December, the church hosted a “Holistic Healing Retreat.” In June 2006, he led the church to establish cell groups, and members of the groups began to experience the power of the Holy Spirit. From the beginning, the church experienced rapid growth. By 2009, there were approximately 3,000 registered members with about 1,500 attending services, making it one of the fastest-growing urban house churches in China.(5) Jin Tianming (Yin Yingqiao 2013: 113-117; Xing Fuzeng, Yuan Hao, Liu Shaolin 2016: 64-70)

Jin Tianming (金天明, 1968-) is of Korean descent and was born into a farming family in Jidong County, Heilongjiang Province. He received an atheistic education from a young age and enrolled in the Chemical Engineering Department at Tsinghua University in 1986, graduating in 1991. After experiencing the shattering of his ideals post-June Fourth, he began exploring the Christian faith in September 1989 and regularly attended Sunday services, participating in the Korean gatherings borrowed from the Three-Self Gangwashi Church. In January 1991, he experienced a definite assurance of rebirth and salvation, and in May, during a gathering, he received God’s call, deciding to abandon further academic pursuits and dedicate himself to God. He commenced studying the Bible and engaged in ministry while choosing not to follow the Three-Self path.

Starting in 1993, he began holding meetings at home near the West Gate of Tsinghua University, moving to rented accommodations the following year. From 1997 to 2001, he rented a space in a courtyard in Huangzhuang, Haidian District. During the early development of the church, the main target for evangelism was university students, and multiple fellowships were established near several universities in Haidian, achieving rapid growth. By the second half of 2005, the church had developed into 12 to 13 fellowships, with a total Sunday attendance of about 300 people. At that point, the church decided to transition from a fellowship model to a congregation model, merging the fellowships, completing the merger in March 2008, and renting office space in Huajie Building for gatherings.

In 2004, Jin Tianming led the Watchman Church to apply for registration with the government, setting a precedent for house churches. However, when registration was denied, and meeting spaces faced numerous crackdowns, the church’s original moderate stance became increasingly radical, openly criticizing the government’s oppressive policies. When clarifying why they did not participate in the Three-Self movement, they further reinforced their anti-Three-Self rhetoric. In November 2009 and April 2011, Jin Tianming initiated outdoor worship services to advocate for space for their congregational meetings.

Since the outdoor worship incident in 2011, Jin Tianming has been under house arrest by the authorities for a prolonged period of nine years, until surveillance was lifted in October 2020.

In March 2019, the Watchman Church was declared disbanded by the government.

  1. Wang Dao (Yu Jie 2011: 25-57; World Journal 2018)

Wang Dao (王島, 1968), also known as Wang Tongjiang, is from Guangxi and received an atheistic education from a young age. In 1988, he enrolled in the History Department of Central South University for Nationalities in Wuhan, participating in the Tiananmen Square protests in 1989. Anticipating a government crackdown on student movements, he returned to Wuhan on June 2. After June Fourth, he continued to initiate class boycotts. Later, when subjected to scrutiny, his life fell into a low point, during which he encountered the Gospel through radio broadcasts, learning about Christianity for the first time and starting to read the Bible. In April 1990, he was arrested for posting a large poster commemorating the June Fourth Incident, which read, “Blood debts must be repaid in blood.” In May 1991, he was sentenced for “counter-revolutionary propaganda and incitement,” but due to the minor nature of the case, he was released. He felt it was God’s salvation and prayed, willing to follow the Lord for life. However, as his student status had been revoked, he worked in Hainan Province, and in 1993 established a company, distancing himself from God due to business commitments. Later, facing a crisis in his company, he began to reflect on life and renewed his pursuit of faith.

In 1995, he started attending a house church and was baptized as a Christian in 1996, becoming enthusiastic about evangelism and collaborating with his wife to establish an “Loving Kindergarten.” In August 1998, he was called to full-time ministry, preaching and establishing churches across Hainan. The Loving Kindergarten was registered under the name of an educational company, managed by his wife, while Wang Dao dedicated himself to full-time ministry. However, with increasing pressure from the government, the family moved to Guangzhou in 2004, initially joining a church established by a foreign missionary and engaging with a university student fellowship, setting up a “Student Home” in the university town. In 2005, the Student Home faced government crackdowns, prompting the church to suspend operations.

Consequently, Wang Dao began holding meetings at home, later renting another venue to establish Liangren Church. In 2005, he founded Putian Gospel Theological Seminary. Later, due to government suppression, the seminary relocated several times. By 2008, the church’s membership had grown to 100, renting more extensive space in the Zhuhai District.   1. The China Christian Council Joined the World Council of Churches in 1991 (Wickeri 2007: 320-323; Carino 2019: 175-191; Amity Foundation 1994: 17; Liu Yuhong 2016: 104-109; Tian Yu 1996: 8-10)

The World Council of Churches (WCC) was established in 1948, and Chinese Christianity has actively participated (including Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, and Presbyterian churches), with Zhao Zicheng being selected as one of the six presidents. After the Korean War, Zhao resigned from his position as president and accused the WCC of being a tool of imperialism in China. Since then, the Chinese church severed ties with the WCC. In 1956, as the political situation in China began to open slightly, Bishop Ding Guangxun of the China Anglican Church attended the preparatory meeting for the Lambeth Conference and later participated as an observer in the WCC’s Central Committee meeting in Hungary. During this period, the WCC’s general secretary suggested Ding change his observer status to that of a representative, but Ding did not agree. However, as the Anti-Rightist Movement unfolded, the limited contact between the Chinese church and the World Council of Churches was once again interrupted. In 1975, WCC’s general secretary Philip Potter privately invited Ding Guangxun to attend the Nairobi Assembly, but Ding felt the timing was not yet right.

In 1983, the China Christian Council sent a delegation to the WCC Vancouver Assembly for the first time, where Ding and Potter discussed their relationship. In November, the China Christian Council visited the WCC in Geneva. During a short speech at the Ecumenical Centre in Geneva, Ding Guangxun stated that the Chinese church needs to become part of the universal church, emphasizing that the Three-Self Patriotic Movement does not include self-isolation. In 1984, Potter made a return visit to China.In 1989, the China Christian Council once again visited the WCC.

The participation of the China Christian Council in the WCC involved two issues. First, according to the WCC constitution, members are represented as “churches,” but the China Christian Council, established in 1980 as a religious institution during the post-denominational period, is only a “church management organization.” From the WCC’s perspective, as the four Chinese denominational churches had never formally withdrawn in 1948, the Council could still be seen as part of the WCC to some extent as its successor organization. In 1990, Ding Guangxun requested WCC General Secretary Emilio Castro to send a letter inviting the Council to become a formal member. The second and more complicated issue was the Taiwan problem. Since the Presbyterian Church of Taiwan (PCT) is a WCC member, under the CCP’s “One China” principle, whether the Council’s joining the WCC would violate this principle involved the critical question of the Chinese government’s consent to the Council’s membership.

Ding Guangxun proposed that the Council request to join the WCC with the condition that “Taiwan is located in Taiwan, China.” In response, the PCT reiterated: (1) that it welcomes the participation of Christian groups from around the world in WCC work; (2) that new members should not interfere with the dignity and rights of existing churches; (3) that they support the China Christian Council’s joining under appropriate application conditions; and (4) that they absolutely do not accept the PCT being categorized under “China” or “Chinese region,” asserting that Taiwan is Taiwan, and China is China. Later, during negotiations with Bishop Ding Guangxun, PCT Pastor Kao Chun-Ming and others expressed their welcome for the Council to join without proposing additional conditions, especially regarding including Taiwan under the “territory of China.” Ding insisted that Taiwan falls within the geographical scope of China and that the “China” of the Republic of China and the People’s Republic of China are the same. Kao countered that this was “political geography,” and the undeniable political entity of Taiwan’s independence must be acknowledged. The two sides could not reach a consensus. However, days later, Ding Guangxun ultimately decided to abandon all conditions for joining. On February 18, 1991, at the WCC Canberra Assembly, the China Christian Council became the 317th member of the WCC, receiving only one abstention (anonymous vote). Ding stated: “The membership of the China Christian Council will in no way harm the independence and integrity of any church outside mainland China.” After Ding’s speech, Pastor Kao Chun-Ming shook hands and embraced Ding, and they greeted Bishop Shen Yifan and Han Wenzhao.

Ding Guangxun’s change in attitude was undoubtedly due to the PCT’s firm stance. At the same time, WCC General Secretary Castro reiterated acceptance of the “One China” principle, emphasizing that members would be listed by continent rather than by country, and that the geographical entities in church names would not refer to “countries.” Of course, the most crucial factor remained the Chinese government’s acceptance of these arrangements, anticipated as a way to break through the diplomatic isolation faced by China from the international community following June Fourth.

However, the frequent expression of the China Christian Council’s strong Taiwanese subjectivity towards the PCT had led to repeated dissatisfaction. For instance, in September 1994, the China Christian Council issued a statement criticizing the PCT for supporting Taiwanese independence. In October 1996, the fifth Asian Conference on Religion & Peace was held in Thailand. Representatives from various Asian countries, including China, were invited to attend, while the Taiwanese representatives were included as observers. However, when the Chinese religious representatives arrived at the venue, they found that the Taiwanese observer representatives were wearing official representative name tags, with “Taiwan” or “Republic of China” listed under the country column. Furthermore, the commemorative booklet for the 20th anniversary of “Asian Religion and Peace” oddly featured the “Blue Sky, White Sun” flag. Chinese religious representatives expressed deep dissatisfaction upon witnessing Taiwanese representatives engaging in activities promoting “two Chinas” or “one China, one Taiwan” in the international arena. Both Ding Guangxun and Buddhist representatives drafted a statement overnight, protesting vigorously during their speeches against the organizers’ violation of the “One China principle” and the creation of “two Chinas.”

  1. Songs of Canaan（徐頌贊2020；Sun 2007: 98-116；Starr 2016: 264-268；Chan 2019: 150-157）

The “Songs of Canaan” is a collection of hymns widely sung in Chinese churches, originally derived from personal spiritual songs by 呂小敏 (Lu Xiaomin, 1970-). After becoming a Christian at the end of 1989, 小敏 experienced “the inspiration of the Holy Spirit” and began to compose hymns. In 1990, she joined 張榮亮’s (Zhang Rongliang) “Fangcheng Team” and continuously wrote over a thousand praise songs during her service, which were compiled and edited by the team into the “Songs of Canaan.” By December 2014, more than 1,800 songs had been created, collectively known as “Songs of Canaan.”

Initially, there were differing opinions within the Fangcheng Team about whether to accept 小敏’s songs. Some coworkers opposed her use of popular tunes for worship, but others insisted that spiritual songs come from the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, valuing the importance of the lyrics over the melody. Later, with the support of 張榮亮 (Zhang Rongliang), 張義南 (Zhang Yinan), and 高國付 (Gao Guofu), the position of these songs within the Fangcheng Team was affirmed and further spread to the 唐河 Team (Tanghe Team) in Henan (China Evangelical Fellowship), and teams in 安徽 (Anhui) such as 穎上 (Yingshang, China Truth Church) and 利申 (Lishen, China Blessing Church), where they were accepted for use in worship services and evangelism. For these rural teams, the Songs of Canaan embody rich local experiences and feature simple, easy-to-understand lyrics and beautiful, flowing melodies, which resonate well with the religious sentiments of farmer Christians and are greatly helpful for evangelism. Subsequently, they were also sung in some grassroots 三自 churches and emerging urban house churches. Under the promotion of 趙天恩 (Zhao Tian’en), Christian communities in Hong Kong and Taiwan concerned about the Chinese evangelistic work also gained more understanding of 小敏 and the Songs of Canaan. The 福州 (Fuzhou) Communication Association produced a documentary titled “The Cross,” one episode of which featured “Songs of Canaan.” Thus, the Songs of Canaan also spread within Chinese churches in North America.

The Songs of Canaan carry strong local sentiments and later reflected the experiences and portraits of missionaries who left their hometowns as rural house church teams began cross-regional missions. Additionally, “patriotism” is also a theme in the Songs of Canaan, particularly emphasizing China as the subject of salvation, from national repentance to the gospel reaching China, and being endowed with a unique missionary mission.

In 2014, Chinese director 馮小剛 (Feng Xiaogang) filmed the movie “1942,” with the end theme song being 小敏’s composition “The River of Life,” performed by Christian artist 姚貝娜 (Yao Beina).

The “Regulations for Trial Implementation of Churches in Various Regions of Chinese Christianity” was passed in 1991. After reviewing and discussing its practice, the Church Governance Committee revised it and submitted it to the Sixth National Conference of Chinese Christianity at the end of 1996, where it was formally approved as the “Church Order for Chinese Protestant Christian Churches.”

The “Regulations” encompass five aspects: church, believers, sacraments, clerical status, and church (venue) organization and management. Overall, the content emphasizes practical issues, such as the definition of believers, execution of sacraments, classification and selection criteria for clerics, as well as principles for organization and management of venues, and does not involve matters of faith confession, reflecting the Chinese church’s state of “post-denomination” church union (rather than a united church).

In 1996, the National Christian Council of China published the book “Upholding the Truth, Resisting Heresy,” edited by Pastor 趙志恩 (Zhao Zhien) from Nanjing Union Theological Seminary. He summarized five major issues of heresies in China: (1) Propagating that the end of the world has arrived and Jesus has already returned a second time; (2) Promoting the opposition between believers and non-believers, viciously cursing the party and government as “devils” and “Satanic regimes”; (3) Disparaging the “Three-Self” movement, splitting the church, and attacking “patriots who love their faith” as a great whore; (4) Recklessly claiming in faith to be the “living Christ,” “the second coming of the Lord Jesus,” “the Chinese Savior,” and “the King appointed,” thereby replacing our true Savior—the incarnate Jesus Christ; (5) Their actions are secretive and scheming, though they claim to be prepared to suffer and go to prison, they are actually doing things that cannot withstand public scrutiny.

From the five points revealed by Zhao, the first and fourth can be considered doctrinal errors, while the remaining three reflect more the political and social order considerations behind them. It is particularly noteworthy that in China, heretical teachings are often collectively termed heresies, with the so-called heretical teachings further highlighting the threat heresies pose to the existing political and social order. Thus, heresy and “cult organizations that bear the name of Christianity” become intertwined products in the Chinese context.

The book mentions popular “heresies” in China, including 被立王 (the Appointed King), 李常受 (Li Changshou), 全範圍教會 (All-Spectrum Church), 靈靈教 (Lingling Church), 冷水教 (Cold Water Church), 永生善道 (Eternal Good Way), and 四福音派 (Four Gospel Sect). It also introduces heresies from South Korea and the United States.

In 1992, the China Church Research Center compiled reports on heresies from “天風” (Tianfeng), pointing out that those judged as “heresies” were not solely based on doctrinal errors, but also included “illegal organizations that ‘wear a Christian cloak,’ ‘engage in superstition,’ or ‘criminal activities,’ as well as ‘use Christian language for counter-revolutionary propaganda,’ ‘incite believers to oppose the Three-Self movement,’ and ‘reactionary organizations against the party and government’.” Due to the National Christian Council’s “not strict” use of the term “heresy,” it inevitably raises suspicions about whether “heresy and cult” are being conflated with “church organizations with differing opinions.” Among the most controversial is whether “opposing the Three-Self movement” equates to being “anti-party and anti-government” heresy.

From the context of church history, heresy originally pertained solely to whether doctrinal errors occurred. However, in the unique context of the contemporary Chinese church, it adds substantial political factors, causing the concept of “heretical teachings” to be used interchangeably.These heresies (some of which are admittedly heretical in doctrine, while others are merely “extreme”) often violate or challenge the bottom line and taboos of the party and state regarding social order, and thus are suppressed by the government. The considerations of the party and state reflect the traditional Chinese political-religious relationship that emphasizes politics over doctrine.

The Theological Reconstruction movement officially launched following the second plenary meeting of the National Christian Council of China held in Jinan in November 1998 (the “Jinan Conference”). Bishop 丁光訓 (Ding Guangxun), as honorary chair and president, spoke during this meeting, stating that “God’s revelation in the Bible is gradual, not completed at once, and humanity’s understanding of God’s revelation is also not complete but progresses in stages.” This prompted him to reflect: “Is there development in the understanding of God and other concepts within the Bible?” The premise of theological reconstruction is the acknowledgment that God’s revelation is progressive and that the understanding of God has developed within the Bible; thus, older theological thoughts require adjustment. The Jinan Conference issued a resolution on “Strengthening Theological Reconstruction,” indicating that “to properly manage the church according to the Three-Self Principles” should “leverage theological theory to guide church construction,” enabling Chinese Christian theology to effectively adapt to a socialist society. To promote theological reconstruction, a “Theological Reconstruction Promotion Team” was established, led by 丁光訓. In November 1998, coinciding with the publication of “The Collected Works of 丁光訓,” the focus of theological reconstruction was guided by 丁光訓’s theology.

The so-called theological reconstruction, according to 丁, aims to adjust and update the “old theological thoughts” that do not adapt well to socialism. In 丁’s view, the “old” theology primarily emphasizes the contradictions and oppositions between believers and non-believers as highlighted by fundamentalists, claiming that non-believers will face judgment and be cast into hell upon the Lord’s return. Regardless of any good deeds performed, a person’s “unbelief” renders them ineffective before God. 丁 pointed out that this doctrine can lead to the distortion of “moral uselessness” or “faith without works.” Furthermore, the excessive emphasis on believers attaining eternal life and non-believers facing eternal damnation could disrupt the current stability and unity emphasized in China. If religious believers insist on the distinction between believers and non-believers, even classifying non-believers as unsalvageable and destined for hell, it inevitably challenges the ruling authority of the Communist Party (an atheistic group). On the other hand, viewing adherents of major religions outside Christianity, as well as the general public, as “unsalvageable,” poses serious threats to social stability and unity.

To address the relationship between belief and disbelief, it inevitably touches upon the doctrine of “justification by faith.” 丁 noted that within the Chinese church, justification by faith is understood as: “God does not inquire whether you have lived righteously or unrighteously, nor whether you have lived selfishly or dedicated your life to serving others; He only asks whether you believed during your lifetime or not. If you had faith during your life, no matter how selfish or harmful you were, you will go to heaven and enjoy eternal happiness after death; while if you had not believed, regardless of your contributions to others or society, you will certainly be sent to hell.” As a result, the core message of justification by faith—“from justification to righteous works”—becomes distorted. In 丁光訓’s perspective, the God of Christianity is not a punitive deity like 閰羅 (Yama), but a loving God. Paul states that “these three remain: faith, hope, and love, but the greatest of these is love,” thus illustrating that “love surpasses faith.” Therefore, Christianity must elevate ethics and morality and should not sensationalize the contradictive “justification by faith.” This became a focus in the later stages of theological reconstruction concerning the “attenuation of justification by faith.” In 2002, 丁 stated at a theological reconstruction seminar in Hubei Province: “We should not be too determined to cancel ‘justification by faith’; hence we merely say to attenuate it. Attenuation means, just like a cup of tea that has been partially consumed and then diluted with water, the tea becomes lighter. … Some say that the four words ‘justification by faith’ exist in the Bible and cannot be changed or touched. If there are believers who think this way, that is not surprising. We don’t propose dismantling it. We still have time to slowly cultivate this.”

In 2000, marking the 50th anniversary of the Three-Self Movement, 丁光訓 summarized the Three-Self Movement post-New China into three stages. The first stage from the 1950s to the 1970s focused on “anti-imperialism and patriotism.” After the end of the Cultural Revolution, it entered the second stage of “properly managing the church,” characterized by “autonomy, self-support, and self-propagation.” The Theological Reconstruction movement initiated in 1998 marked the entry of the Three-Self Movement into its third stage.It can be said that 丁光訓 (Ding Guangxun) became the “soul” of the theological reconstruction movement, with his theological thoughts almost entirely dominating the direction of the discussions, leading to a “post-Ding” framework following his retirement.

Since the Jinan Conference, various regional councils and theological seminaries have hosted seminars focused on theological reconstruction, using “The Collected Works of 丁光訓” as the foundation for learning. In 2000, the national councils compiled 丁’s works, thematically categorized as “On the Three-Self Movement and Church Construction,” “On Theological Reconstruction,” “On the Bible,” “On God,” and “On Christ.” In 2001, the councils published the “Chinese Christian Theological Reconstruction Series,” with the first volume being 丁光訓’s “The Bible, Faith, and the Church.” That same year, the national councils held the “Academic Seminar on the Biblical Perspective of Chinese Christianity” in Qingdao, Shandong.

丁光訓’s proposal to “attenuate justification by faith” sparked considerable controversy, even among the councils. In 2002, during the fifth theological reconstruction seminar in Zhejiang Province, he summarized the church’s internal reactions: “Since the proposal of theological reconstruction, there has been significant differentiation within Chinese Christianity; the supporters cannot be said to be few, but they are not enough. Among the clergy in Zhejiang Province, those who have fully understood the need for theological reconstruction are not necessarily many. We can wait; however, if they remain inactive, it will be unfortunate for the church in China and likely for them personally. They live in this world without advocating for something worthy of support; they become pastors or preachers yet feign ignorance regarding this movement, which I believe they will regret when they grow older.” Many workers and believers with fundamentalist backgrounds are concerned that theological reconstruction is a mode of “liberal suppression of fundamentalism,” with the attenuation of justification by faith representing a dilution of foundational beliefs. Due to advocating for theological reconstruction, “天風” (Tianfeng) saw its circulation drop from 100,000 copies in 2001 to 70,000 in 2002. As 曹聖潔 (Cao Shengjie) candidly remarked in the work report presented at the Seventh National Christian Conference, “the development of various churches in promoting theological reconstruction is not smooth”; some responsible workers “have not timely recognized the significance of theological reconstruction,” and “some individuals still have certain concerns in their thoughts.” Ultimately, after celebrating the tenth anniversary of theological reconstruction in 2008, the national councils gradually made adjustments. Due to health reasons, 丁光訓 only delivered a “written” speech during this celebration, marking a transition from the “post-Ding” era of the Three-Self Movement to the “Ding-after” era.

The full text elaborates on its faith positions in seven parts: biblical interpretation, Trinitarian theology, Christology, soteriology, pneumatology, ecclesiology, and eschatology. Some of these are clearly aimed at addressing the claims of certain heresies and cults in China. For example: (1) In response to some heresies that selectively believe in parts of the Bible or accept “the Holy Spirit” as the only authority for biblical interpretation, the “Statement of Faith” opposes anyone altering the Bible in any way. Biblical interpretation should consider the pure traditions of faith left by the church throughout history (biblical interpretation); (2) Against heresies that claim “the Father is greater than the Son, the Son is greater than the Holy Spirit,” or advocate “the Son was created,” or “the modalistic approach,” the “Statement of Faith” reaffirms the equality of the essence of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, while recognizing their distinct functions in salvation: the Father plans salvation, the Son accomplishes it, and the Spirit implements it. The three are inseparable and constitute one. It denies the misinterpretation of the Trinity as “one essence in three forms” or “one essence in three identities” (Trinitarian theology); (3) In response to heresies that suggest Christians can become gods or claim to be the returning Christ, the “Statement of Faith” states that Jesus Christ is the only begotten Son of God who will return to judge the world on the last day. Christians gain the title of God’s children but remain human and do not become gods. No one knows the specific date of Christ’s return, and any individual claiming to be Christ is considered a heretic (Christology); (4) In response to the doctrine of “once saved, always saved,” the “Statement of Faith” believes that God will keep His children “forever,” and humans must also steadfastly hold to the truth without deviation. It opposes the notion of sinful living based on grace after salvation, the idea of being saved multiple times, and the belief in salvation through law (soteriology); (5) Addressing heresies that deny the personhood of the Holy Spirit, the “Statement of Faith” affirms the various abilities granted by the Holy Spirit, witnessing the powerful works of God through miracles, and refutes the belief that miracles and gifts of the Holy Spirit ceased after the apostolic age. Regarding the matter of speaking in tongues, it neither prohibits nor emphasizes speaking in tongues as proof of salvation (pneumatology); (6) In response to government control over churches through the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, which effectively “determines” churches (e.g., designated locations, appointed personnel, and established areas), the “Statement of Faith” emphasizes that Christ is the head of the church, and the church is the body of Christ, which has both local and universal aspects. The church’s spiritual work is not under the control of secular powers and submits to the constitution at a legal level. The church’s mission is to propagate the gospel, teach, shepherd believers, equip, send out, refute falsehoods, and uphold the truth. Christians are free to gather in the Lord’s name, unrestricted by the number of attendees or location. It opposes the union of church and politics or the infiltration of politics into the church, as well as reliance on domestic or foreign political power for church development. The church also opposes participation in any activities that undermine national unity and state integrity (ecclesiology); (7) Regarding eschatological issues, the “Statement of Faith” reiterates the teaching that Christ will return, but also points out that “no one knows the specific date of Christ’s return.” Therefore, believers should “strive to do the Lord’s work, proclaim the word of life, and shine for the Lord in the world” as they “await the Lord’s return” (eschatology).

Since 1949, the Three-Self churches had not published any communal statement of faith, despite a house church system in Northern Jiangsu drafting a statement of faith in 1997 that did not gain widespread acceptance.

The “Statement of Faith” and “Attitude” can be regarded as the first unifying action among Chinese house churches. 張義南 (Zhang Yinan) pointed out in “Sixty Years of Chinese House Churches” that “the ‘Statement of Faith’ was generated under the severe conditions of persecution faced by the house churches. The primary purpose is to testify that house churches are not cults, but rather initiated by Jesus Christ; another aim of the Statement of Faith is to promote church unity.” The declaration not only seeks consensus amid theological differences but also directly responds to accusations that house churches are heretical or cultish.

On May 12, the National Publishing Administration’s office responded, stating that “The Bible Code” was an “illegal publication” produced by unscrupulous publishers using the name of Dunhuang Literary Press. On June 4, the National Publishing Administration’s office further informed 沈德溶 that the book’s illegal publication had “produced adverse effects on society,” and they would formally notify localities to confiscate and ban the book.

In 1998, the Shanghai Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee and the Shanghai Christian Affairs Committee published a book titled “How to View ‘The Bible Code’,” which included articles from various Shanghai pastors and teachers from East China Theological Seminary and Nanjing Union Theological Seminary critiquing the contents of the book, particularly regarding its teachings on “the Lord’s return.” This book was a compilation of discussions from the monthly meetings of pastors and other seminars in Shanghai.

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Ye Xiaowen (Ye Xiaowen 1996: 3-4; 1997: 4; 2014: 6-7, 79-86)

Ye Xiaowen (Ye Xiaowen, 1950-), a native of Ningxiang, Hunan, joined the Communist Party of China in 1975 and enrolled in Guizhou University’s Department of Philosophy in 1976. After graduating, he studied philosophy in the graduate class at the Guizhou Academy of Social Sciences and later worked at the Philosophy Research Institute of the Guizhou Academy of Social Sciences. In 1984, he was appointed deputy director of the Guizhou Academy of Social Sciences. The following year, he served as the secretary of the Guizhou Provincial Committee of the Communist Youth League. From 1991 to 1995, he was the director of the Ethnic and Religious Affairs Bureau of the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee, and from 1995 to 2009, he served as the director of the State Administration for Religious Affairs (renamed the National Religious Affairs Bureau in 1998). He led China’s religious work for 18 years beginning in 1991.

In 1996, he pointed out that religious work should focus on “three things”: (1) Strengthening the registration of religious activity venues: he stated that registration is a means to enhance the legal management of religious activity venues and religious affairs; (2) Conducting thorough research and analysis to strengthen category-based guidance, focus on key points, address difficult issues, and solve hotspots; (3) Strengthening the construction of two teams: namely, the team of religious work cadres and the teams of patriotic religious organizations and religious personnel.

In 1997, he acknowledged that besides the five major religions in China, there are also various new, sub, mutant, and pseudo types of religions. New types refer to emerging religions introduced with China’s opening up, sub types refer to China’s folk beliefs, mutant types refer to cults disguised as religions, and pseudo types refer to “feudal superstitions.” He noted that how to respond to these different types and strengthen management of religious affairs has become a pressing issue.

In 1996, regarding how Christianity can adapt to a socialist society, Ye Xiaowen supported Ding Guangxun’s advocacy for promoting the deep-seated evolution of religious concepts and engaging in theological innovation. Addressing the contradiction between belief and disbelief, Ye stated, “It should not only be ‘justified by faith’ but also ‘justified by love’.”

Ye Xiaowen acknowledged that from a worldview perspective, materialists must struggle against religious idealism and theistic concepts in ideological propaganda and education work to consolidate and expand their territory; however, he also pointed out that in political work and social management, it is necessary to “unite religious figures and believers, and provide them with necessary social services and economic political guarantees.” To “comply with central tasks, ‘struggle with religion’ often needs to be placed in a secondary position, even compromising to ‘mutual respect in beliefs’ for the sake of ‘political unity and cooperation’.” At the same time, he also proposed paying attention to the issues of “left” and right in religious work; to achieve unity and stability, it is necessary to prevent “left,” which is treating “non-confrontational contradictions” as “confrontational contradictions” or attempting to use administrative power to suppress religion. However, he noted that while correcting the “left,” religious work might also go to the opposite extreme and show a rightward tendency, meaning treating religious freedom as allowing religion to operate freely without regulation, becoming reluctant to manage it, and allowing “administrative power to develop religion.” Therefore, it is essential to “legally strengthen the management of religious affairs” while maintaining atheism, particularly in providing scientific worldview and atheism education to young people.

He has authored works such as “Looking at Social Issues from Multiple Perspectives” (1997), “Tell the American People the Real Situation of Religion in China: Transcripts of Ye Xiaowen’s Q&A” (1999), “Transforming Confrontation into Dialogue: Transcripts of Ye Xiaowen’s Talks with the U.S. ‘International Religious Freedom Commission’ Delegation” (2005), “Steps Starting from the Heart” (2006), “Religious Issues: How to View and Address Them” (2007), “Seven Days of Religion” (2007), “A Hundred Essays by Xiaowen” (2008), “China’s Theoretical Innovations and Practical Explorations in Solving Religious Issues” (2014), among others.

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. The Chinese Communist Party (Compilation by the Comprehensive Research Group of the Central Office of the Communist Party of China, Policy and Regulation Division of the State Administration for Religious Affairs, 1995: 189-204, 249-255, 278-285; Xing Fuzeng 2003)

After the “June Fourth” incident, the CCP reiterated that “one central task, two basic points” is the party’s basic line, which affirms that economic development is the central task while insisting on the Four Cardinal Principles and continuing to push for reform and opening up. Therefore, religious work must also align with the aforementioned basic line. Additionally, after the 1989 pro-democracy movement, in light of the drastic changes in domestic and international situations from 1989 to 1990, the leadership of the party-state further strengthened the focus on religious work, especially concerning the issue of religious infiltration.

In December 1990, the National Religious Work Conference was convened, marking an important meeting for the CCP’s adjustment of religious policy after June Fourth. At the meeting, Premier Li Peng stated in his speech titled “Further Emphasizing, Caring for, and Properly Managing Religious Work” that working well in religious affairs and properly addressing China’s religious issues are of significant importance for maintaining stability and unity both domestically and internationally; it is also vital for economic and social development in the 1990s. However, he also emphasized that one must not forget the overseas hostile forces, which have consistently used religion as an important means to promote peaceful evolution. Such activities exacerbate internal contradictions into confrontational ones, aiming to subvert the regime of the People’s Republic of China and the socialist system. General Secretary Jiang Zemin also delivered a speech titled “Ensuring Religious Work is Done Well,” stating that although the religious community remained stable during the “1989 political turmoil,” one must not ignore the fact that overseas hostile forces are indeed using religion for infiltration and subversion. “The religious issue is a significant issue. It relates to the stability and unity of our entire country, to national unity and the unification of the motherland, to the construction of our entire socialist material and spiritual civilization, and to the struggles against infiltration, peaceful evolution, and counter-peaceful evolution.” Therefore, it is necessary to continue implementing religious policies and adequately work to unify patriotic individuals in the religious community with the broad masses of believers. “Good religious work can play a positive role in promoting socialist construction; if done poorly, it will be exploited by domestic and foreign hostile forces.” Good religious work does not mean solving or eliminating religion hastily, but rather ensuring the stability and continuity of religious policies and alleviating various concerns of religious figures.

In November 1993, Jiang Zemin, at the National United Front Work Conference, further raised the issue that “ethnic and religious matters are critically important,” summarizing religious work in “three phrases”: (1) comprehensively and accurately implementing the party’s religious policy: the focus of the religious policy is that administrative power cannot be used to “eliminate” religion, nor can it be used to “promote” religion; efforts should be made to unite those who hold beliefs in the socialist cause; (2) strengthening the legal management of religious affairs: management cannot be abandoned or weakened; (3) actively guiding religion to adapt to socialist society.

Among these, the theory of adaptation became a new guiding ideology of the CCP’s religious theory in the 1990s. Jiang Zemin emphasized that religion must adapt to socialist society; otherwise, conflicts will arise. “This adaptation does not require believers to abandon theism or their religious beliefs, but demands that they politically love the motherland, support the socialist system, and uphold the leadership of the Communist Party; at the same time, reforms must be made to religious systems and doctrines that do not adapt to socialism, utilizing certain positive elements from religious teachings, regulations, and morals to serve socialism.” Because, “only socialism can save China and develop China, and this is the consensus among the people of the nation.” This serves as the political foundation for religion to adapt to socialism. It is clear that “adaptation” requires the religious community to change itself to align with the development of Chinese socialism.

Jiang Zemin’s statement that “there are no small matters in religion” serves as a reminder to party-state officials not to use extreme “left” methods to suppress or eliminate religion; only through the implementation of the religious freedom policy can national stability and unity be achieved. At the same time, the party-state cannot abandon its management and control over the religious field, as complete liberalization would mean religion could be exploited by anti-communist, anti-China elements, providing a pathway for peaceful evolution in China. This attitude against “leftism” while guarding against a rightist push is the true meaning of Jiang Zemin’s statement that “ethnic and religious matters are critically important.”

As early as the mid-1980s, the CCP had largely abandoned the position that “religion is the opium of the people,” no longer defining religion purely as a reactionary and negative ideology, indicating that the party-state had to acknowledge the long-term existence of religion in socialist society.The book “The Religious Issues in China During the Socialist Period,” edited by Luo Zhufeng (1986), set the tone for this controversial issue. By the early 1990s, the theory of “the adaptation of religion to Chinese socialist society” had been formally proposed.

In July 1994, at a symposium for students discussing ethnic and religious issues in the new situation, Li Ruihuan pointed out that the differences between atheists and religious believers “are secondary,” while “the fundamental political and economic interests are aligned.” The focus should be on “maintaining political unity and cooperation, and mutual respect in beliefs.” “Adaptation” is the key aspect of this context. His understanding of “adaptation” was: “Religion must comply with the national laws, regulations, and policies of the socialist society at this stage,” rather than being “in conflict.” It is evident that managing religious affairs in accordance with the law, while requiring religion to adapt to various aspects of socialist society, became the central theme of the CCP’s religious policies in the 1990s. Meanwhile, “resisting infiltration” also became an important direction in religious work. As a one-party authoritarian regime, the Chinese Communist Party will absolutely not abandon its management and control over religion.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Religious Activity Venues Registered According to Law 1994 (National Religious Affairs Bureau Policy and Regulation Division 1999: 4-6, 10-11; Tianfeng 1992: 20; Tianfeng 1994: 3; Tianfeng 1995: 28-29; Qiao 1995: 3-11, 14-15; Qiao 1996: 13)

In February 1991, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China requested in Document No. 6: “All religious activity venues should be registered according to law (specific methods will be prescribed separately).” In January 1994, the State Council issued Order No. 145 (Regulations on the Management of Religious Activity Venues), Article 2 states: “Establishing religious activity venues must be registered. The registration methods will be formulated by the State Council’s religious affairs department.” In April, the State Council’s Religious Affairs Bureau published the Regulations on the Registration of Religious Activity Venues, officially establishing the registration standards for religious activity venues.

According to Article 2 of the Regulations on the Registration of Religious Activity Venues, to establish a religious activity venue, the following conditions must be met: (1) there must be a fixed location and name; (2) there must be citizens who regularly participate in religious activities; (3) there must be a management organization composed of religious citizens; (4) there must be religious personnel or individuals who meet religious guidelines to host religious activities; (5) there must be management regulations; (6) there must be legal economic income. In terms of the registration procedure, the person in charge of the management organization of the venue must submit a registration application to the religious affairs department of the people’s government at or above the county level (Article 4). Those who meet the regulations will be registered, while those who are “partially compliant” will be subject to “temporary registration or deferment of registration,” and those who do not meet the regulations “will not be registered” (Article 6).

The Communist Party of China attempts to address the issues of house churches through “registration according to law.” House churches have differing attitudes toward registration, with participation in the Three-Self Patriotic Movement being a prominent point of concern and doubt regarding the conditions for registration. As early as August 1992, a letter from a reader of the magazine Tianfeng, published by the National Two Meetings of Christianity, specifically asked Bishop 丁光訓 whether “participation in the Two Meetings is one of the conditions for registration.” In response, 丁 indicated that “he did not know such a condition existed.” At the same time, 丁 encouraged striving for registration and reiterated that the registration process is the government’s responsibility; the Three-Self “has no right to intervene,” and participation in the Three-Self is completely “voluntary” and should not be “rigidly specified.” With the promulgation of the relevant registration methods, Bishop 丁 once again emphasized in 1994 that “participation in the Three-Self Organization or the Two Meetings is not a prerequisite for licensing applications and approvals,” “registration is a church’s registration with the government, not with the Three-Self Organization or the Two Meetings,” and there is no requirement for churches applying for registration to obtain recommendations or recognition from the Three-Self Organization or the Two Meetings. Moreover, “registration does not imply participation in the Three-Self Organization or the Two Meetings.” In March 1995, 丁光訓 again answered similar inquiries.

However, during implementation in various regions, there has been a tendency to reinforce the role of patriotic religious groups. For example, the “Shandong Province Regulations on the Management of Religious Activity Venues,” promulgated in November 1993, lists “religious personnel or other clergy recognized by related patriotic religious groups for hosting religious activities” as one of the registration criteria. Similarly, the “Tianjin City Regulations on the Management of Religious Activity Venues,” issued in February 1994, require the submission of “the opinions of religious groups” when applying for registration; similar requirements appear in the “Interim Regulations on Administrative Management of Religious Affairs in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region” (March 1994) and the “Wuhan City Regulations on the Management of Religious Activity Venues” (July 1994). Notably, even if some local regulations or provisions do not directly grant a role to patriotic religious groups during the registration application process, when referencing “religious personnel,” it is also stipulated that their identity must be recognized by “relevant religious groups according to their prescribed procedures” (e.g., Article 16 of the “Regulations on Religious Affairs in Shanghai,” November 1995). It can be seen that although the registration methods do not emphasize that churches must belong to officially recognized religious organizations, the problem is that the “methods” also stipulate that qualified churches must “have religious personnel or individuals who meet the regulations of various religions,” which provides sufficient flexibility for the religious affairs departments at or above the county level. The issues involved in registration, especially in relation to officially recognized religious groups, are far from as simple as 丁光訓 suggested. How provinces and cities understand and implement these matters reflects the varied situation of religious policy at the local level.

The registration work becomes the government’s effort to rectify illegal religious activity venues. For instance, in Huoqiu County, Anhui, there are 166 open churches and several hundred house churches. However, starting in 1994, local governments launched actions to ban them, and both believers and preachers faced varying degrees of repression and punishment.Gansu Province Qingyang Ethnic and Religious Affairs Office in August 1995 independently formulated conditions for non-registration, the first of which states, “historically was a religious activity venue but has been repurposed or has not implemented policies to be managed by the religious community,” reflecting behind-the-scenes interests related to the return of church properties. The third condition, “religious meeting points established by impersonating religious personnel,” effectively eliminates the possibility of registering house churches.

In November 1995, the General Office of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and the General Office of the State Council forwarded the “Ministry of Public Security on Cracking Down on Cult Organizations Such as the ‘Shouting Sect’ and Related Work Opinions,” which indicated that organizations like the Shouting Sect pose a serious threat to rural China and proposed a special campaign to suppress these cult organizations. The Blue Book of Chinese Society published in 1997 first mentioned the issue of cults, reflecting the concerns of the authorities. This religious report co-authored by the religious research institute of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences and the policy research office of the United Front Work Department detailed “some cult organizations operating under the guise of religion (especially under the guise of Christianity)” and listed five characteristics: (1) using the guise of religion to fulfill political ambitions and satisfy various personal desires; (2) leaders often claim to be “king,” “lord,” “living Christ,” etc., thereby deifying themselves; (3) distorting normal doctrines, spreading heretical and superstitious thoughts, inciting so-called beliefs in “ascending to heaven” and “seeking the lord”; (4) vigorously promoting anti-government and anti-socialist rhetoric such as “abolishing the kingdom of man to establish the kingdom of God” with the intent to subvert the people’s regime; (5) establishing secret organizations, appointing core members, setting up hierarchical structures, and developing a tightly-knit organizational system.

Since most officially recognized cults have Christian backgrounds, many originating from two main rural house church bases—Henan and Anhui—house churches often become targets during crackdowns on cult organizations. In 1997, the Henan Provincial Party Committee included house churches as a focus in the crackdown on cults during the “Stop the Unorthodox and Investigate Cults” campaign (legally stopping illegal activities related to Catholicism and Christianity and cracking down on cult organizations). In the investigation report by the Henan Provincial Cult Research Group, it was mentioned that this campaign dealt a “near-fatal blow” to the Chinese Gospel Fellowship, resulting in “the arrest of several key members and the destruction of organizational structures.” However, the report acknowledged that “arresting key members does not equate to eliminating the influence of cult ideology among the masses”; after 1998, the activities of the Chinese Gospel Fellowship “revived.”

On July 22, 1999, the Ministry of Civil Affairs of the Chinese government officially announced the ban on the Falun Dafa Research Association and classified the Falun Gong organization as an illegal entity. On the same day, the Ministry of Public Security ordered the prohibition of all activities promoting Falun Dafa. A nationwide crackdown on Falun Dafa commenced. According to the Ministry of Civil Affairs, the “charges” against the Falun Dafa Research Association included: conducting illegal activities without registration, promoting superstitious heresy, deceiving the masses, instigating and creating disturbances, and undermining social stability. Once Falun Gong was comprehensively banned, Chinese media (radio, television, newspapers, internet) widely revealed the “true nature” of Falun Dafa. Various groups and agencies also criticized Falun Gong, condemning its “pseudo-scientific” nature. Official public opinion systematically exposed the “distorted theories” behind Falun Dafa, including: mystical cosmology, creationism regarding the origins of life, fatalism regarding birth and death, doomsday theories, and arguments for the uselessness of government.On July 23, Wang Zhaoguo, Minister of the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee, conveyed the central policy to various democratic parties and non-party individuals, describing their political struggle with Falun Gong as “the most serious political event since the 1989 ‘June Fourth’ political turmoil.”

At the beginning of the crackdown on Falun Gong, the People’s Daily emphasized the importance of “distinguishing between normal religious beliefs, legitimate religious activities, and the activities of the ‘Falun Gong’ organization.” However, while the Chinese government cracked down on Falun Gong, it also vigorously promoted “atheism” and critiqued feudal superstitions and cult activities. Yet, properly distinguishing between religious beliefs and feudal superstitions in practice is clearly not an easy task.

Although Falun Gong’s teachings are not related to Christianity, Han Wenzhao, president of the Chinese Christian Association, noted that “as the crimes of Falun Gong are continuously uncovered, I increasingly feel the similarity between Falun Gong and cult organizations that operate under the guise of Christianity.” This includes four aspects: (1) Both promote the imminent end of the world to deceive and intimidate their followers. Cult organizations posing as Christian often demand their followers abandon everything, stop working, and “pray piously,” awaiting ascension to heaven to avoid apocalyptic disasters. “This is completely contrary to our encouragement for believers to love the country and the faith, actively participate in socialist construction, and benefit God and others.” (2) Both have tightly organized structures. Cult organizations posing as Christian incite followers to “create disturbances” at local law enforcement agencies, employing tactics similar to those of Falun Gong. (3) Both promote the idea of not seeking medical treatment for illnesses. (4) Neither is truly theist. “Being a theist does not equate to being politically reactionary.”

Cao Shengjie also pointed out: “We must help Christian believers remain vigilant and absolutely not accept some religiously colored nonsense as faith. If various forms of heretical ideas are allowed to influence religion and lead it towards superstition, this will not only harm the country and society, but also endanger the religion itself.”

On October 9, 1999, the Supreme People’s Court and the Supreme People’s Procuratorate issued the “Judicial Explanations on Crimes by Cults,” defining “cult organizations” legally: “Illegal organizations that establish themselves under the guise of religion, qigong, or other names, deify key members, and use deception, heretical beliefs, and other means to lure, deceive others, develop, and control members, thereby harming society.” On October 30, the Standing Committee of the National People’s Congress passed the “Resolution Concerning the Banning of Heretic Cult Organizations, Prevention Measures Against Them, and Punishment for Cult Activities,” mobilizing the whole of society to carry out comprehensive governance and making the prevention and punishment of cults a long-term task.

During the theological thought construction movement, conflicts arose at the Jinling Union Theological Seminary in Nanjing in 1999 between school leadership and certain students and faculty, resulting in some students being forced to withdraw or leave voluntarily, and some faculty being dismissed or announced as retired.

Due to Ding Guangxun’s advocacy for theological thought construction, which has a clear inclination towards liberal theology, strong tensions formed with many believers in the Chinese church who have fundamentalist and evangelical backgrounds. Following the Jinan Conference in November 1998, materials circulated within the Jinling Union Theological Seminary viewed the Jinan Conference as the beginning of the modernist movement opposing fundamentalism. At this time, the Religious Affairs Bureau was also entering Jinling to carry out evaluation work, which evolved into an ideological inspection of faculty and students, where attitudes towards theological thought construction became an important indicator.

In January 1999, a speech was held in the seminary’s chapel during the evaluation group discussions, with many speakers expressing their support for Ding Guangxun and the theological thought construction.Among the faculty, some criticized the Bible for containing negative and pessimistic elements, pointing out that certain students used phrases like “crooked and perverse generation” during prayers, implying a critique of socialism. Vice President Chen Zemin publicly declared that the attitude toward Ding Guangxun’s writings is the same as the attitude toward the “Three Self” movement, and the treatment of the seminary reflects the party’s stance. The teaching evaluation process placed immense pressure on students with more conservative theological views.

On May 4, 1999, Jinling Theological Seminary held a May Fourth singing event, during which three graduating students (Cui Xiuji, Chen Shunfu, Chen Yong) refused to sing any social songs other than hymns. On May 7, President Ding Guangxun and Vice President Chen Zemin met with the three students, demanding their voluntary withdrawal from school, citing that they fundamentally opposed the school’s theological education policy. The three students defended themselves by drafting a statement titled “Regarding Our Request for Voluntary Withdrawal” and another called “Why We Refused to Sing Social Songs at the Event,” reiterating their theological stance: “We take the Bible as our standard for faith. We also express our position on building God’s church, which is to recognize Christ as the head of the church, and that the church, as the called-out community of God’s people, should unconditionally obey Christ.”

Subsequently, three other undergraduate students (Luo Yunfei, Liu Yichun, Li Zhimin) issued a “Withdrawal Declaration” and “Why We Are Leaving” on June 18, protesting a series of retrogressive measures taken by the seminary. In addition to expressing dissatisfaction with the school’s forced withdrawals of students, they lodged objections to the evaluation process, directing issues at Ding Guangxun and the liberal theological tendencies of the Three Self Movement. Ultimately, they announced, “We solemnly condemn the serious errors of the ‘Three Self’ in faith interpretation and the relationship between church and state! We strongly protest the exclusion and vilification of ‘Evangelicals’ by the ‘Three Self’! We formally declare our complete break with the ‘Three Self’!” On July 9, the seminary cited the three undergraduates for jointly writing “Why We Are Leaving” and characterized the incident as a serious political event, deciding to expel them.

Within the Jinling Union Theological Seminary, some faculty members who held evangelical positions also became targets of purging for not agreeing with the theological thought construction. Among them was Wang Weifan, who had maintained a close relationship with Ding Guangxun for many years. He was reserved towards the proposal to “downplay justification by faith,” viewing this move as harmful to the “Evangelical” essence of the Chinese church. This tendency to “favor one particular viewpoint while neglecting, alienating, or even excluding others” was deemed detrimental to the broader goals of the Three Self Movement and national unity. Due to serious disagreements with Ding Guangxun over theological thought construction, Wang was forced into retirement from Jinling Seminary.

On June 7, 2000, Ding Guangxun announced the expulsion of Ji Tai during a conference, accusing him of utilizing his status as a teacher and pastor to engage in illegal religious activities. At the same time, the Two Meetings of Jiangsu Province also suspended Ji’s pastoral duties. On July 10, Ji Tai drafted a letter to the brothers and sisters in the Lord, responding to the school’s accusations and pointing out that the reasons for his expulsion were “opposition to the Three Self Movement” and “opposition to theological thought construction.”
