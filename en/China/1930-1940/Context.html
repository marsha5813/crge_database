China: Context (1930-1940)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. Church Schools Prohibit the Display of Religious Books and Newspapers in 1930 (Zhu Baochen 1930: [Wu] 110-111)

In July 1930, the Ministry of Education issued an order to education departments across provinces and cities, strictly prohibiting church schools from displaying religious books and illustrations in their libraries, “to prevent the intoxication of youth.” This order was based on a public letter No. 1468 from the Central Committee of the Kuomintang (Guomindang, 國民黨), which indicated that the Jiangsu Province Party Affairs Reorganization Committee’s propaganda department reported that the Wu County Party Affairs Reorganization Committee presented evidence that in various church schools’ libraries, “the display of religious books and newspapers is intoxicating youth’s thoughts, hence, we pray to forward this to the Central Government to instruct the Ministry of Education to strictly prohibit it, to prevent future troubles.” The ban stated that imperialism uses cultural propaganda as a tool for invasion, “confusing youth’s thoughts with poisonous plots of national destruction.” The people understood its scheme, and across the country there arose movements resisting the cultural invasion of imperialism. However, recently many church schools “again utilized libraries as places for students to research academics, displaying various religious books, newspapers, and illustrations, making this an intangible propaganda; conversely, party loyalty books were scant in number.” This situation of “reversing the main and the secondary,” “is truly not a phenomenon that schools under party governance should exhibit.” Therefore, at the 24th Committee meeting, it was decided to forward this to the Central Government for strict prohibition. Subsequently, all church schools “are to uniformly prohibit the display or hanging of any books promoting religion; regarding religious books, newspapers, and magazines, they may only be allowed in universities and senior high schools if related to elective subjects and philosophical reference materials, and otherwise should be uniformly prohibited.”

  1. “Guidelines for the Management of Foreign Missionary Groups” 1931 (Zhongguo Di’er Lishi档案馆 1994: 1029-1032)

In February 1931, the Central Public Training Department of the Kuomintang sent a proposal regarding the guidelines for Christian groups to the Central Executive Committee. The Central Training Department indicated that Christian organizations (such as various church buildings, youth organizations, etc.) are present across the country, “in the past, they have used religious propaganda for cultural invasion, causing significant issues. Although religious belief is allowed by our party, appropriate guidance from the party office is necessary, and the government should carry out effective supervision, using their propaganda to correct errors and strictly prevent cultural invasion.” Therefore, they drafted the following guidelines:

  • All Christian groups established in China, such as church buildings and youth organizations, shall be governed by these guidelines.
  • All local Christian groups shall receive guidance from the party office and supervision from the government.
  • Local Christian groups must not violate the propaganda of the Three Principles of the People.
  • Local Christian groups must submit regulations, staff resumes, and other documents to the local senior party office for registration within four months after the issuance of these guidelines; if there are branches, those branches must also register.
  • Local Christian groups may only apply to the government for approval after registering with the party office; if there are branches, those branches must also register.
  • Local Christian groups, besides regular meetings, must report in advance to the local senior party office when holding all types of conferences, and must report to the supervising authority for personnel supervision.
  • Local Christian groups operating schools, hospitals, and other ventures must respect the relevant laws and regulations.
  • Local senior party offices may investigate the affairs of local Christian groups and the status of their operations when necessary.
  • Local Christian groups that violate these guidelines shall be dissolved by the government according to the law.
  • These guidelines are enacted by the Central Executive Committee of the Kuomintang.

In June 1931, Chen Lifu 陳立夫, Secretary-General of the Secretariat of the Central Executive Committee of the Kuomintang, informed the Central Public Training Department that the Central Committee’s 131st regular meeting had reviewed relevant guidelines and made revisions, renaming the guidelines to “Guidelines for the Management of Foreign Missionary Groups”:

  • Any foreign missionary groups established within China, such as church buildings, shall be guided by these guidelines.
  • All foreign missionary groups must receive guidance from the party office and supervision from the government.
  • All foreign missionary groups must not violate the propaganda of the Three Principles of the People.
  • The general association of foreign missionary groups must submit their constitution, staff resumes, and other documents to the Central Party Office for registration within four months after these guidelines are enacted.
  • After registering with the Central Party Office, each foreign missionary group must seek filing with the local government.
  • Besides regular meetings, local senior party offices may send representatives when foreign missionary groups hold gatherings.
  • Foreign missionary groups operating schools, hospitals, and other ventures must be in compliance with relevant laws and regulations.
  • Any foreign missionary group that violates these guidelines shall be dissolved by the government according to the law.
  • These guidelines are enacted by the Central Executive Committee of the Kuomintang.
  1. Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China during the Period of Political Tutelage 1931 (Zhang Su 张甦 1933: 197-198)

The “Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China during the Period of Political Tutelage” is the highest legal code promulgated during the Political Tutelage period of the Republic of China before the Constitution came into effect.It was on May 12, 1931, that the National Assembly enacted this law, consisting of a total of 8 chapters and 89 articles. Among these, Article 11 in Chapter 2, “Rights and Duties of the People,” stipulates, “The people have the freedom to believe in religion.”

However, during the discussions on the constitutional amendments at the National Assembly in Nanjing, some individuals proposed that “religion is a tool of imperialists used to intoxicate youth, and should be limited,” advocating for an amendment to the article concerning the freedom of belief. Many were in agreement at that time, while Zhang Zhijiang (張之江) emphasized that freedom of religious belief “cannot be restricted,” “should not be restricted,” and “does not need to be restricted.” As a result, Zhang’s suggestion received majority support from the review committee.

  1. Outline of the Constitution of the Chinese Soviet Republic 1931 (He Husheng 2004: 49-50; Wang Jianlang and Huang Kewu 2021: 939)

In November 1931, the Central Committee issued a directive to the Central Soviet Area, stating in Article 13 of the “Key Principles of the Constitution”: “Guaranteeing the working masses have genuine freedom of belief and freedom of anti-religious propaganda.” Subsequently, the Outline of the Constitution of the Chinese Soviet Republic was passed by the Chinese Soviet Representative Congress, which established the religious policy in the Soviet area. Article 13 states: “The Chinese Soviet government aims to guarantee the genuine freedom of belief for the working farmers and laborers, and strictly implements the principle of separation of religion from government. All religions shall not receive any protection or financial support from the Soviet state, and all Soviet citizens have the freedom to engage in anti-religious propaganda. Imperialist churches may only exist under the compliance of Soviet laws.”

In December, the “Central Resolution on the Tasks and Current Work of the Red Trade Union in the Soviet Area” stipulated: “Monks, Taoists, pastors, and other religious leaders who work in the Soviet area cannot join trade union organizations; those who have joined should be expelled.”

  1. The Work of Christianity by the CCP under the Anti-Japanese United Front (Zhonggong Zhongyang 1991: 18; Liu Qun 1936: 146-147; Liu Shaoqi 1981: 63-64; Luo Fu 1991: 693-698, 715-718)

In April 1936, the CCP passed the “Declaration of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China for the Establishment of a National Anti-Japanese People’s Front” (April 25, 1936), reaching out to various groups, including the National Christian Youth Association, emphasizing that “regardless of our differing views and beliefs, and regardless of our past conflicts and struggles,” under the common demand of “fighting against Japan and saving the nation,” a “People’s Anti-Japanese Front” could be established.

In September 1936, the left-wing publication “Xian Shijie Bianhuikan” published an article by CCP member Liu Qun entitled “The New Awakening and New Path for Christian Youth.” Liu Qun noted that students from Yenching University actively engaged in the Beiping (Beijing) movement to save the nation, which had dispelled past misunderstandings against Christianity. Therefore, he urged more Christian youth to “reassess” their beliefs and, based on the Christian spirit of “justice, equality, and fraternity,” to actively participate in and support the national salvation movement. He particularly mentioned that youth associations and church schools could connect “youth from all factions” and undertake more responsibilities in the “common cause of saving the nation.”

It is clear that under the premise of establishing a united front for national defense, the CCP abandoned its previous opposition to Christianity and worked to unite Christians. However, at the same time, the CCP actively deployed underground work within Christianity. In 1937, Liu Shaoqi provided guidance on the CCP’s work in the “white area” under Kuomintang rule, mentioning the role of “gray groups”: “We should also utilize various gray groups to organize the masses and join existing legal organizations to carry out work. Our comrades should fight faithfully for the direct demands of the masses within these gray legal organizations, to win over the masses and gain leadership positions, without turning these groups into so-called ‘red groups,’ while maintaining their gray and legal appearances on the surface.” Undoubtedly, Christianity belonged to such “gray” groups.

From September to November the following year, the CCP convened the 6th Plenary Session of the 6th Central Committee. Zhang Wentian (Luo Fu) reported on the anti-Japanese national united front and organizational issues within the party, stressing the need to develop, expand, and consolidate youth organizations that were “progressive but not communist” across various regions. At the same time, he required party members to participate in all public activities as “active elements in saving the nation or ordinary members of the masses, without exposing themselves as party members or part of the organization,” to conduct prolonged and secretive work, and to develop party members and establish branches systematically. At the meeting, Chen Yun, Minister of the Central Organization Department and Secretary of the Youth Committee, clearly pointed out that secret work should be conducted through the Christian Youth Association in the rear.(6) The Executive Yuan Issued the “Foreign Church Leasehold Land Contract” in 1937 (Li Chuanbin 2011: 288-289; Shengjiao Zazhi 1937: 635)

In July 1937, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Ministry of the Interior, in conjunction with the Judicial Administration Department of the National Government, discussed amendments to the leasehold land contract for foreign churches and submitted it for approval to the Executive Yuan for implementation. This action was primarily due to discrepancies in contracts across regions, necessitating uniformity. Discussions began in June between the Ministry of the Interior, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and the Judicial Administration Department, and in July it was formally submitted to the Executive Yuan, which instructed various provinces and cities to enforce it.

The full text of the contract is as follows: “The landlord, [Name], is a [Status] in [Village or City] of [County Name]. Hereby, the following described land, (including any buildings listed), measuring [amount] acres [ a measure of land], is transferred. Based on the survey, the east boundary is [boundary], the west boundary is [boundary], the north boundary is [boundary], as determined by [Name]. The rental amount is [amount] yuan, and the landlord will submit this to the competent local authorities together with the lessee, according to the temporary regulations on the rental of land and buildings by foreign churches in the mainland, for permanent lease for the [Country] [specific religion] church to build a church or for other uses. The church shall not use this land for alteration or commercial purposes. All taxes due for this land shall be paid by the lessee as required. If in the future the lessee does not need this land, it may be transferred to another church of a contracting country for ongoing missionary work, or transferred to the people of China. The construction on this land shall be transferred as well. The landlord is not responsible for recovery. Both parties willingly enter into this contract. Landlord [Name], Lessee [Name], Intermediary [Name]. Signed on [Date].”

  1. Decision by the Military and Political Affairs Department Regarding Church Missionaries’ Exemption from Military Service (Li Chuanbin 2011: 290-291)

In 1938, M.V. Allworden, an American missionary from the China Inland Mission in Duchang County, Jiangxi Province, wrote to the county magistrate, hoping to secure exemption from military service for all personnel of the mission because its missionaries “were ordered to serve in the military.” The magistrate believed that the exemption for missionaries had no legal basis and that “it would create more problems,” hence he reported to the Jiangxi Provincial Government for a decision. The Jiangxi Provincial Government found that according to Article 27 of the “Implementation Regulations of Military Service Law,” already registered school teachers could be exempted, but “regarding church staff such as evangelists, preachers, and other social service members (e.g., Youth Association staff), there were no explicit regulations from the central government on exemptions or deferments; if they were required to serve, it could inevitably affect the conduct of church affairs, potentially violating the freedom of missionary work stipulated in treaties, and could lead to international disputes. Therefore, the central authorities reluctantly allowed for exemptions or deferments, although the central legal provisions did not specify this.” Consequently, the Jiangxi Provincial Government decided to seek a resolution from the Military and Political Affairs Department. The response from the Military and Political Affairs Department stated: already registered school teachers could be handled according to Article 27 of the Military Service Implementation Law, however, “since church staff do not perform government duties, it is not appropriate to grant deferments.”

  1. Religious Policy in the Border Areas during the War of Resistance (Wang Jianlang and Huang Kewu 2021: 941)

In January 1938, the “Resolution of the Military and Civilian Representative Conference of the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Area” stated: “The border area government should support the organization of people’s groups, allowing the people freedom of assembly, association, speech, publication, and religion and belief.” The “Policy Guidelines for the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Area during the War of Resistance,” passed in 1939, stipulated: “Without harming the sovereignty and principles of the border area, protect all people, industrialists, and believers sympathetic to China’s anti-Japanese efforts, in their activities in production, business, and cultural endeavors in the border area.”

  1. Liu Zhanyin, President of Hujiang University, Assassinated in 1938 (Li Yading n.d.)

Liu Zhanyin was the first Chinese president of Hujiang University. After his appointment in 1928, he advocated the slogan “to make Hujiang University more Chinese and more Christian.” After the September 18 Incident, he actively called for anti-Japanese resistance and invited prominent figures like Feng Yuxiang, Tao Xingzhi, and Li Gongpu to give speeches at Hujiang University. In 1935, he, along with 283 individuals from Shanghai’s cultural community, jointly issued the “Shanghai Cultural Community Declaration for National Salvation,” demanding the Nanjing government take up the fight against Japan and grant the people freedom of speech and assembly.

After the outbreak of the War of Resistance in 1937, Hujiang University, Dongwu University, and St. John’s University formed a United Church University, and Liu was elected chairman of the Shanghai National Salvation Association. After the Japanese forces occupied Nanjing, he led the establishment of the International Friendship Association to correspond with foreign diplomats in Shanghai on behalf of the Chinese government, promoting China’s anti-Japanese stance to governments and peoples worldwide. In November 1937, following the Japanese capture of Shanghai, the Shanghai International Settlement became an “island.” In 1938, the Japanese supported the establishment of a reorganized government in Nanjing, attempting to lure Liu Zhanyin to become the Minister of Education, which he refused. Due to his anti-Japanese position, he was considered a thorn in the side by the Japanese puppet regime.

On April 7, 1938, Liu was ambushed by gunmen assigned by the Japanese puppet regime at the intersection of Jing’an Temple Road and Dahua Road in Shanghai, resulting in his immediate death at the age of 42.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Opposition to Christianity 1930 (Min Guo Ri Bao (民国日报) 1930; Qing Hua Zhou Kan (清华周刊) 1930; Xu Baoqian (徐宝谦) 1930: 1-6; Lu Lu (鲁鲁) 1930: 121-132)

On June 30, 1930, Shanghai’s published a “Special Issue Against Christianity.” The editor pointed out in the “Foreword” of this issue that in response to the “Five-Year Movement” launched by the Chinese National Christian Council, which suggested that “to save China, one must believe in Christianity,” a decision was made to “begin the movement against Christianity” in the “Awakening” supplement. The special issue included articles such as “The Path of Christianity Towards Oblivion,” “Analysis of Chinese Christians,” “The Five-Year Movement,” and a declaration to “Eliminate the Christian Youth League.”

also published a “Special Issue to Eliminate Christianity,” which included 20 articles. In the “Preface,” it explicitly stated that analysis of religious consciousness would be grounded in “the dialectics of materialism,” and noted, “The success of eliminating Christianity relies much more on actual political struggle than on publicity work.” After reading the special issue, Xu Baoqian wrote “A Letter to the Tsinghua Anti-Christian League” in , indicating that understanding religion through materialism “unavoidably simplifies the issue.” Xu also disagreed with the notion that religion is a tool of invasion, pointing out that not only religion has been utilized by the state, but science, morality, and political education have also been exploited. While Christianity is indeed associated with treaties, it simultaneously “advocates conversion, upholds justice, and encourages public opinion to urge the government,” urging the Anti-Christian League to take note. Additionally, he refuted the claim that Christianity’s lack of revolutionary discourse serves as a “narcotic,” emphasizing that Christianity “advocates the reform of people’s hearts, which is in fact the greatest of revolutions.” “Social institutions… certainly require reform; however, if the hearts of the people do not change, then the change of institutions will ultimately be in vain.”

Xu’s article prompted a response from “Lu Lu” in , arguing that Xu lacks an understanding of dialectical materialism and suggesting he is “a tool of the church, thus cannot avoid defending Christianity.” It is evident that in the early 1930s, Christianity was still seen as a target. However, following the September 18 Incident, the nationalism directed at Britain and the United States began to be increasingly replaced by rising anti-Japanese sentiment.

  1. Petition to the Ministry of Education by National Churches 1930 (Zhu Baochen (朱宝琛) 1930: [Wu] 104-108)

In 1930, representatives from 15 religious denominations nationwide petitioned the National Government’s Executive Yuan Ministry of Education, requesting that based on the principle of religious freedom, churches be allowed to set up religious elective courses in schools and hold religious ceremonies in primary schools. Their rationale was as follows: the purpose of church-run education is to cultivate sound character and serve the country and society. The true value of education lies not only in transmitting knowledge and vocational training but also in stimulating students to appreciate art and moral ideals, “to awaken their natural sense of good and evil, and to promote the well-being of the populace.” Such appreciation, “can only be adequately cultivated through the highest doctrines of religion.” According to the principles of Christian love, equality, and freedom, as well as self-sacrifice for the benefit of others, “it is especially worthy of being a model for diligent students.” As for religious ceremonies, “they merely provide students with an opportunity for spiritual renewal.” Therefore, schools should continue to offer religious studies and ceremonies, “so that young students, through religious training, comprehend the true meaning of life and cultivate sincere and genuine character.”

The petition stated that in recent years there has been a notion among the public of “the separation of education and religion,” suggesting that churches should not use education as a tool for proselytizing, advocating for the abolition of church schools and the prohibition of teaching religion and conducting religious ceremonies. They argued, “If the purpose of religion is indeed to obscure intelligence, how then could it use education as a tool to propagate its doctrines?” In reality, church-run education, “is fundamentally based on the principle of imparting knowledge,” and the subject of religion does not contradict other sciences; rather, it seeks the overall development of young people’s characters, thus religion does not “shackle the freedom of thought.” Furthermore, the churches advocate for religious freedom, and therefore have never used schools to force or coerce students into joining the church. “Even with religious courses and ceremonies, it only aims to guide students to awaken their moral conscience.”

The petition also reiterated that the state should allow private schools to experiment with new educational ideals, rather than “insisting on a single educational theory and using political power to deprive private schools of the freedom to experiment with educational theories.” The religious curriculum and ceremonies at church schools represent an educational theory that the government should allow certain restrictions on freedom to facilitate experimentation.

Finally, the petition pointed out that the party constitution confirms the rights of the people to freedom of belief and freedom of speech, therefore the religious curriculum and rituals of church schools, “are purely a matter of conscience, not compelled participation by students, and belief is absolutely free; what need is there to impose restrictions?” The term “to persuade” is also ambiguously used and can lead to confusion.Representatives from 15 Religious Denominations: Fan Dingjiu (范定九) (China Christian Church), Luo Yunyan (羅運炎) (Methodist Church), Bao Zheqing (鮑哲慶) (China Baptist Church), Wu Deshi (吳德施) (Logan Herbert Roots, China Anglican Church), Ke Tingyuan (柯廷元) (China South Highway Church), Gu Hongchuang (顧宏創) (China Alliance Church), Wang Aitang (王爱棠) (Li Xian Hui), Liu Guiyi (劉貴義) (Methodist Church), Luo Renfu (羅仁輔) (Sui Zhen Hui), De Fu Lang (德輔廊) (China Holy Way Church North China District), Zu Qiwu (祖起舞) (China Lutheran Church), Sun Xiangfu (孫香甫) (All Shaanxi China Christian Baptist Church), Huang Qiming (黃啟明) (Liangguang Baptist), and Xiao Wenruo (蕭文若) (Sichuan Methodist Church).

On July 17, 1930, the Ministry of Education, in Document No. 366, responded to the petition with the following points: (1) The claim that “through religious teachings, one is molded in life” should “not be limited to one religion, especially not be merely a matter of form.” Therefore, setting up specific subjects limited to one religion and holding religious ceremonies “is merely formality, and does not constitute what is termed educational molding”; (2) If doctrines are imposed on students below junior high school, it would lead to them being “indoctrinated by one religion,” which “amounts to depriving them of their future freedom to choose religion, and is essentially a ‘shackling of freedom of thought’”; (3) Given that churches aim to popularize education, “there is no intent to use education to persuade or coerce students to join the church,” thus the restriction of “forbidding religious propaganda” does not contradict the founding purpose of institutions like this; (4) Religion “is a nebulous imagination, not a foundation for educational theory, thus should not be allowed experimental status.” In summary, the church’s petition “is difficult to approve…and should not be contested further.”

  1. The Spy Case of Italian Missionaries (Yang Weihua (楊衛華) 2020: 170-178)

In 1938, a shocking spy case occurred in Laohekou, Hubei, involving Italian Bishop Alfonso Maria Corrado Ferrani of the Catholic Diocese and Priest Li Jun, who were accused of acting as Japanese spies. This incident reflected the changing relations among China, Italy, and Japan against the backdrop of the Second Sino-Japanese War.

In 1935, Italy invaded Ethiopia, and China supported sanctions against Italy in reaction to Japan’s invasion of China, leading to a breakdown of Sino-Italian relations. In November 1937, during the Second Sino-Japanese War, Italy joined the Anti-Comintern Pact and recognized the puppet state of Manchukuo, adopting pro-Japanese policies and further deteriorating Sino-Italian relations.

Amid this context, a missionary of Liuzhou ancestry reported to the Italian Consulate in Guangzhou that local people were increasingly hostile towards them, spreading rumors that Italy was assisting Japan in the conflict. The Italian Consulate in Guangzhou informed the Guangdong provincial government, which issued an order prohibiting the spread of rumors. Fears of collusion with the enemy among Italian missionaries grew increasingly. In March 1938, Guangdong Provincial Chairman Wu Tiecheng reported that several Italian priests in Shaoguan and Lianyang were suspected of espionage and requested that they be advised to leave the country. However, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs rejected the request due to insufficient evidence. In February 1938, the commander of the Third War Zone, Gu Zhutong, telegraphed Chiang Kai-shek, hoping the Ministry of Foreign Affairs would inform all foreign ambassadors in China to order the escort of missionaries in the war zone to the rear, to prevent them from being used by the enemy. In April, Chiang instructed all war zones to have county magistrates conduct thorough investigations and tightly monitor Italian missionaries, cutting off any possibility of them communicating information to the enemy, though outwardly this should be done under the pretext of protection. Later, various reports indicated questionable conduct by Italian missionaries.

Hubei was a key mission area for Italian missionaries, making Italian missionaries a target of concern for military and political authorities. At that time, the commander of the Fifth War Zone stationed in eastern Hubei, Li Zongren, ordered all Italian missionaries in his jurisdiction to withdraw. Li also pressured two missionaries to leave, prompting the Italian embassy in China to approach the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The Ministry requested that the Military Commission clearly stipulate that it was unnecessary to order all Italian clergy to leave, in order to avoid diplomatic disputes. This was during the defense of Wuhan, intensifying the imagined threat of espionage by Italian missionaries.

In October 1938, Wuhan fell, and Li Zongren moved his headquarters to western Hubei. Northwestern Hubei was also a stronghold for Catholic missions, with the Northwestern Hubei Diocese established in 1870, and reconfigured as the Laohekou Diocese in 1924, managed by Italian Franciscans. By early 1941, there were 37 Italian missionaries there. In 1932, Ferrani was promoted to bishop. In September 1938, when the Third Route Air Command in Laohekou was sending messages, radio signals became confused, leading to an investigation that concluded the interference was from a secret radio transmitter in the Laohekou Catholic Church, which also indicated that the Italian bishop there punished students, teachers, and Chinese priests who participated in the anti-Japanese movement.

In November 1938, authorities arrested two staff members. Following Li Zongren’s consent, they conducted a surprise raid and seized a handgun, five boxes of ammunition, a radio receiver, and a radio transmitter. In December, Li reported to Chiang, stating that many German and Italian clergy were acting as spies for Japan, requesting guidance on handling the situation.Meanwhile, Li Zongren also supported the establishment of the Cultural Work Committee of the Fifth War Zone and set up a work station in Laohekou. On December 9, 1938, to commemorate the December 9 Movement, it was decided to hold a large anti-espionage parade surrounding the church, which became a public confrontation between the locals and the Italian priests. A public rally to denounce traitors was held, during which a petition was submitted to the National Government, asserting that there was solid evidence of espionage activities by the Italian priests and demanding strict punishment. The incident was widely reported by the and subsequently reprinted. The military publication , from the Nationalist Party in Chongqing, condemned Italian missionaries as “heartless and treacherous,” claiming they acted as Japanese spies and harmed the war effort, while calling on the public to hold an anti-espionage movement. Not only the Nationalist Party, but even the Chinese Communist Party utilized the Laohekou espionage case, emphasizing the need for comprehensive espionage prevention and vigilance against enemy scouts and traitors, advocating that the clergy should be dealt with accordingly.

Li Zongren demanded that the National Government take a hardline approach, but the Ministry of Foreign Affairs suggested that Chiang Kai-shek issue a telegraph to Li Zongren, instructing him to send the Italian priests along with the evidence to Chongqing, while advising caution to prevent igniting anti-Italian sentiments among the public. The event then entered the realm of Sino-Italian diplomatic negotiations, with the Italian embassy stressing to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Executive Yuan that it was a planned anti-Italian backlash. Executive Yuan member Kong Xiangxi also communicated with Li, stating that it was common for foreigners to possess firearms, radios, generators, etc., and called for a thorough investigation, implying that according to the Sino-Italian treaty, the matter should be referred to the Italian consulate for legal proceedings. Chiang Kai-shek also leaned towards handling the situation prudently to prevent escalation. Later, Bishop Ferrani, the central figure of the case, made a statement denying the espionage allegations. The Italian Ministry of Foreign Affairs also officially engaged in discussions with the Chinese embassy in Italy.

In January 1939, the Central Government dispatched Huang Jianping, from the military law enforcement bureau of the Fifth War Zone, to Laohekou, where he signed a peaceful resolution agreement with Bishop Ferrani, confirming that the clergy were not involved in espionage activities, and stating that the church and local charitable organizations were under the guidance of the Chinese government.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. Japan’s Invasion of China (Guo Tingyi (郭廷以) 1983: 626-636, 651-658; Wang Jianlang (王建朗), Huang Kewu (黃克武) 2021: 381-394)

From 1931 to 1935, Japan’s invasion of China can be divided into several significant phases, reflecting Japan’s ambitions for aggressive expansion:

The September 18 Incident (1931): The September 18 Incident marked the beginning of Japan’s invasion of China. On September 18, 1931, the Japanese Kwantung Army used the Liuqiaohu Incident as an excuse to accuse the Chinese army of blowing up a section of the South Manchuria Railway, subsequently launching military action. The Japanese quickly attacked Shenyang. Due to Chiang Kai-shek’s (“Jiang Jieshi” / 蔣介石) policy of “stabilizing internal affairs before resisting external threats” and the “non-resistance policy,” the Northeast Army made no effective resistance, and after Shenyang fell, the Japanese soon controlled the entire Northeast region. In 1932, Japan established the puppet state of Manchukuo (偽滿洲國) in the Northeast, creating a puppet regime represented by the deposed Emperor Puyi (清廢帝溥儀). This marked Japan’s complete incorporation of the Northeast into its sphere of influence, further expanding its aggressive actions in China.

The January 28 Incident (1932): Following the September 18 Incident, Japan’s ambitions for invasion further escalated, targeting Shanghai. On January 28, 1932, Japan launched an attack on Shanghai under the pretext of protecting its nationals. The 19th Route Army (十九路軍) in Shanghai fiercely resisted, leading to intense battles, particularly in the Zhabei (閘北) area, resulting in a war lasting over a month. This event is also known as the “Songhu Campaign” (淞滬抗戰). Despite the tremendous efforts of the Chinese army, they had to cease hostilities in March due to limited forces and international pressure, leading to the signing of the “Songhu Armistice Agreement” (《淞滬停戰協定》). This agreement stipulated the withdrawal of Chinese troops from certain areas of Shanghai, thereby effectively enhancing Japan’s influence in China.

The Rehe and Great Wall Campaigns (1933): In February 1933, Japan entered Rehe (熱河) under the pretext of pursuing the Northeast Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army, rapidly capturing Chengde (承德) and extending its aggression from the Northeast into North China. Rehe was subsequently incorporated into Manchukuo. Japan further continued its advance along the Great Wall, attacking areas including Xifengkou (喜峰口) and Gubeikou (古北口). Despite the nationalist government’s troops putting up a valiant resistance, they were ultimately forced to retreat due to the disparity in strength. After the Great Wall Campaign, the nationalist government was compelled to sign the “Tanggu Agreement” (《塘沽協定》), acknowledging Japanese control over Rehe and parts south of the Great Wall and allowing Japanese activities in North China.

Specialization in North China (1935): Following the September 18 Incident, the Japanese Kwantung Army considered controlling North China an essential step in expanding its control over China. In 1935, Japan coerced the nationalist government into signing the “He-Mei Agreement” (《何梅協定》), demanding the Kuomintang (國民黨) troops withdraw from Hebei (河北) and Chahar (察哈爾) and prohibiting Kuomintang activities in North China. In the same year, through oppression, Japan established the pro-Japanese “Jichahar Political Affairs Commission” (冀察政務委員會), led by local warlords such as Yin Rugeng (殷汝耕). Additionally, Japan supported the establishment of the autonomous government in Chahar centered around Zhangbei (張北), and the “Jidong Anti-Communist Autonomous Government” (冀東防共自治政府) centered in Tong County (通縣). These puppet regimes, while formally claiming autonomy, were under Japanese control, providing a political foundation for further infiltration into North China.

  1. The International Community’s Response to Japan’s Invasion of China (Iriye (入江) 1986: 504-509)

During Japan’s invasion of China in the 1930s, the international community’s response generally involved condemnation but limited action. Against the backdrop of the Great Depression and the rising tensions in Europe (the rise of Germany and Italy), countries tended to adopt a passive stance (appeasement) regarding Japan’s acts of aggression, failing to effectively halt Japan’s invasion.

After the September 18 Incident, China submitted a protest to the League of Nations, requesting an investigation into Japan’s actions in the Northeast. The League dispatched the Lytton Commission (李頓調查團) to investigate in China, which reported that Japan’s actions in the Northeast constituted aggression and did not recognize the legitimacy of Manchukuo. In 1933, the League passed a resolution demanding Japan withdraw from the Northeast. However, Japan was dissatisfied with this outcome and subsequently withdrew from the League, indicating its disregard for international opinion. As for the attitudes of Britain and the United States, they were not proactive. Britain maintained a wait-and-see attitude towards the Northeast incident, considering its own interests in Asia, and took no punitive measures against Japan. U.S. Secretary of State Henry L. Stimson expressed a position of “non-recognition” (Stimson Doctrine), stating that the U.S. would not recognize Japan’s illegal occupation in the Northeast but took no actual actions to stop Japan.

During the January 28 Incident, the League of Nations attempted to promote a ceasefire through diplomatic means. Countries like Britain and the U.S. played a certain mediating role in facilitating the signing of the “Songhu Armistice Agreement.” However, the agreement did not impose substantial punishment on Japan’s acts of aggression, merely providing a temporary relief from hostilities in the Shanghai area.The League of Nations once again expressed condemnation of Japan’s acts of aggression but failed to prevent further Japanese expansion. Due to the League’s lack of enforcement power, it could not compel member states to impose collective sanctions against Japan.

During the 1930s, the international community’s response to Japan’s invasion of China was mainly limited to diplomatic condemnation, lacking substantial punitive measures. Japan exploited the international community’s passive attitude, gradually expanding its acts of aggression from the Northeast to North China and eventually throughout all of China, leading to the outbreak of full-scale Sino-Japanese war. The international community’s inability to intervene also became one of the significant manifestations of the collapse of the global security system in the 1930s.

  1. The Anti-Japanese National Salvation Movement (Guo Tingyi (郭廷以) 1983: 660-666, 670-679; Zhang Yufa (張玉法) 2001: 367-377; Wang Jianlang (王建朗), Huang Kewu (黃克武) 2021: 376-379)

From the September 18 Incident (1931) to the Xi’an Incident (1936), the Kuomintang (國民黨) government implemented a “non-resistance policy” in response to Japanese aggression. This policy, guided by the principle of “stabilizing internal affairs before resisting external threats,” prioritized suppressing the Communist Party and postponed a comprehensive war against Japan. The non-resistance policy sparked widespread social discontent, with the national crisis growing increasingly severe. Students and intellectuals across the country initiated the anti-Japanese national salvation movement, demanding the Kuomintang government resist Japanese aggression.

After the September 18 Incident, the Chinese Communist Party (CPC) followed the directives from the Comintern given to the CPC Central Committee at the end of December, calling for the overthrow of the Kuomintang as a prerequisite for opposing imperialism in the national revolutionary war. They demanded the initiation of strikes, leadership of student movements, and a united front against imperialism and the Kuomintang.

During this period, the CPC launched proactive anti-Japanese national salvation movements, which primarily included extensive propaganda mobilization and calls for armed anti-Japanese actions. On September 20, 1931, the CPC Central Committee issued a declaration condemning the Japanese military’s invasion of Northeast China. On September 22, the CPC released a resolution regarding the Japanese military’s occupation of Manchuria, advocating for the organization of guerrilla warfare in the Northeast.

In 1932, the CPC established the “Central Soviet Area Anti-Imperialist Alliance” in Ruijin (瑞金), and in 1933 initiated the “National Self-Defense Rescue Association” in Shanghai, both focused on anti-Japanese objectives. In July 1935, the Comintern convened the Seventh World Congress in Moscow, deciding to form “People’s United Fronts” in various countries to engage in the struggle against imperialism and promote collaboration between the anti-Communist forces and various factions within China. CPC representative Wang Ming (王明) reported at the Congress that the CPC should establish an “anti-Japanese national united front.” In August, Wang, on behalf of the Central Committee and the Chinese Soviet Government, issued the “August 1st Declaration,” emphasizing that “as long as the Kuomintang army stops attacking the Soviet area, and as long as any troops engage in the war against Japan, regardless of any past grievances with the Red Army or differences on domestic issues, the Red Army will not only immediately cease hostilities, but is also willing to join forces closely to save the nation together.” The release of the August 1st Declaration occurred while the CPC was on the “Long March,” avoiding the Kuomintang’s suppression of communism.

In December, students in Tianjin (天津), Nanjing (南京), Shanghai (上海), and Beiping (北平) successively petitioned, creating a national wave of the anti-Japanese movement. Between 1935 and 1936, various anti-Japanese organizations were established across the country, such as the “Student National Salvation Federation” in Beiping, the “Pioneer Team for National Liberation,” in Tianjin the “Student National Salvation Federation” and “Patriotic Vanguard,” and in Shanghai the “Student National Salvation Federation,” the “Cultural Circle for National Salvation,” and the “National All-Sector Salvation Federation.”

In July 1936, G. D. Mikhailov, the General Secretary of the Comintern, stated that the CPC had no choice but to cooperate with Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石). The urgent task was to initiate an anti-Japanese united front that would compel Chiang to accept the basis for a united front against Japan and coexist with the CPC. On August 15, the Comintern officially instructed the CPC that it should not conflate the Kuomintang, Chiang Kai-shek, and the Japanese invaders, as the primary enemy of the Chinese people was Japanese imperialism, and everything should currently be subordinated to anti-Japanese efforts. Therefore, it was never acceptable to simultaneously oppose the Japanese invaders and Chiang, nor to consider the entire Kuomintang and Chiang’s military as allies of Japan. The Comintern demanded the CPC formally propose to the Kuomintang and Chiang Kai-shek to immediately cease military actions and sign a joint anti-Japanese agreement for negotiation.

Following the directives from the Comintern, on August 25, the CPC Central Committee issued a letter to the Kuomintang, urging it to “immediately stop the civil war, organize a national anti-Japanese united front, and launch a sacred national self-defense war.” In September, Mao Zedong (毛澤東) sent letters to Cai Yuanpei (蔡元培), Song Qingling (宋慶齡), Li Jishen (李濟深), Li Zongren (李宗仁), Bai Chongxi (白崇禧), and others, calling on them to leverage their influence to encourage the Kuomintang to change its course. He also wrote to Chiang, requesting “to turn enemies into friends and unite to resist Japan.”The Chinese Communist Party (CPC) raised the banner of “anti-Japanese resistance,” but the Kuomintang (KMT) government continued to actively pursue a policy of “internal stability.” By October 1936, the CPC’s base in Shaanxi was precarious, particularly as Japanese forces invaded Suiyuan. Mao Zedong (毛澤東) once again urged Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石) to cease the civil war and unify against Japan. In November, the “Seven Arrests Incident” in Shanghai involving the National Salvation Association caused a tremendous uproar. In December, Zhang Xueliang (張學良), the vice commander of the anti-bandit operations in the Northwest, suddenly kidnapped Chiang Kai-shek in Xi’an, demanding the cessation of the civil war and the inclusion of all factions to convene a National Salvation Conference, an event known as the “Xi’an Incident” (西安事變). Following the Xi’an Incident, Chiang halted the suppression of communism. In January 1937, the CPC, located in Yan’an (延安), sent a telegram to the KMT, indicating that it would cease the armed rebellion aimed at overturning the KMT government nationwide, renaming the Soviet government as the Special Region of the Republic of China (中華民國政府特區) and the Red Army as the National Revolutionary Army (國民革命軍), directly under the guidance of the military commission of the central government in Nanjing. In February, Chiang made a public speech suggesting the opening of discussions and accepted the central call for an “anti-Japanese national united front,” leading to a united front against Japan popularly referred to as the “Second United Cooperation of the KMT and CPC.”

During this period, the CPC first established a Soviet regime in Jiangxi (江西) from 1931 to 1934, implementing land reforms to attract peasant support, while promoting the slogan “Down with Japanese Imperialism.” Later, under Chiang’s full-scale suppression of the Communist Party, the CPC undertook the Long March (1934-1935), ultimately establishing its base in Shaanxi’s Yan’an.

  1. The European War and China in 1939 (Guo Tingyi (郭廷以) 1983: 705-707)

On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland, prompting Britain to change its previous policy of appeasement towards Germany’s aggressive actions, and the United States also abandoned its isolationist stance. On September 3, both Britain and the U.S. declared war on Germany, officially marking the start of World War II.

In August 1939, the Soviet Union and Germany signed the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (德蘇互不侵犯條約), agreeing not to attack each other and secretly dividing their spheres of influence in Eastern Europe, which allowed Germany to engage comfortably in conflict with Britain and the U.S. In 1941, the Soviet Union signed a neutrality treaty with Japan, recognizing Manchukuo (滿洲國), while Japan acknowledged the Mongolian People’s Republic, facilitating Japan’s southern expansion.

On May 10, 1940, Germany officially invaded France, entering Paris on June 14 and compelling France to sign an armistice on June 22. Northern France was occupied by Germany, while a pro-German Vichy government (維希政府) was established in the south. In September 1940, Japan joined the Axis powers, signing the Tripartite Pact (《三國同盟條約》), aimed at establishing a military and political alliance against Britain and the United States, marking a further consolidation of the fascist camp and having a profound impact on the global situation. The Vichy government collaborated with Japan, allowing Japanese troops to station in French Indochina, which not only placed severe pressure on China’s war of resistance but also enabled Japan to more effectively block aid to China.

With the outbreak of the European war and Japan’s entry into the Axis powers, the United States began to gradually adjust its policies. U.S. assistance for China’s resistance increased, such as through the Lend-Lease Act, which indirectly provided military supplies to China. This not only helped to curb Japan but also aimed to utilize China as a counterbalance to Japan’s power.

The outbreak of the European war transformed China’s anti-Japanese resistance from a localized conflict into a part of the global antifascist war, presenting both challenges and opportunities for China’s struggle. Influenced by multiple international forces, China’s war further integrated into the broader context of global warfare, becoming a crucial component of World War II.
