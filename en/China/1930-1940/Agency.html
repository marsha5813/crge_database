China: Agency (1930-1940)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Chia Yu Ming (賈玉銘, 1880-1964), whose courtesy name is De Xin (德新) and self-styled as Xing Wu (惺悟), was from Chang Le County in Shandong. He was a third-generation Christian and was baptized at a young age. He studied at the Dengzhou Literary Association established by American Presbyterian missionary Calvin Mateer (狄考文). During his time there, he resolved to dedicate his life to preaching. After graduating from the association in 1901, he completed his studies at the seminary in 1904 and took on the work of preaching in An Qiu, Weixian.

In 1907, Chia was ordained as a pastor and in 1908, he transferred to serve at the Presbyterian Church in Yizhou, Shandong. During this period, he actively participated in the church unity movement, including the Shandong Christian Union and the National Association of Chinese Presbyterians. In 1916, he was appointed a professor at Jinling Theological Seminary, which was co-founded by the Southern Presbyterian Church, the Northern Presbyterian Church, the Disciples of Christ, the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, and the Methodist Episcopal Church, North, reflecting Chia’s commitment to unity. Later, when the Presbyterian Church discussed joining the Chinese Christian Church, Chia was also involved. While in Nanjing, he founded the “Spirit Light News” (《靈光報》), which promoted “Upholding Truth, Developing the Spiritual Life of Believers.”

However, with the emergence of disputes between old and new theologies, the unity movement faced challenges. Fundamentalists sought to maintain the purity of their faith by refusing to cooperate with modernists and even withdrawing from unified organizations. At that time, a conservative faction within the Presbyterian Church refused to join the Chinese Christian Church, of which Chia was a part. In 1922, he criticized the new theology for undermining unity and called upon those with strong faith to be the pillars of truth. That same year, Chia left Jinling Theological Seminary and, upon the invitation of Watson M. Hayes (赫士), joined the North China Theological Seminary in Teng County, Shandong, which maintained fundamentalist beliefs.

In 1929, individuals from the North China General Assembly initiated the founding of the League of Christian Churches (基督教聯合會) and held a conference in December of that year where it was unanimously agreed not to join the Chinese Christian Church made up of Presbyterians, Congregationalists, and Baptists, and to organize the League of Christian Churches. Chia actively participated and served as an editorial committee member for the league’s publication “Morning Light” (《晨光》). In 1935, he was re-elected as the president of the league.

In 1930, Chia accepted an invitation to serve as the president of Jinling Women’s Theological Seminary. Aside from teaching, he was frequently invited to lead various gatherings in churches across the country. In 1936, when Jinling Women’s Theological Seminary merged into Jinling Theological Seminary, Chia resigned from his position to focus on preparing for the “Chinese Christian Spiritual Academy.” In October of that year, the Spiritual Academy officially opened in Nanjing with its charter stating: “The school’s holy work looks to the Lord; we will not solicit donations or incur debts, living by faith.” The aim was to cultivate preachers who wholeheartedly devote themselves and live by faith.

With the outbreak of the war and the fall of Nanjing, the Spiritual Academy was forced to suspend operations. Chia followed the government inland and in 1939, he resumed the Spiritual Academy in Gu County, Sichuan. The following year, it moved to Chengdu, and finally in 1941, it settled in Huangjiao Ya, Nanshan, Chongqing. After the war ended, he handed over the Sichuan Spiritual Academy to the Western China Spiritual Work Team and returned to Nanjing to establish the Chinese Christian Nanjing Spiritual Academy. In 1949, it moved again to Shaanxi Road in Shanghai.

In 1948, Chia was invited to attend the World Evangelical Conference in the Netherlands, where he was elected as the vice president of the International Council of Christian Churches (萬國基督教聯合會). While passing through Singapore on his way back home, many advised him to stay in Southeast Asia. However〉and “Promote the Three-Self Patriotic Movement of Christianity, Cutting Off All Relations with American Imperialism.” In July 1954, the National Conference of Chinese Christianity was held in Beijing, and Chia Yu Ming (賈玉銘) was elected as one of the 34 members of the presidium.

In 1954, the “Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee of Chinese Christianity” was established, with Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗) serving as its chairman. The meeting advocated “Great Unity,” and the spiritually-oriented Chia became one of the six vice-chairmen; however, he did not attend, and Yang Shaotang (楊紹唐) represented him. Because Chia was willing to participate in the Three-Self movement, his Shanghai Spiritual Academy was spared from merging. In 1956, the Shanghai Spiritual Academy merged with the Chinese Christian Ministerial Training Institute, the Huai’en Evening Theological College, and the Shanghai Bible Society to form the Chinese Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Academy, where Chia continued to serve as the president. During the Anti-Rightist Campaign in 1957, Chia was classified as a “designated rightist.” At the Second National Conference of Chinese Christianity held in November 1960, Chia was again elected as the vice-chairman of the Second Committee of the Three-Self Patriotic Movement of Chinese Christianity. He passed away in Shanghai on April 12, 1964, at the age of 84.

Chia Yu Ming was a prolific writer, having published works such as “New Dialogues” (《新辯惑》, 1923), “Lectures on Isaiah” (《以賽亞書講義》, 1924), “Spiritual Daily Devotions” (《靈修日課》, 1925), “New Lectures on Romans” (《羅馬新講義》, 1925), “Lectures on Hebrews” (《希伯來講義》, 1926), “Pastoral Theology” (《教牧學》, 1926), “Methods of Preaching” (《宣道法》, 1926), “The Vision of Patmos” (《拔摩異象》, 1928), “Lectures on the Gospel of John” (《約翰福音講義》, 1929), “Sermons on the Song of Songs” (《雅歌講題》, 1930), “Sermons on Fifty-Two Spiritual Journeys” (《五十二靈程講題》, 1930), “Theology” (《神道學》, 1949), “Complete Salvation” (《完全救法》, 1949), and “Essentials of the Bible” (《聖經要義》). He also made significant contributions to writing Christian hymns. For the needs of spiritual gatherings, he edited and published hymnals such as “Spiritual Fellowship Hymns” (《靈交詩歌》, 1930), “Victory Hymns” (《得勝詩歌》, 1938), and “Voices of the Saints” (《聖徒心聲》, 1943), many of which he composed himself.

  1. Fan Tianxiang (范天祥, Bliss M. Wiant, 1895-1975) was born in Clark County, Ohio, USA. His father, William Allen Wiant, served as the head pastor of Central Methodist Church in Springfield, Ohio, from 1911 to 1917.

In 1914, after graduating from Springfield South High School, Fan entered Wittenberg College (Ohio Wesleyan University) that same year. During World War I, he was drafted and served in the United States Navy, completing his university studies in 1920. During his college years, he aspired to missionary work in China and received a calling to compile a Chinese hymnbook.

After marrying Mildred K. Artz in 1922, the couple set off for Beijing, China, where Fan established the music department at the newly founded Yenching University. His wife taught voice there. In 1925, after the death of Sun Yat-sen, Fan was selected to serve as the organist for the funeral. In 1927, he formed the Yenching University Choir, and in May 1928, they performed Handel’s “Messiah” for the first time, which later became a Christmas tradition at the university until 1950. From 1928 to 1929, he returned to the United States to study for a year at Harvard University’s music department.

While at Yenching University, Fan arranged Chinese classical and folk melodies with harmonies in Western style, collaborating with the dean of the university’s divinity school, Zhao Zichan (趙紫宸), to publish “The Fellowship Hymnal” (《團契聖詩集》) and “The People’s Hymnal” (《民眾聖詩集》) in 1931. His exploration and adaptation of local melodies, combined with Zhao’s lyrics, laid the foundation for indigenizing Christian hymns in China.

In 1936, Fan participated in editing “Universal Praise,” a hymnbook collaboratively produced by six major church associations in China, which included 72 hymns with Chinese melodies, 46 of which were related to Fan. From 1935 to 1936, his family returned to the U.S. for vacation. He continued his studies at Boston University’s music school, obtaining a master’s degree. Upon returning to Yenching, he published a foreign-language choral anthology, “A Choral Anthology from a Millenium of Music,” in 1937. In April of that year, the Yenching University Choir was invited by the Nationalist Government to perform “Messiah” in Nanjing, which was broadcast nationwide. In 1940, he published the “Yenching Musical Collection.”

From 1941 to 1942, during another return to the U.S. for vacation, Fan studied sacred music at Union Theological Seminary in New York.After the outbreak of the Pacific War, Fan Tianxiang (范天祥) stayed in the United States with his family. During this time, he taught at Scarritt College and pursued a doctorate in music at Peabody College. In 1946, he completed his dissertation titled “The Character and Function of Music in Chinese Culture.”

From 1948 to 1951, the family returned to China amidst the Chinese Civil War. Fan was dissatisfied with the autocracy of the Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石) regime, felt repulsed by American support for Chiang, and sympathized with students opposing the civil war. In June 1948, he took the position of financial director at Yenching University, even temporarily serving as acting president. Although he was inevitably concerned about the future situation, some of his Chinese colleagues at Yenching, such as Lu Zhiwei (陸志韋), were confident in the Communist Party. By the end of 1948, hearing that the Communist Party would treat Yenching well, he grew optimistic, firmly believing that he could still bear witness for God. After the liberation of Beijing, Yenching University continued to operate as usual, further boosting his optimism for the future. However, the space for church universities increasingly narrowed, and issues surrounding American imperialism after the Korean War led to worsening conditions. In early 1951, Yenching University was officially taken over by the government, prompting Fan to decide to leave. He left Beijing on April 21, traveling through Tianjin, Qingdao, Hong Kong, Taipei, and then back to the United States via Europe.

From 1953 to 1955, Fan served as the pastor of St. Paul’s Methodist Church in Delaware; from 1955 to 1957, he was responsible for music ministry in Youngstown; and from 1957 to 1963, he served as the music director for various national and Ohio church organizations. From 1963 to 1965, he came to Hong Kong to work as a music professor at Chung Chi College while also leading an Asian music project for the National Council of Churches in the U.S., teaching at several seminaries in Southeast Asia.

Fan Tianxiang passed away in Ohio in 1975 at the age of 80.

  1. Yang Yinliu (楊蔭瀏, 1899-1984), courtesy name Rujing (如靖), also known as Liangqing (亮卿), was born in Wuxi, Jiangsu Province. He received traditional Chinese education from a young age, studying Chinese classics, history, poetry, and music theory in a private school from the age of six. He showed a strong interest in music early on, learning traditional Chinese instruments such as the sheng, xiao, di, and huqin from the age of seven. At around ten years old, Yang’s musical talent was recognized by American Episcopal missionary Louise S. Hammond (郝路義), who encouraged him to study piano and Western music theory, laying the foundation for his Christian music composition. After graduating from Donglin Primary School in Wuxi in 1915, he spent another year in private tutoring to review ancient texts before entering Wuxi Third Normal School, from which he graduated with excellent grades at the age of 21. In 1920, Yang was baptized and joined the Episcopal Church.

In 1923, Yang enrolled at St. John’s University in Shanghai to study economics, later switching to English literature, during which he served as the president of the Guoyue (National Music) Society. After the “May Thirtieth Incident” in 1925, he withdrew from school due to dissatisfaction with the administration and transferred to Guanghua University. While studying there, his research on Chinese music attracted attention, and he won first prize in an English essay competition with “An Outline History of Chinese Music.” Due to financial difficulties, his studies were interrupted, but he actively engaged in education and music research. After leaving Shanghai, he returned to Wuxi to work as a teacher in a middle school.

In 1928, the National Assembly of the Chinese Episcopal Church passed a resolution to edit a general hymnal for various Episcopal congregations, later named “The Hymn Book of Praise” (《頌主詩集》). Based on a recommendation from Hammond, Yang was hired as a full-time member to participate in the editing work. The hymn book was published in April 1931. At this time, other denominations also expressed interest in compiling hymnals and planned to collaborate. Thus, a joint committee consisting of the Chinese Christian Church, the Chinese Episcopal Church, the Methodist Episcopal Church, the Northern Chinese Congregational Church, the Eastern Baptist Church, and the Methodist Church was formed to prepare for the editing of a unified hymn compilation. Hammond again recommended Yang as a committee member, and he later became the general secretary of the editorial board, responsible for translation, editing, and composition. In 1932, he went to Beijing to collaborate with Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) at Yenching University on the editing work. In the winter of 1933, he assisted Liu in editing “Zijing” and served as associate editor. Starting in 1934, he contributed a column on religious music theory titled “Hymns and Sacred Music” to “Truth and Life” (《真理與生命》), co-editing with Liu Tingfang.In 1936, “Universal Praise” (《普天頌讚》) was published. Yang Yinliu (楊蔭瀏) collaborated with Dr. Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) in translating and revising 210 hymns, with Yang translating 150 of them and composing melodies for 13. These works laid the foundation for the development of Chinese Christian music.

Yang’s use of elements from traditional Chinese music was particularly prominent. He adapted ancient Chinese songs such as “Yangguan Sandie” (《陽關三疊》) into hymns, imbuing them with Christian significance using techniques like harmony and counterpoint. His compositions not only showcased the fusion of Eastern and Western music but also reflected a profound cultural cultivation and religious faith.

From 1936 to 1937, Yang served as a music researcher at the Harvard Yenching Institute in Beijing and taught Chinese music history at Yenching University. In the 1940s, he held professorships in the National Music Academy’s National Music Department and at Jinling Women’s University. After the end of the War of Resistance in 1945, he moved with the National Music Academy to Nanjing, where he received an invitation from Harvard University to conduct research on Chinese music and literature in the United States. However, he declined, believing that the “living treasury of Chinese music” resided in the folk traditions of China, and that it should be studied locally.

After the establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, Yang joined the Central Conservatory of Music as a professor. He also served as an executive director of the Chinese Musicians Association. Following the outbreak of the Cultural Revolution, he was sent to a cadre school in Hebei and was later transferred back to Beijing in 1972. In 1973, he suffered from a cerebral spasm, which resulted in declining health. Around 1980, he served as an advisor at the Chinese Academy of Arts. Yang Yinliu passed away in Beijing on February 25, 1984, at the age of 84.

Yang Yinliu is considered one of the most important music historians and theorists in 20th-century China. He made outstanding contributions to the study of Chinese music and the translation and composition of Christian hymns.

  1. Zhang Xuegong (張學恭, 1897-1962), courtesy name Zhongwen (仲溫), was born in a poor rural area of Weixian, Shandong Province. At that time, American Presbyterian missionary Grace Mary Rowley (李恩惠) came to Weixian to preach the gospel to the Zhang family. First, his grandmother converted, followed by other family members. Zhang Xuegong participated in the Presbyterian Church from a young age and was guided by Rowley, establishing a solid foundation of faith.

In 1912, Zhang enrolled in Guangwen Academy and later, with Rowley’s support, attended Shandong Christian University (which was later renamed Qilu University). After graduating, he was called to preach, attending seminary for a year in Qilu before transferring to the North China Theological Seminary. After graduating in 1924, he first preached in Guangrao, and then at the invitation of Watson Hayes (赫士), he taught at the North China Theological Seminary. In 1927, he briefly served as the principal of Weixian Wenmei Middle School. In 1928, he went to Princeton Theological Seminary in the United States for further theological training and also attended courses at Moody Bible Institute in Chicago. During his time in the U.S., he completed his first missionary work, “Personal Evangelism.”

In 1930, Zhang returned to teach at the North China Theological Seminary. In 1934, he became vice president, assisting Hayes, and in 1937, he took over as the second president. After the Pacific War began, the Japanese military closed the seminary, and all foreign teachers were detained in internment camps. Facing threats from the Japanese military, Zhang insisted on maintaining the operation of the seminary. He not only led students and church members to overcome difficulties but also successfully protected girls in the orphanage from harm.

In the winter of 1945, after years of war, the seminary’s buildings and equipment were severely damaged, and Zhang was forced to temporarily relocate in Xuzhou for the following spring to maintain operations. However, he was already planning to establish Taidong Theological Seminary in Nanjing, thus resigning from the presidency of the North China Theological Seminary in April 1946.

In May 1949, Zhang moved the Taidong Theological Seminary from Huangnigang in Nanjing to the south via Changsha, eventually relocating to Guilin, Guangxi, by winter, and establishing a chapel (Longzhu Road Chapel) for the faculty and students to gather for worship. By the end of 1950,On September 22-23, 1956, the first representative meeting of the Guilin Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was held, with Zhang Xuegong (張學恭) serving as the chairman (編纂委員會, 1997: 3212).

Christian Population in China (Ying 2009: 75)

Regarding the statistics of communicants in China during the 1930s, the only available figures are from 1933 (488,539), 1935 (512,873), and 1936 (536,089). After 1937, China fell into the situation of the Anti-Japanese War, with the country divided into occupied regions and free areas, making it difficult to gather such statistics.

According to Charles L. Boynton (鮑引登) in the 1936 Handbook of the Christian Movement in China Under Protestant Auspices, he noted that in the mid-1930s, there were 430,000 members across 12 denominations (Chinese Christian Church (中華基督教會), China Inland Mission (中國內地會), Methodist Episcopal Church (美以美會), Southern Baptist Convention (美南浸信會), Chinese Anglican Church (中華聖公會), Chinese Lutheran Church (中華信義會), Methodist Church (循道公會), Seventh-Day Adventist Church (復臨安息日會), North China Congregational Church (華北公理會), Church of the Nazarene (監理會), American Baptist Convention (南), and Basel Mission (巴色差會)). He estimated that the total number of communicants in the country should be close to 500,000. Daniel H. Bays (裴士丹), a scholar of the history of Christianity in China, pointed out that Boynton’s 500,000 did not include several indigenous Chinese sects and independent churches (such as the True Jesus Church (真耶穌教會), the Christian Assembly (基督徒聚會處), and the China Jesus Church (中國耶穌教自立會)), estimating that the omitted number may be as high as 200,000.

It is noteworthy that due to the September 18 Incident (九一八事變) in 1931, when the three northeastern provinces (Liaoning (遼寧), Heilongjiang (黑龍江), and Jilin (吉林)) were established as Manchukuo under Japanese support in 1932, surveys by missionaries in the 1930s excluded these provinces. Missionary M. Searle Bates (貝德士) indicated that if the approximately 31,000 Christians in the three northeastern provinces were included, along with members of indigenous Chinese sects and independent churches, the number of communicants in 1936 should amount to 623,000. In other words, by the outbreak of the war, the number of Christians in China had reached 700,000, marking a 56.7% increase compared to 1928’s figure of 446,631 communicants. Comparing this with the increase of 9.8% in communicants from 1920 to August 1928, it is evident that the development of Christianity in China had moved beyond the impacts of the anti-Christian movement, achieving notable growth even in the face of the Japanese threat in the 1930s.

After the July 7 Incident (七七事變) in 1937, intense fighting broke out between Japan and China in Shanghai in August. Faced with a large number of injured soldiers, the National Revolutionary Army could not provide timely rescue. Premier Kong Xiangxi (孔祥熙) acknowledged that the government had failed to address the problem of wounded soldiers and called on churches to provide assistance. During his journey, Kepler witnessed the lack of adequate medical care for a large number of wounded soldiers and the insufficient organization of their transportation. Consequently, he advocated for the establishment of a service organization for the wounded within Christian communities.

With Kepler’s efforts, the National Christian Church Assembly decided to initiate wounded soldier services, forming the “National Christian Service Council for Wounded Soldiers in Transit.” Key participants at the time included A. R. Kepler, Fan Dingjiu (范定九), Zhang Bohuai (張伯懷), and Geng Yuanxue (耿元學). After the establishment of the council, Zhang Bohuai was sent to Zhengzhou, Kaifeng, and Gui De to prepare for operations, communicating with church leaders as well as local military officials and other charitable organizations.

Zhang Bohuai and Geng Yuanxue served as regional coordinators, while A. R. Kepler was appointed director of the general office.

Concurrently, the council conducted evangelistic outreach in rear hospitals or led soldiers to churches, seeking opportunities to share the gospel with the wounded.

Student Relief Committee of Chinese YMCA and Student Service Center 基督教青年會學生救濟委員會與基督教學生公社, 1938 (左芺蓉2005: 284-292; Mulready-Stone 2018: 143-160; 全國學生救濟委員會1943: 30-35)

Since the full-scale resistance began in 1937, a large number of students fled the Japanese-occupied areas, becoming refugees. In 1937, the YMCA and the Young Women’s Christian Association jointly initiated “Student Relief Work in Extraordinary Times,” first establishing the “Shanghai Student Relief Committee” to assist refugee students from Beijing and Tianjin. Besides Shanghai, student relief committees were also established in Beijing, Fuzhou, Xi’an, Wuchang, Hankou, Changsha, Guangzhou, and Guilin.

In March 1938, the national associations of the male and female YMCA decided to establish the “National Student Relief Committee” in Shanghai, chaired by Li Zhaohuan (黎照環), the president of Shanghai Jiaotong University (J. Usang Ly), with Liang Xiaochu (梁小初) as the executive secretary, and Jiang Wenzhen (江文漢), a secretary from the national committee’s student department, along with Shi Baozhen (施葆真), the executive secretary of the Young Women’s association’s student department, as administrative secretaries. After the outbreak of the Pacific War in December 1941, the committee temporarily relocated to Chengdu, and then to Chongqing in September 1942. During this time, the number of student relief committees across the country increased to 28.

Simultaneously, for student outreach at remote national universities, the National Christian Council cooperated with both the male and female YMCA to establish the “Evangelism among Government University Students” committee.

At that time, the refugee students included three categories: (1) those who continued their studies at their original locations or moved to safer areas; (2) those who could not study due to the war and enlisted; (3) those who participated in anti-Japanese rescue work or found employment in the rear areas.The work of the Student Relief Committee primarily targeted the first category of students. Its main focus was to raise funds domestically and internationally to alleviate the plight of students: (1) to establish work-study opportunities to provide financial support and prevent students from dropping out; (2) to provide assistance for refugee students to return to their hometowns or move inland for their studies; (3) to arrange accommodations for refugee students and those studying in the rear areas, and to organize activities; (4) to arrange short-term jobs for temporarily out-of-school students. The funding for student relief primarily came from abroad, such as international organizations like the International Student Service, the Far-East Student Emergency Fund, and the Far-East Student Service Fund.

To support the refugee students, the Student Relief Committee established “Christian Student Service Centers” (also known as “Christian Student Communities”) at national universities in remote areas, which also served local church-affiliated universities. By 1943, a total of 12 student service centers or communities had been established: (1) Chenggu, Shaanxi (National Northwest United University, National Northwest Normal University); (2) Jiading, Sichuan (National Wuhan University, Central Arts and Crafts School); (3) Shapingba, Chongqing (National Central University, Chongqing University, Sichuan Education Institute, Nankai High School); (4) Zunyi and Meitan, Guizhou (National Zhejiang University); (5) Liangfeng, Guangxi (National Guangxi University); (6) Pingshi, Guangdong (National Sun Yat-sen University); (7) Guietou, Guangdong (Guangdong College of Arts and Sciences); (8) Huaxi, Chengdu (Huaxi University, Jinling University, Qilu University, Jinling Women’s College); (9) Majia Miao, Hanzhong (National Northwest Medical University); (10) Santai, Sichuan (National Northeast University); (11) Kunming, Yunnan (National Southwest United University, National Yunnan University); (12) Gulu Ba, Shaanxi (National Northwest Institute of Technology).

The student service centers (or communities) mainly provided collective activity spaces for students, where they could sing, play games, study or conduct research, discuss various life issues, and even receive vocational training. Given the limitations of the campus environment during wartime, these centers became the primary leisure and activity hubs for students. Each service center or community had student leaders responsible for activities, and students could also take on responsibilities themselves, fostering a spirit of service among the students.

After the victory in the War of Resistance, universities gradually returned to their original locations, and the student communities continued to serve students at various universities. The “Evangelism among Government University Students” committee was renamed the “National University Religious Work Committee.” By 1949, there were still eight student communities in the country, located at Tsinghua University in Beijing, Northeast University in Shenyang, Jinling Women’s University in Nanjing, Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou, Zhejiang University in Hangzhou, the student community at Luojia Mountain in Wuchang, the student community at Shapingba in Chongqing, and the student community at Huaxi in Chengdu.

From the wartime period to afterwards, student communities remained targets for infiltration by the underground Communist Party. For example, the famous underground pastor Li Chuwen (李儲文) worked at the student service center in Kunming during the War of Resistance.

National Federation of Student Christian Unions 全國基督教學聯協會, 1939 (張麗霞2018: 28-63)

The Chinese Christian student movement began to take shape in 1922, when a proposal was passed at the eleventh conference of the World Student Christian Federation (世界基督教學生同盟) in Beijing to establish a Christian student movement in China. Subsequently, the male and female YMCA and the student voluntary evangelism movement collaborated to organize it. Based on local school YM/YWCAs and student fellowships, the Preparatory Committee for the Chinese Christian Student Movement was established in July 1927 at Jinling Women’s University in Nanjing. The committee defined the movement’s goals as “to embody the spirit of Jesus, create youth fellowships, build sound personalities, and seek the liberation and development of the people’s lives.” Initially, there was an emphasis on “carrying out revolution” between “building sound personalities” and “seeking the liberation and development of the people’s lives,” but this was later removed by the student movement leaders who felt that while there were ideals in “national revolution,” it could easily become mere rhetoric.

In 1933, the committee held a national fellowship conference at Hujiang University in Shanghai, establishing a temporary national assembly for the Chinese Christian Student Movement (referred to as the “Temporary Assembly”), with plans to formally establish the movement in 1936. However, due to the war, the originally planned national conference was postponed until it was finally realized in 1939.From August 14 to 20, 1939, the National Christian Student Representative Conference was held in Guandu, Kunming, at the rear during the War of Resistance. Twenty-five representatives from Beijing, Shanghai, Fujian, Hong Kong, Yunnan, Guiyang, Chongqing, and Chengdu attended the conference. The main theme of the conference was “Christianity and the Construction of a New China,” with speakers including Pan Guangdan, Zhao Zichan, Gilbert Baker (白約翰), Shi Baozhen, Ding Guangxun, and Jiang Wenzhen. The conference ultimately approved the establishment of the National Federation of Student Christian Unions (簡稱「全國學聯」). The federation had three executive secretaries: Jiang Wenzhen (director of the YMCA school committee), Shi Baozhen (executive secretary of the Young Women’s Christian Association student department), and Ding Guangxun (executive secretary of the Shanghai union). Born from a sense of urgency, the Chinese Christian student movement successfully formed a formal national organization after facing many challenges.

The 1939 “National Christian Student Representative Conference Declaration” reiterated the vision of “salvation and nation-building,” reaffirming its stance toward the times: (1) advocating unity, opposing division, and completing nation-building; (2) insisting on fighting to the end without compromise; (3) supporting the establishment of a democratic political system, promoting a socialist economic principle of “each according to their ability, each according to their needs”; (4) committing to assist in the mobilization of national spirit, transforming the life of individuals trapped in indulgence; (5) advocating for deepening international friendships and connecting all forces that uphold justice.

It first implemented the “Three Selfs” (自自治、自養及自傳) policy, especially in personnel and financial matters, severing ties with “imperialism,” forming a board of directors, and registering with government departments. The National Assembly suggested that all social service work be taken over by the government; if the government was unwilling to undertake it, efforts would be made to terminate those activities.   1. Hunan Bible Institute and Scofield Correspondence Course, 1930 (Yao Xiyi 姚西伊 2021: 111-114)

  1. Lichuan Experiment Project, 1934 (Zhang Li 張力 1985: 1035-1039; Xu Baoqian 徐寶謙 1936: 98-102; Liu Jiafeng 劉家峰 2008: 160-179; Wu Liming 吳利明 1981: 202-209; Thomson 1969: 97-121)

In the late 1920s, a reconstruction movement for rural development began across various regions of China. By 1934, there were 691 public and private organizations engaged in rural construction activities nationwide, undertaking different projects. In 1930, the Chinese Communist Party established a temporary Soviet in Jiangxi, but by 1934, the Kuomintang began expelling the Communist Party from Jiangxi through their “suppression policy.” During the Communist rule, land reforms had been promoted locally, and the National Government sought to eliminate the “poison” of communism through rural reconstruction. As a result, rural construction services in Jiangxi commenced. In June 1934, the National Economic Commission established an office in Jiangxi to formulate plans for new construction. The Jiangxi provincial government invited two experts, Pan Ji (潘驥), a Harvard University graduate, and a Cornell University doctoral director, to serve as the provincial health director and agricultural institute president, respectively, to work in conjunction with the Jiangxi office. At the invitation of Finance Minister Kong Xiangxi (孔祥熙), Zhang Fuliang (張福良) from the National Christian Council of China (Zhonghua Quanguo Jidujiao Xiejin Hui 中華全國基督教協進會) agreed to serve as a technical adviser in the Jiangxi office. Zhang had studied in the United States twice, earning master’s degrees in forestry and agriculture, and had seven years of rural work experience. In July, the National Economic Commission established ten rural service areas throughout Jiangxi. In March 1935, the Jiangxi office was reorganized into the “Management Office for Jiangxi Rural Service Areas” under the National Economic Commission, with Zhang Fuliang appointed as the commissioner.

As early as the summer of 1933, Song Meiling (宋美齡), wife of Chiang Kai-shek, expressed to the missionary William R. Johnson her intention for the government to implement the rural reconstruction experiment in the reclaimed areas of Jiangxi. In August, a group of missionaries convened at Guling and invited Song Meiling to attend. She once again expressed the hope that churches could participate in rural construction efforts in Jiangxi. The Christian Council appointed Zhang Fuliang to investigate Jiangxi in September (later, Zhang was called to participate in the work of the Jiangxi office).In October, the council held a meeting in Nanchang, attended by Chiang Kai-shek (Chiang Jieshi 蔣介石) and his wife, where it was decided to establish the “Jiangxi Christian Rural Service Union.” At that time, Song Meiling (Song Meiling 宋美齡) was willing to cover half of the funding for this project, which was set to last for five years.

In September 1934, the Lichuan Experiment Project officially began. The Lichuan experimental area was a church-led initiative, separate from the ten rural service areas established by the government. The first general secretary was George Shepherd (牧恩波), an American missionary from the North China Christian Church. However, he was recalled to the U.S. at the end of 1934, during which time Eugene A. Turner (狄爾耐) from the YMCA and Pastor Huber (胡本德) from the Congregational Church served as acting general secretaries. In August 1935, Xu Baoqian (徐寶謙) from Yanjing University’s Institute of Religious Studies took over as general secretary. Although Xu was not an expert in rural development, he bravely accepted the challenge, believing that since the government was willing to allow the church to participate, the church should seize this opportunity to contribute to the reconstruction of Chinese society. Thus, with a spirit of religious calling, Xu left his comfortable life and moved to the impoverished Lichuan.

The experimental project established its center in Wulinlang within Lichuan’s fourth district, using several nearby villages as the initial scope of experimentation. The work was organized by village and divided into educational, healthcare, religious, women’s, agricultural, and industrial areas.

However, the realities proved far more challenging than the ideals could address, as the entire Lichuan experiment faced many difficulties. First, Shepherd initially recruited over ten young people from various countries for service, but collaboration among them was not easy. Due to the backward conditions and unsatisfactory pay, the project struggled to find suitable high-caliber professionals (in medicine and agriculture) to come to Lichuan. Even idealistic young people soon became disillusioned due to the harsh environmental realities. As a result, the turnover rate among staff was extremely high, significantly impacting the progress of the work. Secondly, some members of the council began to doubt the project, especially those who criticized it for lacking religious activities, claiming it had merely become a social welfare scheme. Xu, however, believed that the experiment itself was a religious initiative, as religion encompasses the entirety of life. Nonetheless, Xu’s approach of “serving with the spirit of Jesus” and the vision of constructing Christianized rural areas differed from the views of some church leaders who saw the project merely as an evangelistic tool. Additionally, since the project was not a government initiative, it lacked government support, making it challenging to promote. In 1936, Xu strengthened cooperation with the government; a staff member was appointed by the provincial government as district chief, and the Lichuan experiment area joined the provincial government’s rural improvement committee, integrating it into the provincial government’s three-year plan. However, this relationship with the government also raised doubts within the church. In 1937, Xu Baoqian resigned due to differences with the council. After the five-year funding period, Song Meiling did not continue the support, resulting in the project’s failure.

  1. The Christian Farmer, 1934 (Chen Jianming 陳建明 and Liu Lu 劉路 2007: 48-57)

In August 1934, The Christian Farmer (《田家半月刊》) was published by the North China Christian Rural Development Association’s literary department, with the editorial work primarily undertaken by “Tianjia She” based at Shandong Qilu University, with Zhang Xueyan (張雪岩) serving as a core figure, holding various roles such as deputy editor, editor-in-chief, and president. The Christian Farmer was one of the earliest Christian publications targeting rural communities in China, aimed at popularizing scientific culture and improving the quality of farmers. The content included columns on “Opinions,” “Common Knowledge,” “Literature and Art,” “Religion,” “Livelihood Supplement,” “Health Supplement,” “Family Supplement,” and “Children’s Supplement,” particularly utilizing language from the “Farmers’ Thousand Characters Course” published by the China Popular Education Promotion Association, accompanied by phonetic annotations for individual characters. Its publication coincided with the promotion of the Christian rural development movement at the time.

During the wartime period, the editorial operations of The Christian Farmer also relocated along with Qilu University (moving to Changsha in October 1937 and to Chengdu in September 1938), and it did not cease publication due to the war. During this time, the journal published numerous articles to awaken the public’s spirit in resisting Japanese aggression. Zhang Xueyan personally wrote articles such as “The Unwavering Self-Respect and Confidence of the Chinese Nation,” “The Voice of Justice,” “Military First and Morale,” “The Domestic War Situation and the People’s Belief,” and “In Commemoration of the Fifth Anniversary of the Anti-Japanese War,” calling for the unity of the masses to achieve victory. After the war’s victory, he also wrote articles calling for an end to the civil war, proclaiming “Peace First, Nation Building First, Democracy First, Freedom First.”

The publication of The Christian Farmer was well received by readers. By the first anniversary of its launch, it had 8,000 subscribers distributed throughout the country. By the time of the war, the circulation had increased to 50,000. In March 1941, the circulation was still at 15,000. By 1942, it had further increased to 35,000 subscribers. In November 1944, it continued its circulation growth.The editorial office was relocated to Shapingba in Chongqing and changed to a monthly publication. In 1947, it moved to Beiping (Beijing), continuing publication under the North China Christian Rural Service Union.

As Zhang Xueyan (張雪岩) became one of the five representatives of the religious community in the New Political Consultative Conference in 1949, The Christian Farmer continued to be published after the founding of the People’s Republic of China. After the death of Zhang Xueyan in January 1950, Liu Lingjiu (劉齡九) took over as editor. Liu was labeled a rightist in 1957, leading to the cessation of The Christian Farmer.

  1. The New Life Movement of the National Government and Christianity, 1934 (Yang Weihua 楊衛華 2020: 89-100; Thomson 1969: 151-174)

In 1934, the National Government launched the New Life Movement. According to Chiang Kai-shek’s conceptualization, the New Life Movement emphasized the importance of “propriety, righteousness, integrity, and honor,” integrating these principles into daily life—not merely striving for superficial cleanliness or order but “to reform society and rejuvenate the nation and its people.” The movement followed the principle of “Three Transformations”: “artistic living, productive living, and militarized living.” “Militarized living” meant enabling the general populace to maintain order, observe discipline, and act swiftly and decisively in unison. “Productive living” urged citizens to embrace frugality, reduce consumption, and take pride in using domestic products; actively working hard to increase production and serve society was emphasized. “Artistic living” encouraged everyone to value neatness, cleanliness, and that everything should be orderly, leading to natural beauty.

In July 1934, the New Life Movement Promotion Society was established, with Chiang serving as president. In February 1936, the society set up a Women’s Guidance Committee in Nanjing, led by Song Meiling. In April, Song invited Pastor George W. Shepherd (牧波恩) of the Jiangxi Christian Rural Service Union to serve as an overseas consultant for the New Life Movement.

Since the movement began, Chiang and Song have called upon Christian churches to join, encouraging local New Life Movement promotion associations to collaborate with Christian groups to utilize the organization and mobilization strength of the church to aid the movement’s development. In 1935, Chiang formally invited the Christian community to participate. In May 1937, at the 11th General Meeting of the National Christian Council of China (Zhonghua Quanguo Jidujiao Xiejin Hui 中華全國基督教協進會), Wu Yifang (吳貽芳) read an article on “Christianity and the New Life Movement,” promoting Christian involvement and emphasizing that the movement was not a political campaign. The response from various sectors of Christianity towards the government’s call was generally positive. For example, the Chinese Recorder published an editorial in May 1934 affirming the New Life Movement as the beginning of China’s spiritual reconstruction and a symbol of hope for the future. In its May 1937 issue, the editorial reiterated its support and included the above-mentioned article by Song Meiling, along with various other articles (including contributions from Shepherd, Luo Yunyuan, and Ralph A. Felton) advocating for the New Life Movement. Amidst the overwhelmingly positive discussions, the Chinese Recorder also compiled critiques from several Christians regarding the New Life Movement (Chinese Recorder 1937: 271-272, 279-296).

Chinese churches, including the Methodist Church, the China Christian Church, and the Anglican Church, as well as the National Christian Council, also maintained a positive attitude, while the National YMCA encouraged individual participation. Of course, there were concerns about Christianity’s failure to maintain a degree of distance from political power. Also, the strong Confucian flavor of the New Life Movement provoked differing opinions among missionaries, with some highly affirming the return to Chinese traditions and believing that Christianity could integrate with them, while others were skeptical about the value of those traditions.

In October 1936, the National Council submitted an application to the Ministry of the Interior, emphasizing that the church serves the populace in the spirit of Christianity, “focusing primarily on promoting the spiritual cultivation and moral accomplishment of the people. After detailed examination of the relevant regulations, the council found that, due to the absence of specific legislation regarding church registration, religious organizations were subject to the same requirements as other organizations, which included seven mandates: (1) no actions or speeches that violate the Three People’s Principles; (2) acceptance of guidance from the Kuomintang (Nationalist Party); (3) compliance with national and governmental laws; (4) membership limited to legally permitted individuals; (5) anyone accused of counter-revolutionary behavior or stripped of civil rights cannot be a member; (6) all meetings, except for regular meetings, must obtain permission from local party authorities and supervising offices; (7) violations of the above provisions shall be subject to legal penalties. The council felt that these stipulations were incompatible with the church’s mission and submitted a letter to the Ministry of the Interior in December 1936, expressing that points (4), (5), and (6) would undermine the guarantee of religious freedom and requested clarification. The council was concerned that activities would be subject to interference from party and government departments and acknowledged that even if someone violated the law, they could still be a believer, which was not contradictory.

Consequently, the Ministry of the Interior sought the opinion of the Central People’s Training Department of the Kuomintang, which deemed the mission of the National Christian Council pure and its operations essential, granting them a “People’s Organization Certificate.” On December 22, 1936, the Ministry of the Interior issued the certificate and officially registered the council on March 8, 1937. However, it reiterated that any violations of national laws would also constitute violations of church regulations, preventing such individuals from being members; gatherings of religious organizations, such as regular supervisory meetings and ceremonies stipulated by church laws, would be regarded as regular meetings; and any convening of membership assemblies, special committees, or irregular gatherings must be reported for approval in advance to local party authorities and relevant offices. The National Christian Council became the only Christian organization to successfully register before the War of Resistance Against Japan.

In 1937, the War of Resistance Against Japan erupted, and Japanese forces began bombing Nanjing in August.   1. Chinese Fundamentalists and Spiritualists (Yao 2003: 216-222; Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2001: 21-33)

In 1929, members of the Presbyterian Church’s North China assembly initiated the establishment of the “League of Christian Churches” (基督教聯合會). In December of that year, a conference was held where it was unanimously decided not to join the Chinese Christian Church formed by the Presbyterians, Congregationalists, and the London Missionary Society, and to proceed with the organization of the League of Christian Churches. In early 1930, the League announced its five major creeds: (1) We believe that the Old and New Testaments, entirely inspired by the Holy Spirit, are the sole and complete standard for our faith and duty; (2) We acknowledge the Apostles’ Creed, which encapsulates the essential truths of the Bible; (3) There is one unique God in Trinity—God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Spirit—co-equal, co-eternal, and co-glorious; (4) Our Lord Jesus Christ was born of the Virgin Mary, by the Holy Spirit, fully human, whose life and deeds on earth, as documented in the Gospels, led to his sacrificial death for humanity, his atonement for sin, his resurrection and ascension, where he acts as mediator among men, and he will return a second time as King for judgment and blessed hope for believers; the Holy Church is composed of believers who are reborn by the Holy Spirit and saved through faith in Jesus Christ, forming the body of Christ.

Soon after, individual missionaries from the Evangelical Church, Lutheran Church, China Inland Mission, and the China Fellowship began to join. Some prominent Chinese church leaders also became supporters, such as Yang Shaotang (楊紹唐), Jia Yuming (賈玉銘), Ding Limei (丁立美), among whom Jia Yuming was even elected as president.

In the early 20th century, many fundamentalist figures emerged in the Chinese church. These included: Jia Yuming (賈玉銘), Chen Chonggui (陳崇桂), Yang Shaotang (楊紹唐), Wang Mingdao (王明道), Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲), Li Yuanru (李淵如), Wang Peizhen (汪佩真), Ding Limei (丁立美), Cheng Jigu (成寄歸), Wang Zai (王載), Ji Zhiwen (計志文), Song Shangjie (宋尚節), Jiao Weizhen (焦維真), Yu Cidu (余慈度), Zhang Xuegong (張學恭), Gao Shizhu (高師竹), Zhao Liutang (趙柳塘), Huang Yuangu (黃原素), Zhao Shiguang (趙世光), Zhao Junying (趙君影), and others.

The term “fundamentalism” was not commonly used among Chinese churches in the early 20th century. In contrast to the terms “Fundamentalist” (基要派) or “Fundamentalism” (基要主義) used among missionaries in China, Chinese believers frequently referred to themselves as “spiritualists” (屬靈派) or “conservatives” (保守派). Why was the term “Fundamentalism” not popular in Chinese churches at the time? The primary reason is that conservative elements within Chinese churches did not possess a strong self-identity like the fundamentalist camp in America. In fact, the term “Fundamentalism” originated in the United States and was later introduced to various missionary fields by North American missionaries. For those who viewed liberal theology as their main adversary, “Fundamentalism” became the center of their self-identity, serving as the dividing line between “us” and “them.” In contrast, even though the Chinese church shares a theological tradition linked to North American fundamentalism, many Chinese pastors did not necessarily have the same self-awareness or used “Fundamentalism” as their rallying mark.

Another reason for the uncommon usage of “fundamentalism” among Chinese pastors relates to issues of theological orthodoxy. The rise of North American fundamentalism has a deep historical background in orthodox Christian doctrinal theology. Many fundamentalist figures had received orthodox theological training and had a solid understanding of the “core beliefs” they sought to defend. Conversely, many Chinese pastors did not have profound insights into systematic doctrinal issues; while they understood the conflict between old and new theology in the fierce debates among missionaries, they most likely lacked clarity on the technical theological problems that underpinned those debates. The opposition from Chinese pastors and believers toward liberalism was often driven more by a reaction against the perceived “unspirituality” of the other side rather than a commitment to protecting fundamental doctrines.

While the term fundamentalism is not common in China, this does not mean that the Chinese church lacks a desire to oppose liberal theology. For many Chinese pastors who stand against modernist views, they often prefer to identify as “spiritualists.” The origin of the term “spiritualist” is difficult to pinpoint now. It may be related to Ni Tuosheng’s (倪柝聲) book “The Spiritual Man” (屬靈人), published in 1928, which gained wide popularity among believers. Its teaching on the tripartite nature of the soul and body significantly influenced the Chinese church. Liang Jialin (梁家麟) pointed out that Ni’s greatest impact on the Chinese church was the creation and revision of a large number of widely used “spiritual vocabularies.” Most Chinese believers distinguished themselves from modernism primarily based on whether they were “spiritual” or not.Since they often criticize modernists as “unspiritual” and pursue “spirituality” as their ultimate goal, the term “spiritualists” (屬靈派) became a self-definition for the Chinese church in the first half of the 20th century. Those modernists they reject often use the label “spiritualists” to satirize them.

In addition to “spiritualists,” another term synonymous with fundamentalists is “conservatives” (保守派). In principle, the concept of “conservative” is relative to “radical.” The radicals often refer to liberalism or modernism, and in the context of the Chinese church, terms like “modernists” (現代派), “social gospelists” (社會福音派), and “new theology” (新神學) are more commonly used. These concepts are frequently found in the polemical writings of figures like Jia Yuming (賈玉銘) among fundamentalists, and the combative terminology used by Wang Mingdao (王明道) such as “unbelievers” (不信派) has had a significant impact. In the eyes of liberals, church members opposing them are seen as adherents to old doctrines and traditions, clearly marking them as “conservatives” theologically.

Strictly speaking, “spiritualists” and “conservatives” remain somewhat ambiguous concepts, and there is no guarantee of complete theological agreement between the two groups. They can even be mutually incompatible on issues like ecclesiology and eschatology. Additionally, they hold different attitudes toward the Charismatic movement. It can be said that opposing liberal theology has become their greatest common denominator. Moreover, even in their stance against modernists, they can vary in attitude. For instance, Jia Yuming, Chen Chonggui (陳崇桂), Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲), Wang Mingdao (王明道), and Song Shangjie (宋尚節) are recognized as Chinese “spiritualists,” but their “homogeneity” lies solely in their uncompromising stance against liberal theology. Regardless of how these individuals oppose liberal theology, their positions on fundamental doctrines remain quite distinct. There are strict fundamentalists and open fundamentalists; the former has a stronger combative flavor and a clear tendency toward separatism, while the latter holds a more open stance toward academia and social reform. The contrasting approaches from the combative Wang Mingdao to the more moderate Jia Yuming and Chen Chonggui reflect the diverse orientations of Chinese fundamentalists.

  1. The Amethyst 紫晶, first published in 1930 (紫晶 1930: 1; 1932: 61-83)

In January 1930, “The Amethyst” (《紫晶》), edited by Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳), was first published, focusing primarily on themes of spirituality, worship, and fellowship. Unlike many opinion-oriented journals of the Republican era, “The Amethyst” emphasized the creation of prayers, hymns, and liturgies, seeking a fresh approach to building a culturally relevant Chinese church.

In the article “A Conversation with Friends Meeting The Amethyst for the First Time,” the emphasis is on the importance of “individual heart,” “personal spirituality,” and “public worship” in the Christian life. It discusses three key elements: “reverence,” “sincerity,” and “beauty.” It asserts that “reverence and beauty without sincerity are mere forms, and sincerity without beauty can easily lead to indifference.” Therefore, believers “should gain not only moral guidance from communal worship but also a sense of spiritual beauty and joy.” Worshiping God requires not only effort and dedication but also heartfelt involvement. “Beautiful music, beautiful hymns, beautiful writings, beautiful architecture — all are crystallizations of human effort, and when offered before God, all this beauty should be dedicated to Him.” In today’s Chinese church, where “external ideologies advance vigorously yet chaotically” and “internal life is laborious yet extremely desolate,” it is believed that “advocating for more solemn worship through writing, supporting deeper spirituality through writing, and seeking closer fellowship through writing are urgent tasks.” In light of this realization, “The Amethyst” was founded with the aims: (1) to encompass spirituality, worship, and fellowship; (2) to employ experimentation, discussion, and exchange as methods; and (3) to include content such as discussions on fellowship, sermons, meditations on spirituality, poetry, hymns, personal and communal prayers, worship rituals, notes, and introductions to books and periodicals. “The Amethyst” was not affiliated with any particular group; it was a collaborative effort among individuals like Liu Tingfang, Yang Yinliou (楊蔭瀏), Xie Jingsheng (謝景昇), and Xu Dishan (許地山), dedicated to experimentation in spirituality, worship, and fellowship.

For example, in the January 1932 issue (Vol. 3, No. 1) of “The Amethyst,” the theme was “national crisis,” which featured an article titled “An Appeal for the National Crisis” by Liu Tingfang. He made a heartfelt appeal concerning the national crisis, asking God for mercy and forgiveness for the sins of the people. Additionally, there was “The Credo of National Crisis,” based on Scripture, proclaiming a joint proclamation of faith among nationwide believers before the Lord. Several “Prayers for the National Crisis” were also included as devout petitions.Lastly, two new hymns were published (Battle Hymn and Peace Hymn by Liu Tingfang), attempting to express from a believer’s perspective that they are “fighting fiercely against the violent warlords and invading enemies,” while also “longing for and striving for the realization of God’s love through peace.” “The contributors of this journal wish to join all believers across the nation to worship with a sincere spirit in today’s struggles, passionately singing out our hope for peace.”

In June 1937, after publishing Volume 12, Issues 1 and 2, “The Amethyst” ceased publication.

  1. Laymen’s Foreign Mission Inquiry, 1931-1932 (Hocking 1932: 1-78; Inquiry Group 1934: 1-66; Yao 2003: 239-257; Zhao Zicheng 1934: Preface 3-5)

In 1931, a delegation of lay representatives from seven American denominations (Northern Baptist Convention, Congregational Church, Methodist Episcopal Church, Presbyterian Church, Anglican Church, Ethiopian Church, United Methodist Church) formed an inquiry group to visit India, Myanmar, China, and Japan to conduct a comprehensive study and review of American overseas missionary activities. The inquiry group was chaired by William E. Hocking, a professor in the philosophy department at Harvard University. They arrived in Shanghai on November 7, 1930, returned to the United States on July 27, 1931, and published a report in 1932 titled “Re-Thinking Missions: A Laymen’s Inquiry after One Hundred Years.” The Chinese translation of the report, “宣教事業平議,” was also prepared by Xu Baoqian, Miao Qiusheng, and Fan Dingjiu and published in 1934.

The report begins by examining the concept of “mission” in detail across four chapters, followed by a discussion divided into sections on missionary societies and churches, educational work, literary endeavors, medical services, rural missions, industrial development, and women’s work. Finally, it reviews the administrative issues related to overseas missions.

Regarding missionary principles, the report specifically discusses Christianity’s “uniqueness,” indicating that Christianity shares “many commonalities” with other religions in terms of theology and ethics. Although no religion’s doctrines are identical to those of Christianity, one cannot point to a single principle unique to other religions. Therefore, “we need not argue about principles with them,” since “principles are not patented, and truth is a shared possession of humanity.” As such, the report suggests that Christianity’s characteristic lies in “making a special choice from the shared truths of humanity.” On the other hand, Christianity’s uniqueness is also reflected in “its symbols, traditions, fellowship, and its transformative life as represented by its leader.” When Christianity proclaims, “Our gospel is Jesus Christ,” it presents a profound “mystery” to non-Christians, especially those from Eastern backgrounds. The report opines that “there’s no need to elaborate on the differences between Christianity and other religions” merely to highlight its “superiority,” but rather “one should convincingly demonstrate what it believes and practice in accordance with that faith.”

As for the aims of missions, the report notes that if the objective is “to convert all the people of Asia to Christianity,” this is undoubtedly a “distant prospect.” Therefore, Western groups should abandon such aspirations. “The responsibility of missionary societies lies in planting seeds, not waiting for trees to grow.” In summary, the concept of missionary work needs to be updated, encouraging believers to break away from the old tasks and goals linked with established customs and religious thoughts. Instead, it should be transformed into a “permanent mission” represented by a small group of well-trained individuals, acting as advisors in Christian life for churches or other religious bodies, with an empathetic awareness of cultural shifts, “seeking to minimize the gaps during transitions.” Missionary work should conduct various experiments during this transition to influence social life while also communicating with Eastern cultural scholars. With this conceptual shift, the report reinterprets previous missionary activities as a “temporary mission” that should transition to the new understanding of a “permanent mission.”

The publication of the inquiry report sparked intense discussions in the United States and the four countries studied, particularly regarding its apparent leaning towards liberal theology, which drew criticism from traditional fundamentalists. In November 1932 and March 1933, the Northern Presbyterian Church in the United States passed several resolutions explicitly opposing sections of the report that contradicted New Testament teachings and Presbyterian doctrines. Robert H. G. (高樂弼), director of the China Inland Mission’s North America region, also expressed opposition.Glover pointed out that the report fully revealed the significant issues between Christianity and modernism, and that all missionary societies and churches should clearly state their positions. The China Bible Society also criticized the report’s conclusions as unjust and biased from the outset. The “Chinese Recorder,” a journal from 1933, also noted the debates among missionaries in China regarding the report.

From the perspective of the Chinese church, Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡) stated in the preface of the Chinese translation that the opinions and claims in the book “do not fully satisfy everyone, but they still serve as a beneficial remedy.” “Of course, it cannot be said that the words are flawless, but their genuine intention to seek improvement in the church is indeed commendable.” However, Zhao Zicheng (趙紫宸), the head of the Department of Religion at Yenching University, severely criticized the report in his 1934 publication “Critique of the First Four Chapters of the Missionary Work Report.” He was particularly concerned about the discussion in Chapter Three regarding the uniqueness of Christianity and was dissatisfied with the report’s section on Jesus, which completely omitted the “incarnation” and failed to explain Jesus Christ as the Son of God. He remarked, “If this is what makes Christianity unique, perhaps Christianity has no unique aspects at all!” He believed that “the greatness of Christianity, and its necessity for China,” does not hinge on introducing science, nor on providing humanitarian principles or social gospel to the Chinese; rather, it lies in the religion of Jesus himself.

  1. Hymns of Universal Praise (《普天頌讚》), 1936 (Liang Naqi 梁納祈 2007)

Since the early 20th century, denominational missions have begun to compile hymnals for common usage within their traditions during the process of achieving unity. The earliest completion was “Hymns of Praise” (《頌主聖詩》) published by the China Lutheran Church in 1920. After the establishment of the Church of China (中華聖公會) in 1912, plans were made to unify hymns across its 12 dioceses, leading to the publication of “A Collection of Songs for the Praise of God” (《頌主詩集》) in 1931.

In 1913, after the establishment of the Continuation Committee (中華續行委辦會), a Special Committee on Hymnology was formed in 1916. However, the committee only operated for two years before disbanding. It wasn’t until 1922, following the nationwide Christian conference, that the editing of a united hymnal gained attention.

In September 1931, representatives from the Church of Christ in China, Church of China, Methodist Episcopal Church North, North China Lung Li Hui, and Methodist Episcopal Church South met in Shanghai to discuss plans for a united hymnal. The following year, the East China Baptist Convention also joined. At that time, it was decided that each association would select 300 hymns based on necessary, secondary, and optional criteria, with 100 deemed essential. Of the 300 essential hymns chosen by the six associations, excluding duplicates, a total of 252 hymns were obtained. Among these 252 hymns, only 10 were selected by all six associations.

Subsequently, the Joint Hymnal Editorial Committee added another 260 hymns based on the principles of “ideals and purposes,” resulting in a total of 512 hymns. The committee’s aim was determined as follows: “to produce a hymnal that reflects the praise and highest ardor of all Chinese Christians. It hopes that through spiritual thoughts, the charm of the text, and the standards of music, the quality of Chinese hymns in all churches will be elevated. Therefore, the resulting hymnal must evoke a spirit of reverence among various Christian groups during worship and must include hymns suitable for all seasons and aspects of church life, ensuring that all men, women, and children within the church, regardless of educational background, can understand and, as much as possible, be comprehensible to those outside the church as well.”

Notably, at the second general assembly in 1932, it was passed that one-tenth of the hymns in the collection must be composed by Chinese authors. In 1933, the editorial committee only selected 37 originals from Chinese authors. Ultimately, of the 512 hymns, 62 were original compositions by Chinese authors, accounting for 12%.Among them, Zhao Zicheng (趙紫宸) contributed 10 hymns, Yang Yinliou (楊蔭瀏) 7 hymns, Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) 6 hymns, Yang Jingqiu (楊鏡秋) 3 hymns, and You Lude (尤路得) 2 hymns. The Joint Hymnal Committee also aimed to localize the hymnal, resulting in “Hymns of Universal Praise” (《普天頌讚》) including 72 hymns with Chinese folk styles. Contributors included both Chinese composers (such as Yang Yinliou) and Western composers like Bliss M. Wiant (范天祥). Thus, the preface describes it as “the crystallization of past Chinese hymns. On the one hand, it has the best hymnal background from the West; on the other hand, it contains elements of national creation.”

“Hymns of Universal Praise” took several years to complete, compiling a total of 512 hymns, published by the National Christian Council in March 1936, including versions with staff notation, numeric notation, and text. The first edition printed 119,000 copies distributed nationwide. By 1938, a total of 259,000 copies had been printed.

  1. Love and Revolution (唯愛與革命) (Yao Xiyi 2008: 78-101, 38-55)

After the “September 18” Incident in 1931 when Japan occupied Northeast China, Chinese Christians engaged in intense debates over issues of war and peace, which can be categorized into three different positions: (1) the armed resistance faction; (2) the moderate reconciliation faction; and (3) the pacifist faction.

Firstly, the armed resistance faction supports military opposition to Japanese aggression in response to the national crisis. They attempt to use the Bible as a theological justification, arguing that war and violence are also acceptable under Christianity. In the history of the Israelites in the Old Testament, the rationale for war is evident, and in the New Testament, Jesus also exhibits a deep hatred for evil. Under the premise of justice, they reinterpret “love thy enemy” to exclude “the enemy” from being the devil, viewing Japanese imperialism as the irredeemable “devil.” They see the Japanese invading forces as a dangerous and brutal whole, believing that there is no alternative but to resist militarily. The armed resistance faction emphasizes that Christians, in addition to being the elect of the Kingdom of Heaven, are also citizens of China. Thus, as citizens, they are obligated to serve their country and must step up in times of national crisis. Beyond nationalist perspectives, some also defend armed struggle from internationalist and global perspectives, arguing that all people globally, including Japanese citizens, should oppose these atrocities. For those within Christianity who advocate for love, they believe this form of extreme pacifism and absolute non-resistance fundamentally cannot achieve human justice, rendering it counterproductive to national salvation.

Secondly, the moderate reconciliation faction, primarily composed of leaders from various Christian denominations and organizations, acknowledges the importance of resisting Japan, but is reluctant to abandon the spirit of Christ’s “brotherly love.” For instance, Cheng Jingyi of the National Christian Council expressed a desire for peaceful solutions through international mechanisms while responding to the “September 18” and “January 28” incidents, maintaining a pacifist tone. However, he did not publicly and explicitly condemn violence, nor did he entirely exclude military opposition as a solution to the national crisis. By 1937, as the full-scale war against Japan commenced, the council publicly recognized the righteousness of the war while still upholding the spirit of Christ’s love and peace.

Finally, represented by the Chinese Fellowship of Reconciliation, the pacifists insist on Jesus’ non-violent stance. They point out that Jesus exemplified love and non-violence throughout his life, with the “Sermon on the Mount” embodying Christian pacifism. In response to the criticisms from the armed resistance faction, the pacifists argue that love is not about passive non-resistance but about active love; the former is cowardice, while the latter requires great courage. The method of Jesus was to overcome hatred with love and injustice with righteousness. After the “September 18” incident, pacifists proposed non-violent resistance in the face of national crisis, such as boycotting Japanese goods and promoting movements of non-cooperation with Japan, in order to express protest against Japanese militaristic atrocities and to awaken the conscience of progressive forces within Japan to oppose the aggression.

Undoubtedly, the national crisis triggered by Japan’s invasion of China in the 1930s posed a significant challenge to Chinese pacifism. Even within the Chinese Fellowship of Reconciliation, some members began to waver. By November 1932, of the approximately 200 members, only 35 were Chinese. The magazine “唯愛” (Love) ceased publication in 1935, and in February 1937, its chairman, Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗), withdrew from the fellowship. As the full-scale war unfolded, the space for the pacifist movement in China increasingly narrowed.

  1. Christianity and Communism (基督教與共產主義) (Yang Weihua 2011: 111-125; Xing Fuzeng 2016: xxix-xlii)

In the 1930s, as the communist movement developed in China, more reflections on the relationship between Christianity and communism began to arise within the Christian community. The liberal theological camp recognized that the development of communism in China posed a significant challenge to Christianity.For example, the editorial of the “Chinese Recorder” in 1931 emphasized the need to pay attention to communism as a major enemy of Christianity. E. E. Barnett of the Young Men’s Christian Association also pointed out that it is a well-organized and aggressive opponent faced by Christianity. George S. Eddy noted the common ground between Christianity and communism, particularly in their shared pursuit of a new social order and both declaring themselves as the only saviors. His views were translated into Chinese by Zhang Shizhang and published in the “Voice of the Wilderness” (《野聲》) in 1931. Later, Zhang also translated John Macmurray’s book “Creative Society: A Study of the Relation of Christianity to Communism” into Chinese, which was published by the Young Men’s Association Press in 1936.

In the Chinese Christian community, Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗) emerged as a representative figure actively reflecting on and exploring the relationship between the two. His 1934 book “Social Gospel” includes several essays discussing this topic: “Pacifism and Social Transformation,” “The Meaning of the Social Gospel,” “The Social Gospel and Individual Gospel,” and “Christianity and Communism.”

Wu examined the similarities and differences between Christianity and communism not strictly as a theoretical inquiry but rather to help more Christians shed unnecessary fears and prejudices and recognize the merits of communism. Simultaneously, it served to establish Christianity’s unique contribution to social transformation. Wu pointed out that Christianity could learn from communism in two areas: the zeal for social transformation (Social Passion) and the pragmatic social perspective (Social realism). Regarding the former, he believed that “Christianity inherently possesses the passion for social transformation, although this is not being expressed presently.” In essence, through the actions of communist practitioners, the passion for social transformation within Christianity could be reawakened. For Wu, he saw no substantial issue with this. Conversely, he identified the most critical challenge as communism’s “pragmatic social perspective.” He asserted that the essence of Christianity is “idealism,” and realizing its ideals presents the greatest challenge for the Church. “If we could marry the idealism of Christianity with the realism of communism, I believe it would be beneficial for both sides.”

Furthermore, Wu presented Christianity’s unique contribution by comparing the similarities and, especially, the differences in “goals” and “means” between the two. Using “pacifism” as a foundation, he affirmed the goal of communism to “realize an economically egalitarian society,” arguing that “Isn’t that what Jesus aimed to do?” On the other hand, he noted that “we must oppose certain means of the Communist Party from a Christian standpoint, particularly as someone who believes in pacifism.” He stated, “We can empathize 120% with the Communist Party’s objectives, but we must oppose certain means they employ.” Wu’s reservations about the methods of communism reflect the affirmation of human value inherent in pacifism and its rejection of violent revolution. For him, this constitutes Christianity’s unique contribution.

In 1939, in the foreword he wrote for M. S. Bates’s “Christianity and Communism,” Wu reiterated the unique contributions of Christianity to social issues but expressed deep concern about the inability to realize such claims. He further pointed out that if Christianity itself cannot escape the pitfalls of ideals, what grounds does it have to criticize communism? Additionally, he noted that if the “prophetic mission” represents condemning the old world while aspiring to enter a new era, then communism evidently excels in this regard compared to Christianity. He considered that while Christians repent for their failure to actualize the social significance of Christianity, they should recognize how God’s word has been proclaimed in other movements for social change—whether they are non-Christian or even opposing Christianity—and acknowledge their contributions to social causes. Christians should, within the boundaries of not violating Christian principles, cooperate with such movements.(7) The Vatican Lifts the Ban on Ancestor Worship in 1939 (Chen Congming 2010: 98-134; Xing Fuzeng 1997: 87-90; Minamiki 1985: 183-203)

On October 20, 1939, Pope Pius XII ascended to the papacy and issued the encyclical “Summi Pontificatus,” stating that within the context of world civilization, all customs and practices should not necessarily be tied to religious nature and should be examined with goodwill, aiming to protect and encourage them as much as possible. He also affirmed participation in social rituals motivated by patriotism.

On December 4, 1939, the Vatican’s Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith convened a general meeting, and on December 8, issued a decree lifting the ban, explaining that the significance of ancestor worship activities in China lies in honoring one’s ancestors, thus allowing believers to bow to the deceased, their images, and tablets bearing their names, or to express homage in other folk, non-religious manners. Respecting Confucius is not considered a religious ritual but simply an act of honoring a great figure. Believers are permitted to pay respects to Confucius before his images and tablets, as well as in Confucian temples or schools, including church schools. The decree also annulled the prohibition against Chinese Catholics practicing ancestor worship and honoring Confucius as established in the encyclical “Ex Quo Singulari” issued on July 11, 1742, and required clergy to take an oath.

The long-standing “Rites Controversy” regarding rituals in China, ongoing since the 17th century, was effectively resolved within Catholicism due to the decree of 1939. In contrast, among Christians, discussions about “ancestor veneration” emerged in the 1930s, particularly at the 8th annual meeting of the National Christian Council of China in 1931. Proposed measures included: (1) creating commemorative items for ancestor worship (like memorial halls or monuments); (2) compiling family genealogy; (3) organizing remembrance meetings; and (4) holding ceremonies for honoring the deceased. For remembrance or mourning ceremonies, it was recommended to hold them at home, in churches, or cemeteries around Easter, focusing mainly on silent reflection, teachings, and silent prayers. Regarding bows or gestures of respect, these were left to the freedom of conscience. In cases of honoring ancestors in non-Christian households, such as female believers marrying into non-Christian families, the suggestion was to “first spread the message against superstitions and seek to regain the familial heart,” while if male believers lost the right to inherit family property for not honoring ancestors, it was advised to “sacrifice rights to preserve faith.” It is clear that these suggestions still operated on the premise of opposing ancestor worship and sought to replace it with Christianized forms of “ancestor veneration.” However, no discussions or resolutions were finalized at the annual meeting; it merely encouraged “vigorously practicing family worship.”

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. National Tsinghua University (國立清華大學) Eliminates the Christian Youth Group 1930 (Min Guo Ri Bao (民國日報) 1930; Qing Hua Zhou Kan (清華週刊) 1930a: Editorial, 1930b: 52)

In 1930, National Tsinghua University established the “Elimination of the Christian Youth Group,” which in its founding declaration pointed out that Christianity “is a way to narcotize the masses, cultivate subservience, and adapt to the environment like a cunning fox,” serving as a tool for the ruling class to “coax and oppress class resistance.” It also stated that Christianity “is an anti-scientific faith, an obstacle to the evolution of human civilization.” Tsinghua University, as a place for “the pursuit of academic inquiry and truth,” finds itself “incompatibly opposed” to the anti-scientific nature of Christianity, expressing deep disdain for the “church organizations that serve as lackeys to imperialism.” The declaration recalled the anti-Christian movement triggered by the World Student Christian Federation conference held at Tsinghua University in 1922 and aimed directly at the current Christian Youth Association on campus, declaring, “We can no longer endure, and our conscience and reason compel us to rise up against, no, we shall eliminate the banner of Christianity,” to ensure the youth association “forever disappears from our solemn and splendid Tsinghua University.”

The Elimination of the Christian Youth Group also published a special issue titled “Elimination of Christianity” in volume 33, issue 10 of Qing Hua Zhou Kan (清華週刊), including 20 articles. The “Editorial” explicitly proposed to analyze religious consciousness using “dialectical materialism” as its foundation and pointed out that “the success of the Elimination of Christianity relies much more on actual political struggle compared to the reliance on propaganda work.”

During the Christmas season in 1930, the Elimination of the Christian Youth Group further posted anti-Christian slogans on campus, intending to “use force to reclaim the Youth Association’s headquarters at our school.”

  1. Chinese Communist Party Christian School Student Movement Committee 1937 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2024)

In October 1937, the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party approved the Jiangsu Provincial Committee to be established in Shanghai, assigning work in Shanghai, with Liu Xiao (劉曉) as the provincial committee secretary responsible for rebuilding the underground party in Shanghai. At that time, due to the mobilization of a large number of party members to the rear and their base areas, the number of underground party members in Shanghai was only about 100. In March 1938, the Central Committee issued a “Decision on the Large-Scale Development of Party Members,” calling for “a massive increase in the number of party members,” recognizing this as an “urgent and serious task.” Subsequently, it instructed Liu Xiao to establish “a compact and extremely secret party organization” in “enemy-occupied central cities.” At the end of 1938, in response to the significant number of Christian youth groups and schools within the Shanghai concession, the Jiangsu Provincial Committee of the CCP decided to strengthen work within church schools, establishing the “Christian School Student Movement Committee” (abbreviated as “Christian School Committee”), which was organizationally separate from the Student Movement Committee (abbreviated as “Student Committee”). The party branches directly led by the Christian School Committee include: Hujiang (滬江), St. John’s (聖約翰), Zhejiang (之江), Dongwu (東吳), and Zhendan (震旦), and it promoted the work of Shanghai United (上海聯). In May 1939, the Christian School Committee also set up a “Church Secondary School District Committee,” overseeing the work of the party within church boys’ and girls’ high schools.

The Christian School Committee was led by Chen Xiuliang (陳修良), the secretary of the Women’s Committee of the underground party of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee. Under the deployments of the Christian School Committee and the Church Secondary School District Committee, efforts were made to send party members to study at various church schools, developing party members on campus and establishing party branches. Church universities, secondary schools, and the Christian Fellowship of Shanghai United provided favorable conditions for the CCP’s underground work to remain legally hidden. By October 1939, the Christian School Committee system had developed 54 party members.

In September 1942, the Christian School Committee merged into the Student Committee for unified leadership, while the original Church Secondary School District Committee, responsible for the party branches of Maillen (麥倫), Qingxin (清心), Dongwu Affiliated High School (東吳附中), Youth Association High School (青年會中學), East China United High School (華東聯合中學), and Hujiang Affiliated High School (滬江附中), was also transferred to be uniformly led by the Secondary School Work Committee (hereafter referred to as “Secondary School Work Committee”). In October 1944, the Student Committee abolished the Secondary School Work Committee, with the work of each secondary school district committee reporting directly to the Student Committee. During the War of Resistance, the CCP established party organizations in 68 ordinary secondary schools in Shanghai (including church schools), developing about 560 party members. By 1945, Shanghai had 25 secondary schools with party organizations, totaling 158 party members. By the end of 1946, the number of underground party members in Shanghai exceeded 5,000.
