China: Agency (1960-1970)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Zhang Wenxin (Zhang Wenxin 1969; China Group n.d.)

In 1962, Zhang Wenxin, who had been a Methodist missionary in China for many years, published “The Church in Communist China: A Protestant Appraisal,” attempting to organize the development of Christianity after the establishment of the People’s Republic of China. He paid particular attention to the development of the Three-Self Movement of Chinese Christianity and regarded the participation of Christians in the movement as a form of “pragmatic compromise” (modus vivendi). The following year, he published an English translated collection of documents on the Three-Self Movement, “Documents of the Three-Self Movement: Sources Materials for the Study of the Protestant Church in Communist China,” which became an indispensable English historical resource for studying the Three-Self Movement.

Zhang Wenxin (Francis P. Jones, 1890-1975), a missionary of the Methodist Church, was born in Dodgeville, Wisconsin, USA. In 1907, he entered Platteville Teachers’ Training College. In 1911, he studied at Northwestern University and Garrett Evangelical Theological Seminary, continuing his studies at the University of Chicago after graduation. In 1915, Zhang came to Fukien, China, for missionary work. During his vacation from 1929 to 1930, he studied at Union Theological Seminary in New York, focusing on critical commentary on the original text of the Gospel of John, receiving his Master of Divinity degree.

After returning to China in the same year, Zhang taught in the English Department at Jinling University in Nanjing and participated in the hymn editorial committee, which included Fan Tianxiang (Bliss Wiant), co-editing “Praise to the Lord.” In the spring of 1937, he was appointed to teach New Testament studies at Jinling Theological Seminary in Nanjing. From 1937 to 1938, Zhang returned to Union Theological Seminary in New York to pursue a doctoral degree, with his dissertation titled “A Study of the Material Used by the Author of the Gospel of Matthew,” and graduated successfully in 1938. During the Second Sino-Japanese War, Jinling Theological Seminary moved to the Shanghai concession, and Zhang returned to Shanghai. In 1940, he went to Chengdu, Sichuan, to plan for the Chengdu branch of Jinling Theological Seminary, while also starting to organize the translation and editorial work of “Christian Classics.” From 1944 to 1946, he further developed plans in the United States. However, the original plan to translate 54 volumes of classic literature was forced to be shelved due to the war.

In 1946, Zhang returned to Nanjing and experienced the “liberation” of Nanjing in April 1949, but finally left China in December 1951 and returned to the United States. He taught at Drew Theological Seminary while also serving as editor of “China’s News in Brief” for 11 years until his retirement in 1960. During this period, he restarted the editorial project of “Christian Classics.” In 1958, at Zhang Wenxin’s invitation, Xie Fuyan arrived in the U.S. to take on the editorial work for “Christian Classics.”

Zhang Wenxin passed away on September 3, 1975.

  1. Lin Naimei (Lin Naimei n.d.)

Lin Naimei (Lin Naimei, 1928-2006) was the founder of the first family church in Wenzhou in 1960. Born into a Christian family, she participated in the youth group while working in a coal brick factory in 1954 and abandoned her faith. In April 1959, she was injured by an explosion while working in the kiln and, after resting at home for a month, experienced a rebirth, weeping and confessing her sins. From then on, she began evangelizing in the factory, which led to her expulsion from the Party. In October, she received a revelation in a vision to preach the gospel for the Lord. In December, she saw the five large characters “Jehovah is Holy” in a vision. By the end of 1960, she began holding gatherings in her home and faced persecution during this period.

In 1969, she participated in the preparation for the establishment of the “Wenzhou Christian Church General Association,” which was officially founded in 1970 with Lin as the person in charge. From 1971 to 1980, a total of 1,759 meeting points were established in the Wenzhou area, and spiritual retreats for believers were held two to four times a year in various locations.In 1980, Lin hoped to master the Three-Self movement by participating in it, which ultimately led to the first split within the Wenzhou family church.

Lin passed away on March 27, 2006.

  1. Huang Peiyong (Ding Guangxun 1998: 486-487; Shen Derong 2000a: 63-64; Li Shoubau 1962: 23-24)

Huang Peiyong passed away on December 8, 1962.

Huang Peiyong (Hwang Pei Yung, 1917-1962) was from Fuzhou, Fujian, and had a Methodist background. He studied at Fuzhou Yinghua Middle School and Shanghai Hujiang University. He served as the chairman of the Shanghai United Church (1940). After graduating from the sociology department of Hujiang University in 1941, he joined the YMCA, assisting Jiang Wenhan in student work. During the Anti-Japanese War, he relocated with the YMCA and taught at Chongqing Theological Seminary after the war. After 1949, he worked in the Student Department of the National China YMCA and was ordained as a pastor by the Methodist Church in 1953, also serving as an associate pastor at Shanghai Mool Church.

Huang Peiyong was a key figure actively involved in the Three-Self Movement. He was the initiator of the Three-Self Declaration and served as the secretary of the Three-Self Reform Movement’s training class and a member of the study committee. As early as January 1950, he published an article in “Tianfeng” titled “Starting from Understanding to Engage Thoughts—Talking about Christianity and Marxism with Youth Friends,” opposing the attempt to reconcile Christianity with dialectical materialism or historical materialism, arguing that one must never “force a fit,” otherwise it would result in something that was “neither materialism nor Christianity.” Huang also pointed out that the most urgent task for Christians today is to stand in their positions under the overall goal of opposing imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism, cooperating with the Party and the government to serve the people and build a new China. In this issue, Christians did not have a “special mission.” Therefore, Christians and materialists should acknowledge that, although it is “impossible to be entirely consistent,” this contradiction can achieve “unity” in the action of “serving the people.” The reconciliation theory that Huang opposed was precisely the view of Wu Yaozong, whose attempt to reconcile Christianity and materialism was also not accepted by the Communist Party.

In 1955, under the pen name “Qin Mu,” he wrote “The Correct Meaning and Distortion of ‘Believers and Non-believers Cannot Share the Same Yoke’” in response to Wang Mingdao, who opposed the Three-Self Movement on the grounds of “different faiths.” Later, he also played a significant role in the Anti-Rightist Movement, being a member of the Three-Self Study Group in the Anti-Rightist Movement, “standing at the forefront of the struggle and steadfastly repelling the rampant attacks from rightists together with the Party’s leadership and the majority of fellow workers.” During the Great Leap Forward, he “clearly saw the great correctness of the general line, the Great Leap Forward, and the Three Red Banners,” “sincerely supporting the Party’s leadership and the Three Red Banners, and was filled with confidence about the country’s future.”

In the 1960s, Huang mainly wrote articles in “Tianfeng” condemning the imperialist use of Christianity for invasion and participated in the organization of historical materials. It can be said that Huang Peiyong was a key figure in the Three-Self Movement after the establishment of the Communist Party in China.

  1. Zhao Fusan (Wu Zongsu 2017: 5-7)

Zhao Fusan (Zhao Fusan, 1926-2015) was born into a Christian family in Shanghai. His mother, Wang Shuyi, was the tenth daughter of Pastor Wang Yuchu. His father, Zhao Shike, had leftist thoughts. Zhao entered the economics department of St. John’s University in Shanghai in 1943 and graduated in 1946. While studying at St. John’s University, he joined the Communist Party and became an underground member. In 1947, he served as an officer in the Student Department of the Beijing YMCA while also being the executive officer of the Student Relief Association, engaging in the Christian student movement and student relief work. In 1949, he served as an officer in the Beijing Christian Alliance and was the deputy secretary-general of the Beijing YMCA. In 1950, he participated in the Three-Self Movement, being one of the initiators of the Three-Self Declaration, playing a crucial role in promoting the Three-Self Movement in Beijing.

In 1953, he was ordained as a deacon by the Chinese Anglican Church (and was ordained as a pastor the following year), serving as the registrar at Yenching Union Theological Seminary. He later became the general secretary of the North China Diocese of the Chinese Anglican Church and the vice-chairman of the Beijing Three-Self Association. In 1964, when the Institute of World Religions was established under the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Zhao was hired as the director of the Christian Research Department. During the Cultural Revolution, he was subjected to scrutiny for ten years. In 1977, when the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences was established, he was appointed as a researcher at the Institute of World Religions. In 1980, he became the deputy secretary of the Party Committee of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences. In 1981, he served as the vice-chairman of the Beijing Three-Self Association. In 1985, he became the vice-president of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences and was also the vice-chairman of the third and fourth sessions of the Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee. After the “June Fourth Incident” in 1989, he publicly condemned the Chinese government in Paris and subsequently refused to return to China, moving to the United States. In June 1990, he was stripped of his positions, including that of a member of the Standing Committee of the National People’s Congress and of the Foreign Affairs Committee of the National People’s Congress.In 1979, under the pen name “Yang Zhen,” he published “An Outline of Christian History” (Beijing: Sanlian), organizing the history of Christianity from a materialist historical perspective. After moving to the United States, he translated “A History of European Culture” (P. J. A. N. Rietbergen), “A History of European Thought” (Friedrick Heer), and “A Brief History of Chinese Philosophy” (Feng Youlan); he authored “Historical Pages: Essays on Western Cultural History” (2009) and “Reflections” (2011).

He passed away in the United States on July 15, 2015.

  1. Cao Shengjie (Cao Shengjie 2016; Wu Zongsu 2011: 581)

In 1962, Cao Shengjie served as the secretary for Wu Yaozong, taking over the work from the former secretary, Ji Ruilan. According to Wu Yaozong’s son Wu Zongsu, Ji “was not politically reliable,” thus “several trusted individuals were sent from above to take over Ji’s work,” one of whom was “aligned with the ideological direction of our father.”

In her recollections, Cao noted that since 1962, when she was transferred to the national Three-Self organization, she served as Wu’s secretary. “My secretarial duties were not heavy; my daily work mainly involved reading documents, newspapers, and important articles from domestic and foreign publications for him… His health was poor; colleagues from the national Three-Self organization often advised him not to come to the office, that he could have matters reported at home. However, apart from his illness, he came to the office punctually every day, and I read the newspapers for him daily.” Cao served as Wu Yaozong’s secretary until the Cultural Revolution in 1966.

Cao Shengjie (1931-), originally from Yixing, Jiangsu, was adopted by a Christian family in Shanghai. She received Christian education from a young age, attending Mailun Kindergarten, Hui’en Primary School, and Jinde Girls’ School. In 1949, she enrolled in the Central Theological Seminary of the Anglican Church. The Central Theological Seminary merged with Jinling Union Theological Seminary in 1952, and Cao graduated from Jinling in 1953. After graduation, she served as a preacher at St. Peter’s Church while participating in the Shanghai Christian Youth Workers Fellowship (Youth Workers Fellowship). In 1954, she attended the National Christian Conference as a recorder. In 1955, the Youth Workers Fellowship published a youth-oriented magazine called “Grace Words,” with Huang Peiyong serving as the chair of the publication committee and Shen Yifan as vice-chair, while Cao was the head of the editorial department.

During the Anti-Rightist Movement in 1958, Cao served as a group recorder and staff member for the Shanghai Church Study Class. After the combined service in 1958, St. Peter’s Church merged with Huai’en Church, and Cao served as a preacher at Huai’en Church. In 1962, she was transferred to the national Three-Self organization as Wu Yaozong’s secretary and was listed as part of the national Three-Self organization structure in 1965. Starting in 1960, she served as a member of the Shanghai Youth Federation.

During the Cultural Revolution, Cao participated in study and criticism sessions, during which she also wanted to establish a “Rebel Group,” but due to her background as a preacher, she was unable to proceed. At that time, the Rebel Group was led by workers who seized power from the Christian authorities. In October 1968, Cao was sent to the Jiading Outer District Agricultural School, where she remained until 1971. She later returned to Shanghai for study sessions and participated in criticisms to pass assessments. In July, she was assigned to labor at a leather chemical factory. In her memoirs, she mentioned that during the Cultural Revolution, “the most incomprehensible thing for us was that after receiving patriotic education in the 1950s, we recognized that being patriotic and loving our faith meant following the Communist Party, transforming ourselves, and helping everyone to make progress and work hard to build the motherland; how could we suddenly all become bad people, all ‘monsters and demons’?”

After the Cultural Revolution, Cao returned to work in the national Three-Self organization. In 1981, she participated in the “Hymn Group,” responsible for editing “Praise Hymns.” In December 1980, the Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences established a Religious Research Institute, and Cao entered as a Christian representative. From October 1992 to February 1993, she furthered her studies at the Bossy Institute in Switzerland.

From 1980 to 1991, she served as the deputy secretary-general of the China Christian Council. In May 1988, she was ordained as a pastor at Huai’en Church in Shanghai. From 1992 to 2002, she served as the president of the China Christian Council. She was a representative of the Shanghai People’s Congress from 1988 to 1995 and served as a member of the National Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference from 1995 to 2003. From 2003 to 2008, she was a standing member of the National Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

China Christian Population

Due to a lack of data, it is impossible to know the population of Christians in China during the 1960s.

  1. First Family Church in Wenzhou 1960 (舍禾 2015: 495-510; 林乃娒 n.d.)

舍禾 mentioned in “Jerusalem of China: The History of Christianity in Wenzhou” that the first family gathering appeared in Wenzhou in November 1960, located at Wuma Street Wuma Hall and founded by 林乃娒, with the number gradually increasing thereafter. The book also cites a government report from May 1962, mentioning the situation of family gatherings in Wenzhou:

The activities of Christian “family gatherings” have become more serious than last year. According to our internal investigation in the city, the current number of “family gatherings” has significantly increased, from three in October last year to fourteen now, including one church that has resumed (the worship chapel behind Danao Mountain, with about 500 participants in a single count, meeting twice on Sunday afternoons). The number of attendees has increased from 50 people per session each week to around 1300 people per session now. However, the number of attendees at the Western Church has reduced from over 900 people each week to over 500, as the majority have gone to participate in “family gatherings” or religious activities at the chapel behind Nanmen Mountain. Additionally, recently, Pastor 周景堯 (a person from 三溪金嶴) led a “spiritual meeting” at the home of 郑岩壽 in Ximen (a form of religious activity for nurturing believers). It continued for four nights, starting with over thirty attendees on the first night, and reaching over three hundred on the last night.

Currently, most of those participating in religious activities are working-class people, but there is also a significant increase in youth, rising from just over ten to more than eighty. They participate in activities at the Western Church on Sundays and also hold “family gatherings” on Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday nights. They invite preachers from outside to deliver sermons, and also conduct “Bible studies,” singing hymns, and other activities. Most of these youths are factory workers, with a few being students and individual kindergarten caretakers, even some Youth League members participating in worship.

By 1962, the number of family churches in Wenzhou had increased to fourteen. During the Cultural Revolution, the number of family churches in Wenzhou continued to grow. In 1969, after communication among the churches, the “Wenzhou Christian Church General Assembly” was established, with six major divisions set up in the city. Those attending the meeting included 林乃娒, 金道興, 陳榮生, 鄭大同, 張可愛, 江炳壽, 胡恩, 葉忠武, 孫志理, 葉正春, 胡方傑, 吳顯雲, 陳撒母耳, and 金山.

  1. Merger of Jinling Union Theological Seminary and Yanjing Union Theological Seminary 1962 (徐如雷 2022: 253-255)

After the Socialist Education Movement, all faculty and students of Jinling Theological Seminary participated in studies, resulting in a suspension of classes for three years. At the end of 1960, the second national Christian conference decided to continue operations of Jinling Union Theological Seminary. To concentrate manpower, it was decided to merge Yanjing Union Theological Seminary into Jinling.

In early 1961, 丁玉璋 and 徐如雷 from Jinling went to Beijing for discussions. It was agreed that those unwilling to go to Nanjing could remain in Beijing, where Jinling would establish a research office, and older staff could retire. In April 1961, Jinling Union Theological Seminary held its opening worship. However, due to an oversupply of pastoral staff after the Great Leap Forward, most students were from the pastoral staff training course, which lasted for one year, with religious and political content each occupying half. There were 86 students, including 72 men and 14 women.

From May 7 to 12, 1962, the first board meeting after the merger of Jinling and Yanjing was held, attended by 26 board members, with 11 unable to attend due to illness. One of the absentees, 賈玉銘, proposed three points: (1) Not only should theology be emphasized, but also biblical studies; (2) There must be life, and it must be spread; (3) Theological education should not only teach but also lead by example. 吳耀宗 was elected chairman of the board.

On September 13, 1962, the new academic year began, with one class for the pastoral staff training program and another for the undergraduate program. There were more than 20 undergraduates, with the duration of study extended from 4 years to 5 years. However, starting in 1963, rural areas underwent a Socialist Education Movement focusing on “Four Cleans” (cleaning accounts, cleaning warehouses, cleaning property, cleaning division of labor), while urban areas launched the “Five Antis” (against corruption, against speculation, against waste, against decentralism, against bureaucratism). The Four Cleans and Five Antis were seen as significant strategic measures to combat revisionism domestically. Later, all urban and rural socialist education movements were referred to as “Four Cleans” (cleansing politics, cleansing the economy, cleansing organizations, cleansing thoughts) movements. Religion was regarded as feudal superstition in rural areas and faced repression. After 1963, Jinling stopped admitting new undergraduate classes.In August 1966, the Red Guards, under the guise of destroying the “Four Olds,” entered the seminary, starting to burn books and beat people. In late August, all faculty and staff were ordered to gather in the auditorium, where the dissolution of the seminary was announced. Faculty members were taken to the Qinglong Mountain Red Guard Forestry Farm for labor, while a few instructors were first sent to be criticized at the Youth Association before being taken to the Red Guard Forestry Farm. The remaining staff were ordered to return home, and wages were suspended. The seminary became the command center for the Red Guards, which remained occupied throughout the Cultural Revolution for 12 years.

In 1968, the Jiangsu Provincial Revolutionary Committee’s United Front Work Group organized a study class for united front personnel, and those faculty members who had been ordered to return home were also required to attend the studies. Several years later, those laboring at the Red Guard Forestry Farm also participated in the study classes. These study classes continued until the end of the Cultural Revolution.

  1. The China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee Ceased Operations in 1966 (趙天恩, 莊婉芳 1997: 190-191)

The Cultural Revolution severely impacted the CCP’s religious management system. Apart from the United Front and religious work departments, the work of patriotic religious organizations was also paralyzed. The national Christian Three-Self organizations ceased operations at the beginning of the Cultural Revolution, with key personnel from the national Three-Self, Shanghai Three-Self, the Christian Youth Association, and the Christian United Bookstore concentrated at the Wangxianqiao Youth Association to participate in studies and criticism. Most of the leaders of the Three-Self organization became “the power holders” and were exposed and criticized by the “rebels.”

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Christian Self-Transformation (Tianfeng 1960a, 1960b, 1960c, 1961b; Wu Yaozong 1960: 7-9; Liu Liangmo 1960: 13-14, 17)

In March 1960, after publishing readers’ letters, Tianfeng began a column titled “Discussion on the Issue of How to Further Strengthen Self-Transformation,” which ran for a total of three issues by May. The primary content revolved around whether the political awareness of Christians had improved enough after the socialist education movement. In the current situation, how could self-transformation be further strengthened? What should the content of transformation include? From the discussions, it was evident that participants pointed out self-transformation is a long-term task. If some colleagues feel that they have “almost” completed it, it reflects that the “transformation” is still insufficient. In the context of “continuous revolution,” transformation needs to be ongoing. “We must dig out the roots of the bourgeois stance, excavate deeper” and “not only passively accept transformation… but actively strive for transformation.” Some raised the question of whether “pastoral staff” should transform in the same way as workers and farmers or whether they have “special transformation tasks.” However, others stated, “We have experienced over a year of labor and study (although it isn’t very short), and we have a clearer understanding of the Party and socialism. In our daily work, we can diligently follow the arrangements made by the leadership. Under these circumstances, what else should we transform?”

Later, Tianfeng published speeches by Wu Yaozong at the National People’s Congress and Liu Liangmo at the National Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference. Wu’s topic was “Christians Must Strive for Self-Transformation,” indicating that self-transformation for Christians is a long-term, arduous, and repetitive task. He pointed out that many Christians, especially pastoral staff, still have doubts and concerns about the socialist path and the Party’s leadership, and some even exhibit a belief that they have already undergone sufficient transformation. At the same time, there is a need to remain vigilant against imperialism’s conspiracies and to persist in the patriotic task of fighting against imperialism. Liu’s title was “Strengthening Self-Transformation to Keep Up with the Advancing Trends,” stating that “one must change their worldview and undertake an ideological revolution,” because “bourgeois individualism” still deeply entrenched in thought is a “poisonous weed,” “lurking deep within my soul and refusing to leave.” He pointed out that “breaking down the bourgeois worldview and individualism” is a “daunting struggle.”

In January 1961, after the conclusion of the Second National Christian Conference, Tianfeng published an article titled “Discussing the Great Situation, Deepening Anti-Imperialist Patriotic Struggles, Strengthening Self-Transformation,” reporting on the representatives’ speeches at the conference. It stated that “the anti-imperialist patriotic task has not yet been completed, and imperialism is unwilling to resign to its failure, continuously attempting to exploit Christianity to conduct invasive activities in our country.” Among them, Pastor Xiong Zhenpei from Guangzhou pointed out that imperialism continues to exploit Hong Kong and Macau as bases to use Christianity for various conspiracies, including sending reactionary propaganda from Hong Kong. The representatives unanimously affirmed, “Participating in political study, engaging in productive labor and labor training, and social practice are the fundamental ways for us Christians to transform our political stance and ideology,” urging more effort to transform ourselves and keep pace with the situation.

  1. The Second National Christian Conference of China 1960-61 (Shen Derong 2000b: 45-50; Deng Zhaoming 1997: 121-122)

In November 1960, the Second National Christian Conference of China was held in Shanghai. The conference took place from November 12 to January 8, 1961, a total of 59 days of preparatory meetings, with the official session occurring from January 9 to January 14. According to regulations, the national Christian conference is convened once every three years; thus, the first conference was held in 1954, and this conference should have been held in 1957. However, due to the Anti-Rightist Movement and the Great Leap Forward, the meeting was ultimately postponed until the end of 1960.

According to Shen Derong, the content of the “Divine Immortal Meeting” revolved around four aspects: (1) international situation, (2) domestic situation, (3) religious policy, and (4) self-transformation. Representatives were divided into different groups to discuss the four major issues, including “Some people did not clearly understand the nature of the two major camps at that time—the socialist camp and the imperialist camp, expressing the view of ‘whoever has milk is the mother,’” and “Some viewed the natural disasters and work errors of the past three years rather pessimistically, believing that the domestic situation was extremely difficult and the future was bleak.” Regarding religious policy, “Some representatives believed that the individual cadres in their locale who violated religious policy represented a government policy problem.” There were also representatives who denied “the necessity of self-transformation”… Considering that many Christians had experienced the aftermath of the Anti-Rightist struggle and the impacts of the Great Leap Forward, the issues discussed were less about spontaneous proposals by the attending representatives and more about the discussion points outlined by the conference.Shen pointed out that the conference arranged for experts to provide guidance, “to lead everyone’s viewpoints toward consistency and reach consensus.” In the end, each person “arrived at conclusions through their own thinking and collective discussion.” It is evident that the conference had already reached conclusions on the aforementioned issues.

As the chair of the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee, Wu Yaozong did not read the work report of the last session; instead, it was read by Vice-Chair Wu Yifang. The report emphasized that there are “still many issues” in Christianity, particularly that the ideologies of imperialism and the “reactionary forces” within Christianity have not been “thoroughly eliminated,” and that there is a need for long-term “self-transformation” and “complete eradication” of various illegal activities. The report did not touch upon church ministry but only mentioned that “further research and efforts are needed.”

The conference passed a resolution proposing that the tasks of the Three-Self Movement going forward are: (1) to continue to uphold the banner of anti-imperialism and patriotism, oppose imperialist invasion wars, and defend world peace; (2) to follow the socialist path and continue to promote participation in various socialist construction endeavors with all fellow workers and the entire nation; (3) to continue strengthening political study, actively participate in productive labor and social practice, and enhance the transformation of political stances and political thoughts; (4) to assist the people’s government in implementing the policy of religious freedom, arranging religious life on the premise of compliance with production, national policies, laws, and socialist interests.

Wu Yaozong was re-elected as the chair of the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee, with Li Chuwen serving as the secretary-general.

  1. The Chinese Episcopal Church Protests the Statement by the U.S. Episcopal Church 1961 (Tianfeng 1961a: 32)

In August 1960, the presiding bishop of the U.S. Episcopal Church sent a circular to archbishops, primates, and presiding bishops of Episcopal Churches worldwide, stating that as of July 6, 1960, Taiwan was designated as a missionary area of the U.S. Episcopal Church, with Bishop Harry S. Kennedy of Hawaii appointed to oversee the Taiwan missionary area.

In the January-February issue of Tianfeng in 1961, an article titled “The Chinese Episcopal Church’s Statement Protesting the U.S. Episcopal Church’s Infringement on Our Sovereignty” was published. It stated that Taiwan is “an inseparable part of our territory” and “the Taiwan Episcopal Church is certainly part of the Chinese Episcopal Church,” also noting that as early as 1956, the Chinese Episcopal Church’s publication, Shenggong, had published resolutions from the General Assembly of the Chinese Episcopal Church and the Standing Committee, stating that the ecclesiastical jurisdiction of the Taiwan Episcopal Church belonged to the Chinese Episcopal Church and was designated under the jurisdiction of the Chinese Episcopal Church’s Fujian Diocese. Moreover, Bishop Ding Guangxun, while attending the Lambeth Conference held in the UK in July 1956, informed the attending bishops from various countries (including the U.S. bishops) about the resolution. Therefore, the Chinese Episcopal Church pointed out that the U.S. Episcopal Church’s “absurd decision” “completely exposes the conspiracy and intentions of a minority of imperialists within the U.S. Episcopal Church” and believes that this is closely related to the recent aggressive policy of the U.S. imperialists against our country. In response, Chinese Christians and the entire nation “firmly oppose the U.S. imperialist policy hostile to China” and solemnly declare, “The decision to designate the Taiwan Episcopal Church as a missionary area of the U.S. Episcopal Church is completely invalid.”

The statement was jointly issued by “Chairman of the Chinese Episcopal Church’s House of Bishops Chen Jianzhen” and “Secretary of the Standing Committee of the Chinese Episcopal Church General Assembly Ding Guangxun.” This is a rare statement issued in the name of religion after a joint worship service.

  1. Criticism of American Imperialist Missionary Activities (Wu Yaozong 1960: 8-9, 1962: 3-8; Sheng Min 1961: 7; Ping Fu 1961: 13-15; Yang Zhen 1965: 63-71)

In 1960, while speaking at the National People’s Congress, Wu Yaozong indicated that under the circumstances where “eastern winds overwhelm western winds,” American-led imperialism sought to promote the “internal evolution” of the socialist camp through subversion, division, and corrosion. He specifically mentioned that American theologian Niebuhr was “notorious for his anti-communism,” openly advocating the use of nuclear weapons against communism from a theological perspective. He also pointed out that the president of the American Christian Association’s visit to Taiwan at the end of 1959 was also a “blatant invasion conspiracy.”

During the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1961, Tianfeng published several articles stating that American imperialism was using religion to invade Cuba. Additionally, there were criticisms directed at the U.S. Congress’s “Un-American Activities Committee” regarding Chinese people’s communes and religious freedom, highlighting that these criticisms were fabrications by American imperialism.

In 1962, Wu Yaozong published an article titled “The ‘New Strategy’ of American Imperialism’s ‘Missionary Endeavors’,” reviewing how the missionary movement had changed strategies since the 1950s to disguise its aggressive and reactionary nature. He stated that Hong Kong and Taiwan had become bases for American imperialism, attempting to “encircle” New China and “ensure their ‘missionary endeavors’ in China become ‘immortal’.” Furthermore, Wu criticized the missionary enterprises in Africa as serving “neo-colonialism.” He reminded Chinese Christians to remain vigilant against American imperialism’s new conspiracies using Christianity, suggesting they sought to sow discord between us and the broad masses.

Subsequently, Tianfeng published numerous articles continuing to address American imperialism’s use of Christianity for invasion: “Looking at American Imperialism’s ‘New Strategy’ in Using Christianity from the Experience of Chinese Churches,” “The Substance of American Imperialism’s ‘Missionary Endeavors’ from a Publication,” “The ‘New Posture’ of American Missionaries,” “Why American Imperialism’s ‘Missionary Endeavors’ Pay Special Attention to Africa,” “Some Materials from the ‘Christian Century’ Weekly Indicating America’s Rapid Trend Towards Fascism,” and “American Imperialism is Intensifying its Utilization of Christianity,” among others.

  1. Suspension of Tianfeng in 1965 (Shen Derong 1995: 3)

Since 1951, Tianfeng had become the official publication of the national Three-Self organizations of Christianity, and its editorial policy and content had been adjusted in line with the political situation in China and the development of the Three-Self Movement. After 1958, the political tone of Tianfeng became increasingly pronounced. From the specials in Tianfeng, it can be seen that the remarks of some participants were all aligned with the current political situation (such as “eastern winds overpowering western winds,” “clearing illegal activities within the church”), affirming the achievements of the Great Leap Forward (“superiority of communes,” “great changes in rural areas,” “pleasure in labor”), self-transformation, and criticism of imperialism’s use of Christianity, among other heavily politicized topics. Aside from responses and reports on political issues, there were occasional reports on meetings of local Three-Self organizations regarding church activities or faith-based teachings, which constituted only a small proportion. This reflects both the limitations of the Three-Self organizations as patriotic religious groups in their functions and the issues arising from the lack of relevant institutions that coordinate church affairs after the abolition of sectarian organizations.

Starting in July 1960, Tianfeng suspended publication without any announcement, only resuming at the end of February 1961. From 1961, Tianfeng changed from a bi-monthly to a monthly publication. By December 1964, after publishing issues 650 and 651, it ceased publication for half a year starting in 1965. By June, only one new issue was published before it was completely suspended.(7) National Three-Self Organizations’ Learning and Criticism Campaign 1966-1968 (Cao Shengjie 2016: 99-100; Yao Minquan 2012: 4-5; Wickeri 2007: 179)

After the Cultural Revolution, the national Three-Self Movement was managed under the Shanghai United Front system. Staff from the national Three-Self, Shanghai Three-Self, the Christian Young Men’s and Young Women’s Associations, and the Christian United Bookstore gathered at the Buxi Bridge Youth Association to participate in the movement, which primarily focused on learning and criticism. At the beginning of the movement, there were several rebel groups within Christianity, and the Religious Affairs Bureau had its own rebel faction, as did the workers’ rebel teams from patriotic Christian groups. However, the rebel groups within Christianity were later disbanded, and the movement was mainly led by workers’ rebel teams. They seized power from the intellectuals within the Christian elite, demanding that everyone provide confessions and self-criticisms.

At that time, the main authorities in Christianity were Wu Yaozong (Chairman of the National Three-Self), Li Chuwen (Secretary-General of the National Three-Self), Li Shoubao (National Association of Young Men’s Associations), Shi Ruzhang (National Association of Young Women’s Associations), Luo Guanzong (Shanghai Young Men’s Association), among others, who became targets of denunciation through big-character posters. Red Guards would also come in to rebel at any time. Ding Guangxun pointed out that it was the former Secretary-General Li Chuwen who led the persecution against Zheng. At the night struggle sessions, Li Shoubao, Shi Ruzhang, Yin Xiang, Luo Guanzong, Shen Derong, and others questioned Zheng Jianye, asking, “Before liberation, your father (Bishop Zheng Hefu) was just in Canada; how did he return home right after liberation? What conspiracy did imperialism promise you?” “What were you trying to organize in the Zheng Hefu Diocese? What were your intentions?” Zheng replied, “I have a brother who joined the Chinese Communist Party; he is a Trotskyist,” “We returned to support the new China,” “Wu Yaozong wanted me to return to take over Tianfeng”…

By October 1968, the criticism campaign against the Young Men’s Association had basically concluded. Aside from the authorities, others were sent to the Wuwu Re-education Farm in Jiading for rural education.

  1. Western Christian Theology Serving Imperialism 1964 (楊真 1964: 38-44)

In November 1964, the CCP theoretical publication ‘Hongqi’ published an article entitled ‘How Western Christian Theology Serves Imperialism’, signed by “楊真”, which is the pen name of 趙復三 (1926-2015). At that time, 趙 was the head of the Christian Studies Office at the newly established World Religious Studies Institute, responsible for critical work on Christianity.

In the article, 楊 (趙) stated: “Throughout history, all exploitative classes have always proclaimed religion and theology as ‘supra-class’ and ‘super-political’ absolute truths, used to deceive and numb the people, to maintain their reactionary rule.” He particularly pointed out that “bourgeois theologians” use “religious terminology as a disguise”, claiming “starting from ‘God’s revelation’ and ‘the eternal truth of the Bible’, to construct a system of various reactionary thoughts, pressing it down on the vast people opposing capitalist rule.” He noted that the theology of these theologians “originates from politics and ultimately serves politics.”

He also specifically targeted the United States, indicating that “theology is shamelessly used to serve the aggressive policies and war policies of American imperialism”: (1) American imperialism threatens to establish its world hegemony through a new world war, with “nuclear deceit” becoming the foundation of its foreign policy. Western theologians have also transformed “nuclear deceit” into a historical view of Christianity, stressing the horrors of nuclear war and calling for people to bear suffering; he termed this as “nuclear deceit theology.” (2) To suppress the independence and liberation movements of colonized peoples and the people’s revolution, reactionary Western theologians promote a theology of “peaceful coexistence”, while vilifying the “struggle against bourgeois ideology”, portraying the activities of bourgeois individuals as “reflections of universal humanity.” (3) To conceal the crimes of imperialist colonial rule, reactionary theologians promote imperialism’s aggression against Asia, Africa, and Latin America as “Westerners being enterprising” and “Western civilization being vibrant”, depicting capitalism as the pinnacle of Western Christian civilization. Thus, the aggressions of imperialist colonial colonialism bear no crimes, instead blaming the people of Asia, Africa, and Latin America for being “unaspiring” and “lacking initiative.” Furthermore, the current anti-imperialist struggles of peoples everywhere are “distorted as being caused by historical legacies of ‘psychological barriers’ and ‘mutual suspicion’”, masking the class oppression and exploitation of colonized peoples. He also indicated that “the redemption of Christ”, rather than national liberation or social revolution, is the only hope of the world. The essence of this “missionary theology” is “the reactionary historical view of imperialism, bourgeois anthropology, and ‘cosmopolitanism’.”Finally, 楊 (趙) stated that under the illumination of Marxism-Leninism, Christianity and its theology, no matter how they change their appearance, can no longer deceive. “With the exposure of imperialism’s aggressive policies and war policies, the reactionary nature of Western Christian theology is increasingly being recognized by people. The people of the world are bound to revolution; no matter what tricks Western theologians play, they cannot save the doomed fate of imperialism.”

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. You Qiji and Liu Junwang (You Xiang, Liu Junwang 1963, 1964a, 1964b; Zhao Tian’en, Zhuang Wanfang 1997)

Between 1963 and 1964, You Xiang and Liu Junwang published several articles criticizing religion through the lens of struggle. Although their focus was primarily on religion, their anti-religious thinking reflected the struggle and criticism of religion during the period from 1963 to before the Cultural Revolution.

In September 1963, they published “Several Issues Regarding the Marxist-Leninist View of Religion” in “New Construction,” pointing out that in a class society, the oppression of social forces is the main social root of religion. They noted that Marx’s view of religion as “the opium of the people” revealed the common essence of various religions throughout different periods, which is “to deceive and narcotize people,” “to obliterate people’s fighting spirit, seducing them to escape reality,” and hindering people from “correctly waging the struggle to transform nature and society.” They acknowledged that some religious professionals contributed to science and cultural arts, and even historically, some people’s revolutionary movements used the guise of religion for cover, but this “does not equate the revolutionary thoughts of the people with the religious forms they adopted; science and cultural arts are fundamentally not the same as religion.” The aforementioned phenomenon does not negate that religion is “the opium of the people.” Finally, they argued that religion would not fade away on its own; hence, people must create conditions to “promote the disappearance of religion.” The conditions for the demise of religion are the disappearance of its roots, particularly referring to the exploiting classes and their systems, which can only be gradually realized after the proletariat seizes power. Also, only when dialectical materialism occupies a dominant position in people’s thoughts can religion be completely excluded from people’s understanding. Therefore, “the conditions for the disappearance of religion can only fully mature in a communist society” and “religion will only completely disappear in a communist society.”

In February 1964, the Chinese Communist Party’s theoretical publication “Red Flag” published an article by You Xiang and Liu Junwang titled “Correct Understanding and Handling of Religious Issues.” The article established that “religion is the opium that anesthetizes the people” and further pointed to the current situation in China from the nature of religion: “The reactionary class enemies are conducting destructive activities under the guise of religion. Among them, some hold positions of power in certain religious organizations, controlling the believer masses spiritually and thereby influencing the political lives of some believers to a certain extent. In short, the class enemies ‘dressed in religious guise’ are not only the past targets of ‘imperialist elements’ and other ‘reactionary elements,’ but also ‘the ruling faction’ within religious organizations.” This rhetoric is in line with Mao Zedong’s efforts to overthrow the “capitalist roaders” and “ruling factions” within the Party.

The article also emphasized the “political alliance” between the Communist Party and religious believers, reiterating that this absolutely does not represent the Communist Party’s acceptance of the “worldview” of religion. Therefore, “the Party must implement the principle of both unity and struggle with religious figures, continually promoting their political ideological reform, gradually freeing them from the influence of domestic and foreign reactionaries, and assisting them in keeping up with the development of the situation.” In other words, unity must necessarily go through “certain struggles.” The entirely erroneous notion of “peaceful coexistence” without struggle is rejected. This struggle involves continuously “strengthening ideological education” for believers, “gradually eliminating the roots that produce religion, gradually weakening the influence of religion among the masses, and promoting the disappearance of religion.” Thus, “we must struggle against religion” and “we must be adept at struggling against religion.”

So, how do we struggle against religion? How do we promote the disappearance of religion? You and Liu disagree with the view that religion will “fade away on its own.” On the contrary, they assert that “we must rely on the proletarian party to strengthen political ideological education for the masses” and “propagate and educate on atheism,” otherwise “the flood of religious superstitions will emerge.” Notably, although the two do not agree with using “coercive methods such as administrative orders to struggle against religion,” they also state: this “does not mean that in practical work we can set aside the struggle to promote the disappearance of religion, adopt a waiting attitude for religion to disappear, or even allow religious forces to expand unchecked.” Rather, we must “utilize the favorable conditions for social development, actively engage in work, strengthen atheistic propaganda, and popularize scientific and cultural knowledge education, so that the masses consciously abandon religious superstitions.”

In March, You Xiang and Liu Junwang wrote another article in “Guangming Daily,” clearly pointing out how to struggle with religion during the transitional period. They emphasized that religion has always defended “exploitation and oppression systems,” that “the exploiting classes particularly appreciate religion,” and that they “use religion to narcotize the working people.” Currently, in the phase of “transitioning from capitalism to communism,” there must still be “class struggle” in the realm of religion.Taking Christianity and Catholicism as examples, although imperialism has been “driven away,” it still “refuses to accept defeat and is always ready to make a comeback,” “fantasizing about restoring the colonial and semi-colonial old appearance of Catholicism and Christianity in our country.” Therefore, “as long as imperialism exists, this class struggle will not cease.” The article specifically warns that imperialists and reactionaries expect to “weaken the ideological front of socialism by expanding religious power and influence.” This “class struggle” often takes place “under the guise of religion,” bearing “an ‘outer shell’” and “a layer of ‘protection,’” making it difficult for people to perceive. Moreover, even though patriotic figures within the religious community are targets for “unity,” they “have contradictions with the proletariat in terms of political thought and religious propagation,” necessitating the implementation of policies of “unity, education, and transformation.” At the same time, “the state must also adopt appropriate administrative measures” to manage certain religious activities that “conflict with national, social, and collective interests.”

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. The Communist Party of China (Duan Dezhi 2013: 115-124; Jiang Ping 1996: 406-407; People’s Daily 1966a; Lin Biao 1966; Yang 2012: 73-74)

During the Great Leap Forward in 1958, the Communist Party of China (CPC) had already raised its line of eliminating religion, using Wenzhou as a pilot area for being “without religion.” In terms of Christianity, a large number of churches were closed following joint worship, and denominational organizations were considered a form of colonialism and ceased operations. The CPC could be said to have comprehensively controlled Christianity through the Three-Self Patriotic Movement. However, the serious mistakes during the Great Leap Forward led to contradictions within the CPC regarding its line. Between 1960 and 1962, adjustments were made against the extreme “left” line. However, the adjustments made in the early 1960s were not made with the premise of correcting political mistakes; they were merely adjustments in execution. The destruction of the religious sphere caused by the Great Leap Forward was only understood as impatience and excess in execution. However, benefiting from the adjustment policies, the immediate goal of eliminating religion was temporarily shelved, and there was some degree of temporary relaxation in religious work, with religious activities showing signs of recovery across various regions.

The relaxation from 1960 to 1962 lasted only a short time. In the second half of 1962, the leftist trend resurfaced as the political situation in China rapidly deteriorated. In August, Mao Zedong emphasized at the preparatory meeting of the 10th Plenary Session of the 8th Central Committee that in a socialist country, “there should still be recognition of the existence” of class struggle. He even stated, “From now on, we will talk about it every year, every month; whenever we hold a Central Committee plenary session, we will discuss it, and whenever we hold a party congress, we will address it, so that we have a clearer Marxist line.”

From November 1962 to January 1963, the 7th National Religious Work Conference was held. The meeting fully upheld the banner of “class struggle” from the 10th Plenary Session of the 8th Central Committee. From May to June 1963, the 13th National United Front Work Conference was held, during which reports involving unnamed criticisms of the Central United Front Minister Li Weihan were discussed. Scholars described this meeting as implementing the “leftist” thoughts and line, essentially “halting the united front work that included religious work.” On December 26, 1964, the Central United Front Department submitted a report to the Central Committee regarding Comrade Li Weihan’s issues, listing eight charges against him, including “maintaining religious forces and attacking the party’s democratic reform of the religious system.” On December 25, the Central Committee decided to remove Li Weihan from his position as Minister of the Central United Front Department.

In May 1963, the Central Committee issued “Decisions (Draft) on Certain Issues in Current Rural Work,” which judged the current situation as: “Class, class contradictions, and class struggles still exist; there is a struggle between the socialist and capitalist paths, and there is a danger of capitalist restoration.” The document clearly pointed out that “serious and sharp class struggle situations have emerged in Chinese society,” which includes “landlord and rich peasant elements and counter-revolutionaries using religion and reactionary sects to deceive the masses and engage in evil activities.”

On May 16, 1966, an enlarged Politburo meeting of the Central Committee passed the “May 16 Notice” drafted by Mao Zedong, describing that “representatives of the bourgeoisie” had already infiltrated the grassroots of the party and government. On May 28, the “Central Cultural Revolution Group” was established, led by Chen Boda, with members including Jiang Qing, Kang Sheng, Yao Wenyuan, and Zhang Chunqiao. On June 1, the People’s Daily published an editorial titled “Sweep Away All Monsters and Demons,” announcing: “A climax of the proletarian cultural revolution is rising in socialist China, which occupies one-quarter of the world’s population.” The purpose of this cultural revolution was to “thoroughly break the poisonous old ideas, old culture, old customs, and old habits created by all exploitative classes over thousands of years, and create and form new proletarian new ideas, new culture, new customs, and new habits among the broad masses of people.” Religion, as part of the superstructure, was also an important battleground that needed to be fought over. In August, the 11th Plenary Session of the 8th Central Committee passed the “Decision on the Proletarian Cultural Revolution,” marking the full launch of the Cultural Revolution. Lin Biao called on the Red Guards at the “Celebration of the Cultural Revolution” to “destroy all the old ideas, old culture, old customs, and old habits of the exploitative classes.” Red Guards and rebels across various regions, under the banner of “breaking the four olds” and overthrowing “feudalism, capitalism, and revisionism,” attacked party and government institutions as well as individuals from all walks of life. During the Cultural Revolution, the CPC fully implemented the policy of eliminating religion in order to achieve the goal of a “religion-free” nation.(2) Christianity Research Room of the Institute of World Religions, Chinese Academy of Sciences 1964 (Jin Yijiu 2004)

At the end of 1963, the Central Foreign Affairs Group reported to the Central Committee, proposing the establishment of 14 research institutes for international issues. On December 30, Mao Zedong commented on the report: “This document is good. However, it does not mention research on religion. We have no knowledge about the three major world religions (Christianity, Islam, Buddhism), which continue to influence a large population; there is not a single research institution led by Marxists in our country, nor is there any publications worth reading on this subject… Other publications with articles written from a historical materialist perspective are also very few; for example, the few articles on Buddhism published by Ren Jiyu are rare, and I have yet to see any discussions on Christianity or Islam. Without criticizing theology, we cannot write a good history of philosophy, nor a good history of literature or world history. Please consider this point, comrades from the propaganda department.” Thus, in addition to the 14 proposed international research institutes, the Central Committee decided to establish the Institute of World Religions, located within Peking University.

In early 1964, the preparatory group for the Institute of World Religions was established. The group members included Lu Ping (President of Peking University), Ren Jiyu (Professor of Philosophy at Peking University), Yu Guangyuan (sometimes represented by Lin Jianqing from the Central Propaganda Department), Liu Daosheng (Philosophical and Social Sciences Division of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, abbreviated as “Xuebu,” which is the predecessor of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences), and Xiao Xianfa (Department of Religion of the State Council), among others.

At the end of April 1964, Feng Ding (Secretary in charge of the humanities of the Peking University Party Committee) conveyed the report from the Central Foreign Affairs Group to the Central Committee and Mao Zedong’s “Comments on Strengthening Research on Religious Issues,” and announced the establishment of the Institute of World Religions (Preparatory Office) on behalf of the Peking University Party Committee. The basic task defined by the preparatory group for the institute was: “Guided by Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought, to systematically study the current state, theory, and history of the three major religions that influence a vast population in the world (including their origins, developments, classics, sects, doctrines, etc.), critique religious theology, and promote atheistic thought. Research work must target the broad masses and closely integrate with the political struggles of both domestic and foreign realities, serving the socialist revolution and socialist construction. Conditions permitting in the future, research will gradually expand to other religions beyond the three major world religions.”

In May, the leadership of Peking University held an expert seminar at Linhuxuan, gathering the opinions and suggestions of relevant experts on the research of religious issues. In addition to the university’s party and administrative leadership, participants included Jin Yuelin, He Lin (Philosophy Institute of the Xuebu), Ji Xianlin, Jin Kemu, Ma Jian (Department of East Asian Languages of Peking University), Zhou Yiliang (History Department of Peking University), Ren Jiyu, Zhu Qianzhi, and other members of the institute, along with some individuals from the religious community who also attended the meeting.

Due to a shortage of research personnel, in June, the Central Propaganda Department and the United Front Work Department drafted a new list of personnel to be reassigned. Between July and August, the cadres who reported to the institute included Yan Tie, Guo Peng, Zhao Fusan, Shen Xixi, and Du Jiweng, among others. Ren Jiyu, Yan Tie, Guo Peng, Zhao Fusan, and Huang Xinchuan formed the core group of the institute, leading the daily work of the institute. The Institute of World Religions set up a Christianity Research Room, which was overseen by Zhao Fusan.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Regulations Restricting Religious Activities (Zhang Yinan 2010: 4; Chen Wei 2016: 83)

From the late 1950s to the early 1960s, various regions had imposed strict regulations on Christianity to different extents. For example, in Henan in 1959, there were “fifteen prohibitions”: (1) No preaching outside churches; (2) No praying for healing; (3) No exploitative behavior; (4) No discrimination or humiliation of women; (5) No illegal gatherings; (6) No interfering with production; (7) No wandering around; (8) No recruiting minors under eighteen into the faith; (9) No sheltering rich landowners and reactionaries; (10) No undermining socialism and collective economy; (11) No spreading rumors and pessimistic revolutionary propaganda; (12) No setting up places of worship or home gatherings; (13) No privately elevating clergy; (14) No confusing doctrines with party policies; (15) No contact with overseas groups.

Additionally, in May 1963, the Hangzhou Three-Self Patriotic Movement adopted the “six prohibitions”: (1) No using religion for healing or exorcism; (2) No referring patients to churches; (3) Believers must not visit each other for prayer; (4) No hosting non-active clergy for religious activities at home; (5) No instilling religious beliefs in minors; (6) Believers must not lead weddings and funerals. These regulations clearly further restrict various forms of religious activities, reflecting the class struggle occurring in the religious sphere.

  1. Cultural Revolution 1966 (People’s Daily 1966a; Lin Biao 1966)

On May 16, 1966, an expanded meeting of the Politburo of the Central Committee of the Communist Party approved the “May 16 Notification” formulated by Mao Zedong, which described that “representatives of the bourgeoisie” had infiltrated the grassroots of the party and government. On May 28, the “Central Cultural Revolution Group” was formed, led by Chen Boda, including Jiang Qing, Kang Sheng, Yao Wenyuan, and Zhang Chunqiao. On June 1, the People’s Daily published an editorial titled “Sweep Away All Monsters and Demons,” declaring: “A climax of the proletarian cultural revolution is rising in socialist China, which accounts for a quarter of the world’s population.” The purpose of this cultural revolution was to “thoroughly eliminate the old ideas, cultures, customs, and habits that have poisoned the people for thousands of years caused by all exploiting classes, and to create and form a brand new proletarian new thought, new culture, new customs, and new habits among the vast masses of the people.” As part of the superstructure, religion must also become an important battleground for struggle. In August, the Eighth Party Congress passed the “Decision on the Proletarian Cultural Revolution,” marking the full mobilization of the cultural revolution. Lin Biao, at the “Celebration of the Cultural Revolution” conference, called on the Red Guards to “break all old ideas, cultures, customs, and habits of the exploiting classes.” Red Guards and rebels across the country launched attacks on party and government agencies and various social groups under the banner of “breaking the Four Olds” and overthrowing “feudalism, capitalism, and revisionism.”

  1. Red Guards Attack Churches 1966 (Wang Yuhua 2009: 317; Cao Shengjie 2016: 98; Xiamen Christian Trinity Church 2014: 104)

On August 18, 1966, Mao Zedong issued a call for the cultural revolution to the Red Guards and students from various places who came to Beijing for coordination at Tiananmen, and the slogan “Smash the Old World” swept across the country.

On August 21, the Red Guards attacked the last four churches in Beijing (East Church [Dengshikou], South Church [Zhushikou], West Church [Gangwash], North Church [Kuanjie]), “breaking the Four Olds,” halting all religious activities, and gathering all personnel at the Rice Market Church for “re-education.” On August 24, the Red Guards in Shanghai held large banners stating “We are the critics of the old world,” and “We are the creators of the new world,” marching in the streets and launching a total attack on “bourgeois and all exploiting class ideologies.” On the same day, the Red Guards from more than ten schools, including Beijing Anti-Japanese University No.1 Affiliated School and Beijing No. 12 Girls’ School, “waved the great red flag of Mao Zedong Thought” and went to the location of the Maria Franciscan Sisters to “rebel,” ordering foreign counter-revolutionary nuns to get out. Subsequently, the Beijing People’s Committee announced the dissolution of the congregation and took over the Sacred Heart School, expelling eight foreign nuns from the country.

In Shanghai, on August 23, the Moore Church (originally belonging to the Methodist Church) became the primary target of the Red Guards. The church’s organ was destroyed, and Bibles and hymnals were burned. The church’s entrance was plastered with posters proclaiming “Down with God,” and the clergy were criticized. Additionally, in Xiamen, Fujian, the Red Guards on August 31 launched a campaign entitled “Thoroughly Eliminate Imperialist Invasion Marks,” destroying the cornerstone and plaque of the Trinity Church, which also ceased all activities, with clergy being criticized, paraded through the streets, and subjected to labor reform.Since then, the Trinity Church was changed to the District People’s Auditorium.

  1. Early Impact of the Cultural Revolution 1966 (Chi Nai 1998: 127-128)

In September 1966, Li Fuchun, a member of the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau of the Central Committee in charge of united front and religious work and Vice Premier of the State Council, instructed the united front and religious departments to investigate the impact of the Cultural Revolution on religion. In October, the State Council’s Religious Affairs Bureau conducted a survey in 25 large and medium-sized cities across the country (Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Baoding, Hohhot, Nanjing, Xuzhou, Wuxi, Suzhou, Changzhou, Yangzhou, Nantong, Zhenjiang, Jinan, Qingdao, Wuhan, Guangzhou, Shantou, Chengdu, Chongqing, Kunming, Shenyang, Xi’an, Lanzhou, Urumqi) and one special district (Shandong Heze Special District). The report indicated that before the Cultural Revolution, there were a total of 822 Catholic, Christian, Buddhist, Daoist, and Lamaist temples and churches, of which 754 were destroyed during the movement, with the most severe damage occurring to Catholic and Christian churches. Regarding religious professionals, among the 6,151 individuals in the aforementioned areas, 1,516 suffered criticism and persecution.
