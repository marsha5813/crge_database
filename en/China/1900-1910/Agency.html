China: Agency (1900-1910)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

(1) Shi Meiyu (石美玉) (Mary Stone, 1873-1954)
(Shimo 2011; Zha Shijie 1983: 92-95)

Shi Meiyu (石美玉) was born into a Christian family in Jiujiang, Jiangxi. Her father was a pastor of the Jiujiang Methodist Episcopal Church (美以美會, Methodist Episcopal Church, North), and her mother was the principal of a local Methodist girls' school. From 1882 to 1892, Shi attended the Rulison-Fish Memorial School (儒勵女學) in Jiujiang. After graduating, she pursued further studies at the University of Michigan Medical School in the United States, completing her degree in 1896 and then interning at a hospital in Chicago. In the fall of that same year, she was dispatched as a medical missionary by the Woman's Foreign Missionary Society of the Methodist Church and returned to serve in China.

Shi opened a clinic in Jiujiang and began her medical missionary work. In her first ten months in Jiujiang, she treated a total of 2,300 patients alongside another doctor, making over 300 house calls. Two years later, she received funding from Chicago to establish the Jiujiang Women's and Children's Hospital (Elizabeth Skelton Danforth Hospital), named after the donor's wife. The hospital had 95 beds and was well-equipped. However, just as it was about to open, the Boxer Rebellion broke out, and Shi Meiyu's father was killed by the rioters. As a result, she sought refuge in Japan and did not return to Jiujiang until 1901. The hospital opened in December of that year, with Shi Meiyu serving as its director.

The Jiujiang Women's and Children's Hospital also established a nursing school. In addition to teaching specialized medical knowledge, Shi conducted weekly Bible study classes for the nursing staff. During this time, Methodist missionary Hu Zunli (胡遵理) started the Knowles Bible School (諾立聖經學校) in Jiujiang to train female preachers. The school and Shi Meiyu's nursing school supported each other and worked together.

In 1906, Shi Meiyu traveled to the U.S. for surgery due to appendicitis. After her recovery, she raised funds for the hospital in America. Upon her return in 1909, she used the donations to expand the hospital. After the establishment of the Republic of China, Western medicine became more accepted in society. By 1912, the Jiujiang Hospital saw an increase in patients to 15,000, and the numbers approached 25,000 the following year. In 1914, the Rockefeller Foundation began promoting Western medicine in China, though most medical professionals remained in the cities. Shi realized the needs of rural areas and started the "Gospel Nurse" program to send nurses to rural villages, providing simple surgical procedures, free medications, vaccinations, and assistance with difficult childbirth. In 1915, she became the vice president of the Chinese Medical Association.

From 1918 to 1919, she received a grant from the Rockefeller Foundation to pursue further studies at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine (約翰‧霍普金斯大學醫學院). Meanwhile, she shared her vision with American churches about the needs of the Chinese church and encouraged Chinese students studying abroad to return and serve. During her studies in the U.S., her sister Shi Chengzhi (石成志) took charge of the hospital.

After returning to China in 1920, due to disagreements with the new theological stance of the Methodist Church, combined with the interference of the mission society in the curriculum of the Knowles Bible School, Hu Zunli resigned. Out of dissatisfaction, Hu Meiyu decided to leave the Woman's Foreign Missionary Society as well. Shi Meiyu and Hu Zunli moved to Shanghai, where they co-founded the Bethel Mission (伯特利會) and Bethel Hospital (伯特利醫院). From 1920 to 1937, the Bethel Church established secondary schools, elementary schools, a seminary, and an orphanage. Shi Meiyu also opened a nursing school in the hospital, requiring all trainee nurses to take Bible classes. By 1928, there were 137 students in the nursing school, and in the 1930s, the number exceeded 200. Later, to maintain the religious curriculum, the nursing school even refused to register with the National Government.

Shi Meiyu (石美玉) was also an outstanding evangelist, participating in the Evangelical Association of China and the Bethel Evangelistic Team.

After the outbreak of the War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression, the Bethel Theological College moved to Hong Kong, and she and Hu moved to the United States to settle down. After the victory in the war, she returned to Shanghai, but the civil war broke out again, forcing her to relocate back to California. She spent the rest of her life in America.

On December 30, 1954, Shi Meiyu passed away in California at the age of 81.

(2) Yu Guozhen (俞國楨) (Zhang Hua 1998: 57-60; Yan Li n.d.)

Yu Guozhen (俞國楨) (1852-1932), also known by the courtesy name Zongzhou (宗周), was from Fenghua, Ningbo, and was the founder of the China Independent Church. He enrolled in the Hangchow Presbyterian Boys’ School (美北長老會杭州育英義塾) in 1867, was baptized in 1868, and graduated in 1872. After graduating from Shengdao College (聖道書院) in 1877, he preached in various places such as Hangzhou and Deqing in Zhejiang, and Huizhou and Tunxi in Anhui, before being appointed to the Ningbo Presbyterian Church in Huzhou. In October 1888, Yu Guozhen was ordained as a pastor by the Presbyterian Church. In 1894, he was invited by the American Presbyterian missionary John M. W. Farnham to Shanghai, where he became the pastor of the Hongkou Presbyterian Church, the first Chinese pastor of that church.

In 1903, stimulated by the incident at the Chenzhou Church in Hunan, Yu Guozhen, along with Xie Honglai, Wang Zhenting, and others, initiated the China Christian Association, and Yu was elected as its president. At that time, Yu arranged the financing to purchase land at the intersection of Haining Road and Kenenhai Road to build a church. Half of the land was designated for the church and school, while the other half was rented out to repay loans. The new church announced the termination of economic ties with the mission society, naming itself the Zhabei Independent Presbyterian Church (閘北自立長老會堂).

In the winter of 1905, Yu Guozhen and other proponents of church independence convened the founding meeting of the China Jesus Church Independent Association, establishing a temporary office at the Haining Road Church, creating an outline and drafting a declaration. The association was announced to be established in January 1906. Subsequently, they repeatedly wrote to the Suzhou, Songjiang, and Taihu government offices, seeking approval for registration or protection, and finally obtained approval from the Shanghai governor in March 1906. From then on, independent churches were established around the country.

In December 1910, Yu Guozhen, representing 260,000 Christians nationwide, was entrusted by believers from various places to present a Bible to the royal court in Beijing to get closer to the government and “petition for the reclamation of church rights” from the Qing government to support China's independent churches.

In March 1911, Yu Guozhen, along with gentry from Zhabei, formed the Zhabei Commercial Association, which primarily consisted of merchants and aligned with members of the Tongmenghui. After its establishment, the association's president changed several times, but Yu Guozhen remained as the vice president. In November of the same year, he participated in organizing and leading the Zhabei Restoration as a pastor, becoming an important participant and witness of the Xinhai Revolution in the restoration of Shanghai. Many important members of the independent church were also active participants in the Xinhai Revolution, with many serving in the Shanghai military government. After the Wuchang Uprising broke out, as the situation was still unclear, Yu Guozhen used the publications of the Independent Church, the “Holy Newspaper” (聖報), to support the Xinhai Revolution. On January 15, 1912, at a gathering of Christians in Shanghai celebrating the establishment of the Republic of China, Yu Guozhen delivered a speech strongly advocating for the republic. He, along with more than 20 leaders from the Christian community in Shanghai, established the China Christian Republican Association, with the purpose of promoting and supporting the republic, fighting for equality between religion and education, and sharing legal rights.

In June 1920, the first national representative conference of the Independent Church was held at the Zhabei Independent Presbyterian Church in Shanghai, establishing the national headquarters of the Independent Church, with Yu Guozhen elected as the first president (later elected as lifetime president). In 1922, the China Christian Council was established, and Yu Guozhen represented the Independent Church at the conference.

In 1932, the January 28 Incident destroyed the headquarters' office, and the roof of the Independent Church's Yongzhi Hall was also damaged by shells. The main residential area of believers in Zhabei became a war zone, forcing them to flee to the foreign concessions. In November of the same year, while preaching in Wenzhou, Zhejiang, Yu Guozhen unfortunately fell ill and, due to his advanced age and frailty, passed away at the age of 80.

(3) Fan Zimei (范子美) (Xing Fuzeng 1997: 140-174)

Fan Zimei (范子美) (T. M. Van, 1866-1939), also known as Fan Yi (范禕) and by the courtesy name Bihui (皕誨), was born in Suzhou. At the age of 5, he moved to Shanghai with his father, and by 13, he had passed the county examination, qualifying as a Xiucai (秀才) and establishing a strong foundation in classical Chinese studies. In 1893, he passed the provincial examination, earning the title of Juren (舉人). However, after failing both times at the highest metropolitan examination, he decided to stop taking exams and returned to Suzhou to work as a teacher. During this period, Fan was influenced by the wave of reform, leading to drastic changes in his thinking. Discontent with traditional Confucian studies motivated him to focus on practical knowledge and mathematics, while he also extensively explored the translated works from the Jiangnan Machinery Bureau.

The defeat in the Sino-Japanese War deeply affected Fan Zimei's thoughts. Discontented with the way traditional studies failed to respond appropriately to the pressing issues of the time, he vigorously supported reforms and modernization, serving as a reporter for publications such as the Su Bao (蘇報), Shi Xue Bao (實學報), and Zhongwai Ribao (中外日報), advocating for new policies. After the Boxer Rebellion, Fan met the American Methodist missionary Lin Lezhi (Young J. Allen). Lin was the editor of the Wan Guo Gong Bao (萬國公報), which was a key publication in the dissemination of reformist ideas, profoundly influencing Fan. In 1902, Fan formally joined the editorial team of the Wan Guo Gong Bao, becoming an important ghostwriter for Lin Lezhi in his later years, and that same year, his entire family was baptized, becoming Christians.

To Fan Zimei, Christ was an essential foundation for national strength. He believed that the path to a prosperous China lay in establishing a "true education" that recognized all "Western politics" and "Western learning" stemmed from "Western teachings," and in thoroughly dismantling the shadow of "false education" (namely Confucian traditions), enabling China to escape weakness and poverty.

In September 1911, at the invitation of Baptist Convention Secretary Fletcher S. Brockman, he joined the YMCA's publications department and became the chief editor of the magazine "Progress" (進步). In March 1917, "Progress" merged with "Youth" to become "Youth Progress," and Fan continued to serve as editor until his retirement in 1935.

Fan Zimei believed that society is composed of the sum of different individuals, with the thoughts of individual members holding significant importance. This was why, although China had overthrown the monarchical autocracy and adopted a republican government, it had not achieved true democracy: "Eight years after the Republic was founded, their thoughts have not changed, so how could they understand the principles of 'democracy' and 'republic' to realize a genuine republic?" Fan firmly believed that the fundamental way to eradicate the corrupt thoughts of the populace and rid them of their "slave mentality" was to enhance the "quality of citizens," which means improving moral, intellectual, and physical qualities. Through the holistic redemption of individuals (including morality, intellect, body, and spirit) and uniting the community, he aimed to save individual souls, perfecting morality, knowledge, and physical well-being to construct an ideal national character.

The outbreak of World War I prompted Fan Zimei to reevaluate the value of Eastern and Western cultures, causing him to question the so-called "myth of Western perfection." After the May Fourth Movement, he officially proposed the "Preservation of National Essence" theory, shifting from outright opposition to traditional Chinese culture to emphasizing the distinction between "national essence" and "national affliction," asserting that preserving "national essence" did not equate to rejecting other cultures. He also criticized church schools for valuing English over Chinese, which he felt failed to cultivate talent for the Chinese church. In January 1924, Fan established the "National Studies Research Society," advocating for the re-evaluation of the value of Chinese culture to strengthen its roots. He declared that "Youth Progress" would adjust its previous Western-centric approach to equally value Chinese cultural and values.

The outbreak of the anti-Christian movement in 1922 also led him to realize that the hope for "church reform" must come from the study of doctrines, as religious tenets must be revised according to the times, environment, and nationality. The indigenization of Christianity became a key focus for Fan after the 1920s, and in his 1929 publication "My New View of Jesus," he boldly proclaimed: "If Christ is not indigenized in China, China will never be Christianized."

In 1935, at the age of 70, Fan Zimei retired from the YMCA. To acknowledge his many years of service, the YMCA held a grand farewell party and presented him with a silver bowl inscribed with "Mingshan Weiye" (名山偉業) as a commemorative gift. In September 1939, Fan Zimei passed away in Shanghai at the age of 73.

(4) Gu John (古約翰) (Zhao Ruijuan 2017: 93-102; Li Wei 1995: 227-239)

Gu John (古約翰) (Jonathan Goforth, 1859-1936) was born into a Christian family in Ontario, Canada. At the age of 18, he was moved by the Holy Spirit during a Sunday worship service, experienced rebirth, and resolved to serve God. During this time, George L. Mackay (馬偕), a missionary from the Canadian Presbyterian Church, was back in Canada on leave, and Gu was deeply inspired after hearing his speech, which planted the seed for his own thoughts on overseas missions.

After graduating from high school, Gu John entered Knox College (諾克斯神學院) in Toronto to study theology. During his studies, he read J. Hudson Taylor's (戴德生) book "China’s Spiritual Need and Claims" (《中國屬靈需要與要求》). In June 1887, the Canadian Presbyterian Church dispatched Gu John along with Dr. James F. Smith (史美德) to China for missionary work. In February 1888, Gu arrived in Shanghai with his wife and in August, moved to Yantai in Shandong to study Chinese. At that time, other missionaries from the Canadian Presbyterian Church also arrived in Yantai, and they all discussed how to develop work in Henan.

In the fall of 1889, with the assistance of American Congregational missionary Arthur H. Smith (明恩溥), Gu John and Dr. Smith began their first four-week evangelistic tour in Henan, obtaining satisfactory results through medical work. In January 1890, four Canadian Presbyterian missionaries divided into two groups and advanced further into northern Henan, with Gu and Dr. Smith continuing their collaborative work. They treated a total of 1,380 patients, with 350 receiving treatment two or more times, extracting 35 teeth and performing 105 surgeries. They felt that the medical work helped to eliminate local resistance to Christianity and recognized the necessity of establishing a hospital.

In 1891, the church successfully rented a property in Chuwang, located near the border of Henan and Shandong. In 1894, they established a missionary station in Zhangde Prefecture. In 1900, missionaries in northern Henan had to flee due to the Boxer Rebellion, and during their escape, Gu John was wounded in the neck by a knife. After the uprising, Gu returned to northern Henan, investing more resources into medical work through the church, believing that medical services must serve evangelism rather than just modern medical practices. In 1909, the mission received donations to improve medical facilities in northern Henan, but Gu felt that such donations would undermine the priority of evangelism within the church. Ultimately, the mission rejected the donation, but it caused internal conflicts among the missionaries.

In 1907, Gu John visited Korea to investigate the local revival movement. Upon returning to China, he shared his experiences in the Northeast, which resonated with the church. After arriving in Henan, he received an invitation from the Scottish and Irish Presbyterian Churches in the Northeast to lead a revival conference there. In February 1908, Gu set out for the Northeast, resulting in a significant revival in the area. Gu desired to devote more time to revival efforts across various locations, so he decided to leave northern Henan. While he maintained his status as a Canadian Presbyterian missionary, his relationship with the mission became tenuous. In 1918, he officially ended his affiliation with the northern Henan Presbyterian Church.

During his home leave in 1909, Gu John felt uneasy about the rise of liberal theology, especially higher biblical criticism. When he returned to Henan, he accused three missionaries from the northern Henan Presbyterian Church of their theological positions, forcing them to publicly declare their beliefs, which ultimately led to one resigning. In 1920, a group of missionaries formed the "Bible Union of China" to oppose liberal theology, with Gu John serving as vice president. His fundamentalist stance increasingly heightened tensions between him and the mission.

In 1934, Gu John was compelled to return to Canada due to health reasons, concluding his missionary work in China, and he passed away two years later. He authored the memoir By My Spirit, published in 1929. Later, he recounted his missionary experiences in China, which were compiled by his wife, Rosalind Goforth, under the title Miracle Lives of China (1931). After his passing, his wife wrote his biography, Goforth of China (1937).

(5) Bó Gé Lì (柏格理) (Diamond 1996: 138-57; Huang 2014; Zhang Tan 1992; Hu Qingxin 2020; Hou Lei n.d.)

Bó Gé Lì (Samuel Pollard, 1864-1915) was born into a Bible Christians Methodist family in Cornwall, England. He was baptized in 1875 and scored third in the national civil service examination in 1881, subsequently moving to London to work. While in London, he was greatly inspired by the missionary efforts of David Livingstone in Africa and resolved to dedicate himself to overseas missionary work. In 1885, at an overseas missionary conference, the speaker was James H. Taylor (戴德生), the founder of the China Inland Mission, during which Pollard felt the call to propagate the gospel in China.

In January 1887, Pollard and Frank Dymond (邰慕廉) were sent to China by the Bible Christians Methodist Mission (In 1907, the Bible Christians merged with two other Methodist denominations—the United Methodist Free Churches and the Methodist New Connexion—to form The United Methodist Church). After arriving in Shanghai in March, they moved to Anqing to learn Chinese with the China Inland Mission. In November, they traveled the Yangtze River westward through Wuhan and Chongqing, reaching Zhaotong, Yunnan in February 1888, and finally arriving in Kunming (Yunnanfu) in August.

At that time, T. G. Vanstonge (萬斯通) and Thomas Thorned (索恩), who were already engaged in missionary work in Zhaotong and Kunming, were present. In January 1892, during a meeting in Dongchuan, they decided to abandon Kunming, leaving Pollard responsible for the work in Zhaotong. In the early years of his mission, Pollard faced many challenges, and it was not until September 1893 that he baptized individuals for the first time. The work at the Zhaotong mission station mainly involved itinerant preaching, education, and medical services. Aside from returning to the UK for a break from 1895 to 1897 and seeking refuge in Shanghai during the Boxer Rebellion in 1900, Pollard's work remained focused on Zhaotong and its surrounding areas, primarily targeting the Han Chinese.

By 1903, marking 16 years since his arrival in China, the Bible Christians had established four mission stations in Yunnan, with a total of 59 baptized believers and three schools that accommodated 205 students. In addition to work among the Han people, indigenous communities began attracting Pollard's attention. In November 1903, at the invitation of a Yi chief, he entered the Liangshan Yi region in Sichuan, and upon returning to Zhaotong, he began preparing to initiate work among the Yi people.

In July 1904, four Miao seekers, guided by the China Inland Mission missionary J. R. Adam (黨居仁), proactively came to the Zhaotong mission station, which altered the trajectory of Pollard's missionary path. As the number of Miao seekers continued to rise, Pollard quickly initiated nightly worship services for the Miao and began teaching them biblical knowledge. In the autumn of 1904, he entered Miao territories for the first time, realizing the necessity of establishing a church centered on the Wumeng Mountain region for the Dahuamiao people. In early 1905, he undertook a second mission tour into Miao areas, after which Pollard shifted his mission center and settled in Shimenkan with Harry Parsons (張道惠).

In November 1905, the first church and school in the history of the Dahuamiao people were officially established in Shimenkan, with the school eventually named Guanghua Elementary School (光華小學) in 1911. More than 2,000 people attended the dedication service, and 100 were baptized into Christ. From this point on, Pollard's ministry in the Dahuamiao region began entering a phase of rapid development. In 1906, using the Latin alphabet as a basis and referencing symbols on Dahuamiao clothing, Pollard created a written language for the Miao people and translated the Bible into Miao. He initiated bilingual education in Miao and Han Chinese in Shimenkan. He also established the first outdoor swimming pool, the first soccer field, and the first Western-style hospital in the southwest region, while promoting the establishment of leprosy hospitals, orphanages, and more. In the autumn of 1906, Pollard led a group of co-workers to the Miao-dominated areas in Luquan County, Fumin County, and Wuding County, north of Kunming, with great success, gradually starting to build churches and schools, and reallocating pastoral workers from Shimenkan for support. By 1912, the Miao work in this area was handed over to the China Inland Mission.

Addressing the bad customs of the Dahuamiao, Pollard helped them implement reforms. For instance, the Miao people traditionally practiced a "group marriage system," where many villages had "宿寨房" (communal lodging houses) allowing girls to engage in sexual relations with men of their choice starting in their early teens. When Pollard brought the gospel to these villages, he initially tore down these lodging houses to eliminate the harmful effects of the group marriage system. He also applied strict disciplinary measures through the church, imposing high moral standards on believers. For those wishing to be baptized, in addition to basic faith requirements, they were also expected to abstain from opium use, alcoholism, and gambling. Furthermore, he helped the Miao reject traditional shamans. These examples illustrate that Pollard not only undertook the mission of evangelism among the Dahuamiao but also successfully practiced social reform and cultural renewal.

At the end of 1907, Pollard returned to England for a vacation, and in July 1910, he returned to Shimenkan with missionary William H. Hudspeth (王樹德). In 1915, a typhoid epidemic swept through the Wumeng Mountain area, and while caring for typhoid patients, Pollard himself tragically contracted the disease. On September 16, 1915, Rev. Pollard passed away at the age of 51. After his death, Miao believers buried him on a small hill near the Shimenkan school.

At the time of Pollard's passing, there were 4,800 baptized Miao church members, with 900 youths and 5,000 people undergoing observation. Between 1905 and 1915, an average of three chapels were built each year alongside more than ten other evangelistic areas; in total, 23 schools were established with over 1,000 students.

Pollard authored several works, including Tight Corners in China: Missionary among the Miao in South West China (1910), The Story of the Miao (1919), and In Unknown China: A Record of the Observations, Adventures and Experiences of a Pioneer Missionary during a Prolonged Sojourn Amongst the Wild and Unknown Nosu Tribe of Western China (1921).

(6) Li Shuqing (李叔青) (Wu Xiuliang 2010; Yu Ligor 2006: 256-261)

Li Shuqing (李叔青) (1875-1908), courtesy name Yansheng (延生) and also known as Ying Liu (應柳), was from Nanhui County, Jiangsu, and is regarded as the first revivalist evangelist in the history of the Chinese church in the 20th century. His father, Li Ziyi (李子義), was a pastor of the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, serving in Suzhou's Boxi Academy (the predecessor of Dongwu University) and the Jia Ding County Church. He studied at Boxi Academy in Suzhou and St. John's College in Shanghai, being baptized as an infant and affirming his faith at age 13 in the Anglican Church in Shanghai.

At the age of 19, Li Shuqing (李叔青) was admitted to the Beiyang Medical College, becoming a member of the first class. During his time studying, he lived a carefree and indulgent life. At 22, after hearing the gospel preaching of Christian and Missionary Alliance missionaries John Woodberry (伍約翰) and his wife Katherine (凱薩琳), he experienced rebirth, repented thoroughly before God and people, and began to live a simple and devout life, becoming fervent in evangelism, which brought about a transformative change in his life. He started a Bible study group at school and even wrote and published a tract titled "Jesus is Coming Soon" (《耶穌快要來》) to distribute to his classmates. His preaching and prayers were powerful, and he had a beautiful testimony. Influenced by him, more than half of the 50 students at the school became Christians. At the age of 23, he was baptized again by the Christian and Missionary Alliance in Tianjin.

After graduating in 1897, Li Shuqing expressed to the superintendent that he "only loved Jesus and wanted to proclaim the gospel of Jesus," which led to him not being recommended for a job and instead remaining at the school. During this time, he actively evangelized among students and citizens in Tianjin and became an elder at the Tianjin Christian and Missionary Alliance Church. In 1899, after sensing God's clear guidance, he informed the superintendent of his intention to return to Shanghai. Upon arriving in Shanghai, he participated in meetings at the Shanghai Christian and Missionary Alliance Church and worked alongside John Woodberry. In the spring of 1900, he decided to leave the church to teach English at the Shanghai Central and Western School, with the goal of evangelizing the students.

Although Li Shuqing's father and second brother were both pastors, he believed that they had not experienced rebirth and did not understand salvation, so he continually explained the gospel to them until they repented completely and truly accepted salvation. During his time in Suzhou in 1904, he prayed and studied the Bible with his second brother and led students to Christ. In the spring of 1905, he was hired as an English and Bible teacher at Suzhou's Boxi Academy while also evangelizing on campus, leading to a revival.

In October of that year, the Methodist Church held its annual meeting at Tiancizhuang in Suzhou. He preached about the truth of Christ's "complete salvation" to the attending clergymen, delivering a powerful sermon that even led many listeners to genuine repentance. The news of his impactful preaching spread, and people from urban churches in Suzhou flocked to Tiancizhuang to hear him, creating an atmosphere akin to a revival conference.

In 1906, Li Shuqing was invited to Shanghai to lead a ten-day revival meeting, with about 700 attendees each night. Many repented and experienced rebirth. The revival in Shanghai then spread to Nanjing, where five churches invited Li Shuqing and minister Yu Cidao to lead a ten-day revival. Each session attracted over 500 attendees, with many experiencing repentance and feeling the power of the Holy Spirit.

The revival in Nanjing spread nationwide. He subsequently led revival meetings in cities like Wuhu, Ningbo, Hangzhou, Shaoxing, Jiangyin, Chansha in Hunan, Yantai in Shandong, and Zhoujiakou, where many churches and individuals were revived through his powerful preaching. During this period, he published a quarterly known as the "Gospel Guide." In 1908, he translated J. A. Seiss's "Commentary on the Book of Revelation" into Chinese, a book that was popular in the English-speaking world.

From 1905 to 1908, Li Shuqing's revival ministry transformed many lives and later influenced the Chinese church. For example, Methodist missionary Yu Zhizhai experienced revival at the Suzhou annual meeting and later actively engaged in ministry, serving as the senior pastor of Moore Memorial Hall and Young J. Allen Hall in Shanghai. Notable Chinese church leaders such as Chen Chonggui and Cheng Jigui, as well as the theologian Cheng Ziyi, were all inspired by Li Shuqing's preaching and experienced rebirth.

Due to years of exhausting travel, preaching, teaching, and translation work, he wore himself out, eventually passing away in August 1908 at the age of 33. On his deathbed, he left behind a profound statement: "Whether in life or death, I belong to the Lord."

(7) Bǔ Fǎng Jì (卜舫濟) (Xu Yihua 2009: 12-62; Xu 1996)

Bǔ Fǎng Jì (Francis L. H. Pott, 1864-1947) was born into a Christian family within the Episcopal Church in New York, USA. After graduating from Columbia University in 1883, he entered the General Theological Seminary. While studying theology in New York, he was invited to teach English to Chinese laundry workers at a private school, which sparked his interest in China and ignited his desire to serve as a missionary there.

After graduating from the seminary in 1886, Bǔ Fǎng Jì (卜舫濟) was sent by the American Episcopal Church (聖公會) to serve as a missionary in China, arriving in Shanghai in November, where he first studied the language in Jiading. In 1887, Pott was assigned to St. John's College (聖約翰書院), the first institution of higher learning established by the American Episcopal Church in China, as an English instructor. The founders aimed to train missionaries and initially offered courses in Western studies, Chinese studies, and theology. Due to the limited proficiency of the students, instruction was delivered in both Mandarin and local dialects. In June 1888, he married Huang Su'e (黃素娥), the principal of St. Mary's School, an unusual cross-cultural marriage, even among the missionary community at the time.

In June 1888, after Pott took over as the headmaster of St. John's College, he implemented extensive reforms, strongly promoting English education through a fully Americanized English training method. Aside from Chinese literature, all subjects were to be taught using English textbooks, with teachers required to conduct classes in English and students encouraged to communicate in English as well. They were also expected to learn Western etiquette and customs. At that time, St. John's was the first school in China to use English as the medium of instruction. Meanwhile, Pott worked to elevate the school’s status, experimenting with a "main building" in 1892 to establish a college department. By 1896, the college department gained recognition from the American church, and St. John's was reorganized into "St. John's School"; in November 1905, it was officially registered in Washington, D.C. as "St. John's University," becoming an accredited church school recognized by the U.S. government, with Pott appointed as its president. He served as the president for the next 35 years, making his total time overseeing St. John's, including his earlier position as head of the college, a remarkable 53 years. During this time, he also held the position of dean of the liberal arts college and oversaw the secondary school, teaching not only English but also subjects like physics, chemistry, astronomy, and geology. For Pott, education was not just a means to an end; it was an end in itself.

Pott advocated for the separation of education and politics, opposing students engaging in political activities based on their school affiliations, which resulted in multiple conflicts with students eager to pursue political movements. Following the May 30 Incident in 1925, there was a significant clash between students and the school authorities over issues related to a strike. The school announced a holiday to prevent student protests, prompting 17 faculty members led by Meng Xiancheng (孟憲承) and 553 students to leave and subsequently form Guanghua University.

Another challenge Pott faced was the Kuomintang government's demand for church universities to register officially. The government set August 31, 1931 (later extended to June 30, 1932) as the final deadline for registration, threatening closure otherwise. As the president, Pott opposed registration; however, as the deadline approached, he had to engage with the Ministry of Education to discuss the registration issue in July 1930. Facing governmental pressure, Pott ultimately agreed to proceed with registration and submitted an application in August 1931. However, the application was rejected by the Shanghai Education Bureau, citing an excess of foreign board members and the ineligibility of having a foreigner as the vice-chairman. The bureau also argued that the existence of a seminary at the university and the school's Christian values were incompatible with the principles of the Three People's Principles education. As a result, the Episcopal Church and Pott decided not to pursue registration, as it would limit religious courses and rituals, which were "not the desire of the church." Following this, negotiations continued among the Episcopal Church, the school, and the Ministry of Education regarding registration.

After the outbreak of the War of Resistance Against Japan on August 13, 1937, St. John's relocated to the International Settlement to avoid the conflict, joining with Hujiang University, Dongwu University, and Zhijiang University to form the Shanghai United Christian University. In 1939, St. John's returned to its original site. In 1941, Pott resigned from his role as president of St. John's due to his advanced age, returning to the U.S. to spend his later years, marking the end of the "Pott Era" at St. John's. Shen Silang (沈嗣良) became the first Chinese president of the university, assisted by Pott's son, James Pott (卜其吉). In October 1946, at the age of 82, Pott returned to China, serving as the honorary president of St. John's University, with Tu Yuqing appointed as the president.

Bǔ Fǎng Jì (卜舫濟) was not only an outstanding president and professor of a Christian university, but also a Christian leader who actively participated on both the Chinese and international stages. He served as the vice president of the China Centenary Missionary Conference (基督教入華一百週年宣教大會) in 1907 and represented Chinese Christianity at the World Missionary Conference held in Edinburgh in 1910. Additionally, he held various prestigious positions such as president of the Educational Association of China (中華教育會) from 1914 to 1915, president of the China Christian Educational Association (中華基督教育會) from 1916 to 1925, president of the Association of Christian Colleges and Universities in China (中國教會大學聯合會) from 1919 to 1921, and president of the North China Royal Asiatic Society (皇家亞洲學會). During World War I, he also served as the president of the American Red Cross Society (美國紅十字會).

Bǔ Fǎng Jì had a keen interest in Chinese history and culture, with considerable research in this area. He authored works such as "The Rebellion in China" (《中國之暴動》), "The Crisis in China" (《中國之危機》); in 1905, he published "An Outline of Chinese History" (《中國歷史大綱》) and "A Summary of Chinese History" (《中國歷史概略》); in 1928, he published "A Brief History of Shanghai" (《上海簡史》). His writings also included "Commentary on 1 Corinthians" (《保羅達哥林多人前書註釋》) in 1926, "The Life of Christ" (《基督本紀》) in 1924, and "A Brief History of the Shanghai Concessions" (《上海租界略史》) in 1931.

On March 7, 1947, Bǔ Fǎng Jì passed away in Shanghai due to heart failure at the age of 83.

(8) Hú Měi (胡美) (Lian 1997: 25-58; Chapman 2001: 1-33; Lǐ Yàdīng n.d.)

Hú Měi (Edward H. Hume, 1876-1957) was born into the family of American missionaries in India. Influenced by his father, he was determined to engage in overseas missions from a young age. After graduating from Yale University in 1897, he entered the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine to study medicine, earning his M.D. in 1901. From 1903 to 1905, he worked under the United States Public Health Service to combat the plague in India. In June 1905, he was invited by the Yale-in-China Mission to establish a hospital and school in Changsha, Hunan, China.

In early 1901, several Yale graduates initiated the establishment of the Yale Foreign Missionary Society (Yale Mission), with support from the university, selecting China as their mission field. Their purpose was not direct overseas evangelism, but to reflect their Christian faith and service through educational and medical endeavors. They named this mission organization "Yale," derived from a quote from Confucian classic "The Analects" (《論語》), which states, "What the Master said with elegance were poetry, history, and proper etiquette." The name "Yale" also phonetically resonated with the English name of the university.

In 1902, Lawrence Thurston was dispatched to China for an inspection and chose Changsha, Hunan, as the focal point for educational and medical work. In 1905, Hume arrived in Changsha to study Chinese and prepare for the work ahead. By 1906, the Yali School (雅禮學堂) and Yali Hospital (the predecessor of Xiangya Hospital) were established. Yali School utilized English as the medium of instruction, with most teachers coming from Yale University, and the school did not offer religious courses, distinguishing it from typical church schools.

Due to public resistance to Western medicine, the initial patient turnout at Yali Hospital was not promising. However, after successfully treating a patient with a large abscess, as well as a cleft lip patient and a cataract case through anesthetic surgical procedures, Hume demonstrated the advantages of Western medicine over traditional Chinese practices, which helped establish the hospital's good reputation. By 1908, the hospital began admitting inpatients, and that year, Hume treated a total of 1,229 patients, including 40 inpatient cases.

After the Xinhai Revolution, Tan Yankai was appointed as the governor and military commander of Hunan Province. Hume's successful treatment of Tan's mother for severe pneumonia led to collaborative efforts, with Tan sponsoring the construction of a new modern hospital and medical university in 1913, in partnership with the Hunan Yuchun Association and the Yali Association. The hospital and medical university were named "Hsiang-ya" (湘雅), reflecting this collaboration, and the "Hsiang-ya Medical Society" was formed. In the same year, the English name of the Yali Association was changed from Yale Mission to Yale-in-China.

In 1913 and 1914, the Hsiang-ya School of Nursing (湘雅護士學校) and Hsiang-ya Medical College (湘雅醫學院) officially began enrolling students. By 1918, Hsiang-ya Hospital (湘雅醫院) was completed and operational. In 1924, the number of inpatients at the hospital rose to 2,200, with outpatient visits reaching as high as 42,000.

Since the establishment of Hsiang-ya Medical College, Hume initially served as the dean and became president in 1923. Under his leadership, Hsiang-ya became one of China's premier medical schools, cultivating many exceptional Western medical professionals and nurses. However, influenced by rising anti-imperialist sentiments in the 1920s, the medical college experienced a student strike in 1924, with 140 medical students leaving the school. In 1925, the Yali Association withdrew from the board of the Hsiang-ya Medical Society, placing the administrative and educational responsibilities entirely in the hands of the Chinese, while the Yali Association continued to send doctors and teachers for medical and educational duties, as well as financial support. In 1926, Hume resigned from the Yali Association, and soon affected by the Northern Expedition political situation, he and other foreign teachers accompanied by the U.S. government's repatriation policies returned to America. The Hsiang-ya Medical College and School of Nursing were suspended, resuming operations only in 1929. Starting in 1929, the administration of Hsiang-ya was entirely managed by Chinese personnel, and in 1931, they registered with the Ministry of Education.

After returning to the U.S. in 1927, Hume became the director and executive vice president of the New York Post-Graduate Medical School and Hospital. Between 1934 and 1937, he returned to China to conduct a survey on medical equipment. In 1938, he traveled to India for a similar survey. He also co-founded the Christian Medical Council for Overseas Work, serving as its executive director from 1938 to 1946.

As a Western physician, Hume gradually developed a more favorable view of traditional Chinese medicine after coming to China. He later authored works such as The Chinese Way in Medicine and Doctors East, Doctors West: An American Physician's Life in China, expressing his affirmation of Chinese traditional culture. In his eyes, the Chinese were no longer pagan individuals waiting for missionaries to save them but rather lived in a self-sufficient and prosperous culture. Furthermore, in facing the challenges of Chinese nationalism and the anti-Christian movement, Hume reflected on the direction of the missionary movement. He firmly believed that missionaries must confront the challenges posed by Chinese traditional culture and national awakening, rethinking the localization of Christianity. A "new Christianity" must be non-aggressive while embodying humanized values and seeking harmony between Eastern religions and cultures. Missionaries should serve as "messengers from the Orient," bringing the "Orient" to the West.

On February 8, 1957, Hú Měi (胡美) passed away in New Haven, Connecticut, at the age of 81. After his death, his wife, Charlotta Lotta C. Hume, wrote his biography, Drama at the Doctor’s Gate, which was published in 1961.

(2) The Daily Knowledge Society 1901 (Kang Zhijie 2005: 19-24; Zeng Qingbao 2019: 1-12)

In 1901, Pastor Huang Jiting (黃吉亭) from the Anglican Church established the "Daily Knowledge Society" (日知會) reading room at the Church of the Holy Savior on Fuzhi Street. It served as a venue to disseminate revolutionary literature and ideas to the military and academic circles. "Daily Knowledge" implies "to seek knowledge daily for continuous improvement." In 1902, Huang Jiting was tasked by Bishop J. A. Ingle (殷德生) to rent a space in Changsha to establish a mission, bringing the work of the Daily Knowledge Society to Changsha, where it set up in the newly built Trinity Church to promote knowledge through books and periodicals.

After Huang Jiting was transferred to Changsha, the work of the Wuchang Daily Knowledge Society was overseen by Hu Lanting (胡蘭亭). Hu relocated the society to St. Joseph's Church in Gaojiacai, further expanding its scale, acquiring more books and periodicals, and holding weekly public lectures that criticized politics. The Daily Knowledge Society gradually became the center for spreading Western learning and current affairs in Wuchang. Many patriotic revolutionaries, such as Liu Jing'an (劉靜庵) and Yin Ziheng (殷子衡), frequently visited to promote anti-Manchu ideas. In 1904, due to activities against the Qing, Liu Jing'an sought refuge at the Anglican Church in Gaojiacai. In 1905, Liu attempted to use the Daily Knowledge Society to reorganize revolutionary groups with Hu Lanting's support, leading to a restructuring between April and May, with Hu Lanting and Liu Jing'an serving as president and vice president, respectively, gathering like-minded individuals from the military and academia, with membership exceeding one hundred.

In 1906, at a conference of the Daily Knowledge Society's leaders, Liu Jing'an asserted that the society had the responsibility of "enlightening the people's intellect to save China from crisis and establishing a new China, so that descendants of the Yellow Emperor are no longer slaves to a fallen nation." To promote revolutionary ideas, the Daily Knowledge Society organized various activities, including lectures and reading groups, and disguised their political propaganda as preaching in the military or spreading anti-Qing sentiments to students in classrooms. Furthermore, they published various revolutionary pamphlets, such as "The Bell of Vigilance," "A Sudden Turn," and "The Revolutionary Army." At that time, the Tongmenghui's (同盟會) newspaper "Minbao" also used the Daily Knowledge Society to smuggle materials into Hubei. It can be said that the Daily Knowledge Society transformed from an institution promoting religion by the Anglican Church into a hub for revolutionary activities in Hubei and Hunan. The assistance of Chinese Anglican pastors like Hu Lanting and Huang Jiting, as well as the tacit approval of Bishop Logan H. Roots (吳施德) of the Hubei and Hunan Diocese, played a significant role.

(3) The Church of All Saints and the Martyrs' Pavilion 1902 (Chen Xinyu 2020: 50-62, 119-123)

During the Boxer Rebellion, the Catholic cemetery in Beijing was severely damaged. This cemetery was the burial place for many missionaries, including Matteo Ricci (利瑪竇), Johann Adam Schall (湯約望), Nicolo Longobardo (龍華民), Johann Schreck (鄧玉函), Ferdinand Verbiest (南懷仁), and Giuseppe Castiglione (朗世寧). After the turmoil, the cemetery was included in the list of major sites damaged within the Catholic Church, and under the Boxer Protocol, the foreign graveyard was donated, requiring funds for restoration.

To commemorate the 6,000 martyrs of the Catholic Diocese of Beijing during the Boxer Rebellion, a church called "The Church of All Saints" (諸聖堂) or "Martyrs' Church" was constructed within the cemetery grounds. It was basically completed in 1902 and finished in 1903. On the walls of the church, the tombstones of 77 missionaries, which had been damaged and scattered during the Boxer tumult, were neatly arranged and embedded into the church walls. Concurrently, the names of over 6,000 martyrs were inscribed on the exterior walls of the church. Moreover, some remains of the martyrs were buried at the northern end of the cemetery, where a domed hexagonal pavilion, known as the "Martyrs' Relic Pavilion" (致命亭), with an altar, was built on top.

At the same time, a monument called "The Stone of Purification" was erected: "This is the burial place of the Catholic missionaries of all generations, granted by imperial decree. In the twenty-sixth year of Guangxu, the Boxer rebels initiated chaos, burning churches, looting graves, cutting down trees, and trampling stones into the ground. After negotiations for peace, the Chinese court allocated 100,000 taels of silver to redress the humiliation of the deceased missionaries, specifically ordered for restoration, to be a lasting example. Established in the autumn of the twenty-ninth year of Guangxu."

After the founding of the People’s Republic of China (1954), the Beijing Party School committee occupied the cemetery. In 1958, the church and cemetery were donated to the Party School without compensation. During the Cultural Revolution in 1966, the cemetery was heavily vandalized by the Red Guards, and later the Party School was disbanded. In 1973, the Church of All Saints was demolished. Some of the missionaries' tombstones were buried underground or scattered in various locations.

(4) General Committee of the Youth Association Publishing Department 1902 (Zhao Xiaoyang 2005: 126-135)

After the establishment of the China Christian Children's Association (the predecessor of the National Association of the Young Men’s Christian Association) in 1896, it initially published "The Christian Association Journal" and "The School Monthly". In 1902, the "General Committee of the Youth Association Publishing Department" was established, specifically responsible for text promotion work. In 1902, "The School Monthly" was renamed "The Youth Association Journal", changed to "The Youth" in 1906, and published "Advancement" in 1911. In 1917, it merged into "Youth Advancement," which ceased publication in 1932 after the "January 28 Incident."

In 1912, the "General Committee of the Youth Association Publishing Department" was renamed "National Association of the Young Men’s Christian Association Publishing Department," and the purpose of publication and distribution was defined as: "To serve the aims of the YMCA to assist the youth," "to elucidate the principles of the new culture," "to promote the advancement of the nation and society," "to record the activities of various associations for mutual reference."

By 1924, the Youth Association Publishing Department had developed into the "Youth Association Bookstore" with the aim of "guiding and inspiring the conscience and abilities of students and youth," focusing on cultural, social, and political issues, "to assist those involved in the modernization movement in China." In the 1920s, particularly to support and develop the nationwide popular education movement and civic education movement advocated by the YMCA, it published and sold a large number of series on popular education and civic education, establishing the status of the Youth Association Bookstore in society.

In the 1930s, in light of the declining state of "the future of the Christian movement," following the non-Christian movement, the Youth Association Bookstore aimed to "transcend past steps" and focus more on issues like Christianity and China's transformation, publishing more than 100 series of works, including 50 volumes in the "Youth Series" categorized into life, society, religion, and self-cultivation; 20 volumes in the "Social Issues Series"; 20 volumes in the "Religious Issues Series"; and 20 volumes in the "Christianity and China's Transformation Series."

Among the various departments of the Youth Association, the Publishing Department was the first to be led by Chinese individuals. The three leaders were Xie Honglai (謝洪賴, 1903-1916), Hu Yigu (胡貽穀, 1917-1932), and Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗, 1932-?).

(5) Imperial University of Shansi 1902 (Zhang Deming 2018)

The Imperial University of Shansi, established in 1902, was not a church university, but it had a close relationship with the history of Christianity in China. Initially, Shanxi Province was the area where the most missionaries and converts were killed during the Boxer Rebellion. Subsequently, Li Hongzhang, the plenipotentiary representative for peace negotiations, invited Timothy Richard (李提摩太), a British Baptist missionary who had previously served in Shanxi, to Beijing to assist in resolving compensation issues for the missionaries killed in Shanxi. The newly appointed governor of Shanxi, Cen Chunxuan (岑春煊), also hoped for Timothy's assistance in negotiating solutions. In May 1901, Timothy Richard arrived in Beijing from Shanghai and presented a proposed aftercare plan for Shanxi’s religious missions (including compensation, comforting the dead and injured missionaries and congregants, restraining the behavior of officials and the public, punishing bandit leaders, and erecting memorials) to Li Hongzhang. Regarding compensation, he suggested that the fines "should not be returned to the Westerners or the congregants but should specifically be used to enlighten the people of Shanxi, establish schools, and teach useful knowledge so that gentry and commoners may learn without being deceived." This method of "repaying harm with virtue" was accepted, and Shanxi allocated 500,000 taels from the compensation for the funding of Western learning, managed by Timothy Richard for ten years.

In June 1902, the Imperial University of Shansi officially opened, with Timothy Richard serving as the superintendent of the Western Learning College, and British missionary Moir B. Duncan as the chief instructor. In 1907, William E. Soothill, a missionary from the United Methodist Free Churches, was appointed to this position.

Although Timothy Richard hired many missionaries to participate in teaching, he did not use the compensation funds to establish a Christian university. During the negotiations for the establishment of the school, Shanxi officials explicitly forbade the propagation of Christianity at the Western Learning College. In defense of religious freedom, which the Qing government acknowledged in the treaties, Timothy firmly rejected this condition, and ultimately, the clause was removed from the school's operational contract after negotiations. Richard insisted on the principle of "freedom of religious belief," neither forcing students to adopt the faith nor offering theology courses, yet he also did not agree to the local gentry's restrictions on religious activities within the school. He believed that teachers could influence students through the spirit of Christianity, engage in discussions about Christianity in lectures, or invite students to participate in worship, but the decision to believe or not was up to the students, who had absolute freedom to join or reject Christianity.

In November 1910, Timothy Richard handed over the Western Learning College to the Shanxi authorities for self-management.

(6) Shimenkan Church 1905 (Zhang Tan 1992; Cheung 1994: 217-247; Covell 1995: 83-104; T’ien 1992: 22-23, 51-53)

In 1905, Samuel Pollard, a missionary from the Bible Christians Methodist, established a church in Shimenkan, Guizhou, which played a significant role in the evangelical movement among the Dahu苗 (a subgroup of the Miao people) in the border regions of Yunnan and Guizhou in the early 20th century. Shimenkan is located in Weining County, Guizhou, near the Yunnan border, and is a settlement area for the Miao people.

Pollard arrived in Yunnan in 1885, initially focusing on evangelism among the Han population in the provincial capital (Kunming), but with little success, baptizing only two individuals by 1893. At the end of 1903, he crossed the Jinsha River into Liangshan, Sichuan, becoming the first Western missionary in that area. In July 1904, four Miao people actively sought him out in Zhaotong. Earlier, in 1902, John R. Adam from the China Inland Mission began missionary work among the Miao people, establishing a church in the Anshun Miao settlement, which became a center for Miao learning and attracted Miao from Weining. Due to the closer proximity of Weining to Zhaotong than Anshun, Adam suggested they go to Zhaotong to find Pollard. Consequently, the number of Miao coming to Zhaotong increased, totaling about 1,000. The 1904 movement of the Miao seeking truth came to be known as "The Year of the Dragon's Salvation." Pollard thus decided to enter Weining in the winter of 1904, ultimately selecting Shimenkan as his base and establishing a church there. In Shimenkan, he implemented a series of reforms: he developed Miao welcoming rituals, translated the Bible into the Miao language (starting with the Gospel of Mark in 1906, publishing the four Gospels in Miao in 1912, and completing the New Testament translation in 1914), established schools (naming one Guanghua Primary School in 1911), hospitals, and reformed various detrimental social practices among the Miao, such as opium use and alcoholism. His creation of a Miao script known as the Pollard Script also played a significant role in strengthening Miao ethnic identity.

On September 15, 1915, Pollard passed away from salmonella infection and was buried in Shimenkan. At the time of his death, the Miao church had 4,800 baptized members, 900 youth, and 5,000 individuals under observation. Over the course of 11 years, three small churches were established each year, along with more than ten preaching areas. A total of 23 schools were opened, educating over 1,000 students. This not only became an important missionary center for the Methodist Church's Southwest District but also served as a significant model for Miao people converting to Christianity.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

(1) Sino-foreign Protestant Establishment （Bays 1996: 20; Bays 2012: 99-104 ）

Chinese Christian historian Pei Shidan （裴士丹, Daniel H. Bays） proposed the concept of "Sino-foreign Protestant Establishment" in 1996 to describe Christian organizations formed after 1900, such as the Zhonghua Xuhang Weiyuanhui （中華續行委辦會）, the Zhonghua Jidujiao Qingnianhui Quanguo Xiehui （中華基督教青年會全國協會）, and the Zhonghua Quanguo Jidujiao Xiejin Hui （中華全國基督教協進會）. These organizations were dedicated to promoting Sino-foreign cooperation and attracted many Chinese Christian elites, gradually playing an important role in the Christian establishment. Most of these elites were second or third generation believers who received an education in church schools, where Western learning had a more significant impact on them than traditional culture. Many had experience studying abroad and obtained advanced degrees (including theology) from universities in Europe and America. Upon returning to China, they were valued by the missionary societies and became assistants to Western leaders. As Chinese Christianity began to develop diversely in the 20th century, these Westernized Christian elites broke through the boundaries of individual regions or denominations, establishing their leadership image on the national Christian stage. For example, Cheng Jingyi （誠靜怡） served as the Secretary-General of the Zhonghua Quanguo Jidujiao Xiejin Hui, Yu Rizhang （余日章） was the Secretary-General of the Zhonghua Jidujiao Qingnianhui Quanguo Xiehui, Liu Tingfang （劉廷芳） was a professor at Yenching University （燕京大學）, and Zhao Zichen （趙紫宸） became the dean of the School of Religion at Yenching University. They not only thought from the perspective of missionary societies but also devoted themselves to exploring the construction issues of local churches.

(2) Christian Immigrants Cultivating "New Fuzhou" in Sarawak, 1901（Zhu Feng 2009: 81-91; Leung 2002: 337-352）

Between 1900 and 1902, Huang Naishang （黃乃裳） organized more than 1,100 members of the Methodist Episcopal Church from Fuzhou to immigrate in three batches to Sibu （詩巫）, Sarawak in Northwest Borneo to cultivate land, establishing "New Fuzhou," which became an important historical example of collective Christian immigration from China to Nanyang in modern times.

Huang Naishang began his ministry with the Methodist Episcopal Church in Fuzhou in 1869, obtained qualifications as a student in 1877, and achieved success in the provincial examination in 1895. In 1898, while in Beijing for the imperial examination, he participated in Kang Youwei's （康有為） "Public Petition" to call for reform in the Qing dynasty. After the failure of the reform movement led by Kang and Liang, Huang actively supported the efforts. However, due to Empress Dowager Cixi's opposition, the reform was abruptly halted. The Guangxu Emperor was imprisoned, and Kang and Liang went into exile. At this time, Huang hastily left Beijing and returned to Fuzhou. In 1899, he moved south to Singapore. In April 1900, he went to Sarawak for an inspection, where he conceived a plan for immigration and cultivation.

In May 1900, Huang signed a contract with the Sarawak government, which agreed to introduce Chinese immigrants to "plant rice" to alleviate food shortages, naming the cultivated area "New Fuzhou." He established the "New Fuzhou Cultivation Company" and returned to Fuzhou to recruit immigrants. With his background in the Methodist Episcopal Church, he primarily recruited from the church community, and this relationship gave confidence to the plan. At the time, many villagers also hoped to seek a better life through immigration. The first batch of about 500 immigrants (of whom two-thirds were Christians) arrived at the end of 1900. The second and third batches comprised 535 and 551 people, respectively, arriving in May 1901. The three batches mainly came from Gutian, Minqing, and Pingnan counties, with two-thirds being Christians.

This group of Christian immigrants from Fuzhou not only cleared land for cultivation but also established a Chinese Christian community. Although Huang Naishang, as the initiator of the entire immigration movement, left Sarawak in 1904, due to the cultivation efforts of the early Christians, Sarawak became known as "New Fuzhou," and the Methodist Episcopal Church made significant progress in the area.

(3) Independent Church Movement, 1902-1906（Bays 1996: 307-309; Luo Weihong 2014: 320-327; Zhang Hua 1998:）

In the early 20th century, the consciousness of self-governance began to emerge in the Chinese church, leading to the establishment of independent churches advocating for "self-reliance" among Chinese believers in various regions. In 1902, Xie Honglai （謝洪賴, from the Methodist Church） and Gao Fengchi （高鳳池, from the Presbyterian Church） learned about an anti-Catholic incident in Chenzhou, Hunan, and deliberated on founding an independent church to reclaim the rights of evangelism. In November, a total of 13 people, including Song Yaoru （宋曜如）, became the initiators and established the "Chinese Christian Association," with the goals of (1) uniting Chinese believers; (2) encouraging believers' faith; and (3) evangelizing within the country. The articles emphasized self-support, self-propagation, and self-governance as the spirit of the association. In 1903, they published the "Chinese Christian News," disseminating it nationwide to promote the consciousness of self-governance. Subsequently, believers responded in places such as Ganzhou, Chaozhou, Ningbo, Changsha, and Hong Kong, establishing local "Chinese Christian Associations." In 1910, they merged with the "Chinese Jesus Self-governing Church" to form the "Chinese Christian Self-governing Association," but ultimately separated due to dissatisfaction with Yu Guozhen （俞國楨）'s desire to hold a lifelong presidency, restoring their independent identities. In 1915, the Shanghai Chinese Christian Association convened a general assembly and decided to reorganize, merging into the domestic evangelistic work of the Zhonghua Xuhang Weiyuanhui, which also led to the suspension of the "Chinese Christian News."

In 1906, Yu Guozhen, originally from the Presbyterian Church and who joined the Chinese Christian Association in 1905, founded the "Chinese Jesus Self-governing Church." In the founding declaration, he expressed concern over "the severity of church incidents and the urgency of external threats" and invited "believers with thoughts of patriotism, love for the church, and love for others" to establish the church, aiming to "eliminate incidents and preserve the church's reputation," emphasizing "no Western involvement" in the process. Following this, Yu also submitted a petition to the military governor of Suzhou, requesting "gracious approval for establishment," which was permitted and protected by the Qing government. During this period, there were also responses to the Jesus Self-governing Church in various places. In 1910, Yu established the "General Association of the Chinese Jesus Self-governing Church," which published the "Holy News" as its official publication the following year. At this time, the Jesus Self-governing Church adjusted its relationship with foreign missionary societies, no longer emphasizing the exclusion of Westerners. In 1916, the association's constitution added "Remembering the source and not forgetting the root." It joined the Zhonghua Quanguo Jidujiao Xiejin Hui in 1922. By 1935, there were over 350 churches under the national general association, with 20,000 believers. However, the relationship between the general association and its subordinate churches was mostly a loose voluntary union, with many places establishing churches where only a dozen believers would gather under the banner of self-governance.

(4) Response of the Chinese School Association (Chinese Education Association) to the New Educational System of the Qing Government, 1902-1905（Shen Xinmin 2023: 93-102）

In May 1902, the Educational Association of China （中國學塾會）, originally named the Yizhi Book Society, held its fourth three-year convention. Vice President J. C. Ferguson （福開森） pointed out that the Qing government's new education system would limit missionaries' influence on official-sponsored schools and the requirement for schools to hold Confucian ceremonies presented significant challenges for missionaries and Christian students. The meeting decided to express their opinions to the Qing government, and a committee named the "Committee to Prepare Memorial to Chinese Government" was organized by Lin Lezhi （林樂知）, Li Timo （李提摩太）, and seven others to articulate concerns about how the new educational system would affect Christians and church schools. At the same time, eight missionaries were appointed to draft "An Appeal to Foreign Boards for Trained Educators for China," urging foreign mission boards to send more professional teachers to China. The former committee could not change the Qing government's policies and was dissolved in 1905. The latter appeal was completed in November, and its main contents included: (1) mission boards should change their policy of only sending missionaries to "preach the gospel"; (2) in response to the emphasis on education in Chinese society, more professionals were needed; (3) the aim to influence the future official education system in China; (4) the Chinese government’s intolerance towards youths receiving Christianity; (5) mission boards should send trained educators to China to develop educational work.

In May 1905, the Educational Association of China changed its name to the Chinese Education Association and held its fourth three-year convention in Shanghai. After engaging with the Qing government in 1902, Ferguson summarized the new situation and the development of church schools: (1) the Chinese government accepted assistance from individual missionaries rather than suggestions from missionary organizations; (2) the existence of church schools was not to train government officials for the government; (3) in light of the government's implementation of primary education, the development of church schools should focus on secondary and higher education.

In 1905, the Qing government abolished the imperial examination system and established the Ministry of Education. The Chinese Education Association submitted a memorandum to the Ministry, expressing the willingness of church schools to accept government supervision, allowing graduates of church schools to have equal status with graduates of government schools and opportunities to participate in various examinations and public offices. It was decided that missionaries from Beijing and Tianjin would form a follow-up committee to negotiate with the ministry. If no results were achieved, the American minister to China would intervene. However, negotiations between the ministry and the Chinese Education Association did not yield any results.

(5) China Centenary Missionary Conference, 1907（Luo Weihong 2014: 306-316; Chen Zhiheng 2013: 43-44）

During the second missionary conference held in China in 1890, it was decided to hold the third convention in 1900. However, due to the World Missionary Conference held in New York in 1900, it was postponed until 1901, only to be delayed again due to the Boxer Rebellion in China. The conference was ultimately held from April 25 to May 7, 1907, commemorating the 100th anniversary of Morrison's （馬禮遜） arrival in China, and was referred to as the "Centenary Missionary Conference." A total of 1,170 people attended (with 500 missionary representatives), and the venue was located at the Municipal Hall in the Shanghai International Settlement, the Martyrs’ Memorial Hall, Holy Trinity Church, and Tianan Church, among other places. Notably, about ten Chinese attended the conference as visitors, including Chen Weiping （陳維屏） and Dora Yu （余慈度） from the Methodist Episcopal Church in Beijing.

The conference drafted 12 topics, each with missionary reports and discussions: (1) The Chinese Church; (2) The Chinese Ministry; (3) Education; (4) Evangelistic Work; (5) General Women's Work; (6) Women's Educational Work; (7) Christian Literature; (8) Ancestral Worship; (9) Medical Missions; (10) Holy Scriptures; (11) Comity and Federation; (12) The Missionary & Public Questions. In addition, the conference submitted a document to the Qing government, expressing the spirit and charitable objectives of Christian missionary work, and urged the government to grant complete religious liberty to Chinese Christians. Furthermore, an open letter was sent to Chinese churches and mother churches, specifically stating missionaries' positions regarding the opium trade.

A significant resolution from this meeting was the recommendation to establish the "Christian Federation of China," aimed at promoting church unity. The concrete method was to establish federations in various provinces or multiple provinces, with these federations holding provincial meetings every year or two. Each federation would appoint one Chinese and one Western officer. The provincial federations would then send representatives to form a national representative council, which would hold a national meeting every 3 to 5 years. The national council would have one Chinese and one Western officer. To follow up on this resolution, the conference appointed a 25-member committee responsible for assisting the provinces in organizing federations and serving as consultants. Subsequently, federations were established in provinces like Zhili, the Three Eastern Provinces, Shandong, Henan, Sichuan, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Guangdong. However, after the establishment of the China Continuation Committee in 1913, the plan for the Christian Federation ultimately did not materialize, leading to the formation of the National Christian Council of China in 1922.

(6) Guangdong Jesus Church Centenary Celebration, 1907（Zhao Bifang 2019: 146-150）

Following the centenary missionary conference in 1907, Chinese Christians initiated plans for a celebration focused on the contributions of Chinese individuals to commemorate Morrison's arrival in China. On July 27, the Guangdong Protestant Union decided to organize the Chinese Centenary Missionary Conference, electing Liao Zhuoan （廖卓庵）, an official from the Self-Governing Xinghua Church (originally a Baptist pastor who founded the Self-Governing Xinghua Church in 1903), as the general director. Subsequently, various departments were established (planning, arrangements, speeches, music, documentation, reception, and finance). The conference was scheduled to occur from September 5, 1907 (the day before Morrison's arrival) for four consecutive days. The first three days were designated for commemorative activities, while the final day served as the autumn gathering of the Guangdong Christian Union. The venue was a large tent that could accommodate 2,800 people, erected on official land. More than 40,000 attendees were expected.

On the speaking agenda for the first day were topics such as "The History of Morrison" (T. W. Pearce), "The Future Hope of the Church in China a Century Later" (Yang Xiangfu), and "The Peaceful Coexistence of Religious and Civil Authorities in China" (Robert E. Chambers). Other speeches included "Advisory Against Opium" (Xie Enlu), "Revitalizing Industries" (Huang Dengyun), "Morality Reform" (Robert E. Chambers), and "Family Education" (Wu Zunsan).

Besides Guangdong, various Chinese churches in southern regions (Foshan, Hong Kong, Xinning, Xiangshan) also held centenary celebrations.

(7) Revival in Manchuria, 1908（Zhao Ruijuan 2017: 99-100; Ma Kezheng and Xin Yifu 1984: 16）

In the 1908 edition of the Chinese Recorder （《教務雜誌》）, several articles mentioned the "Revival in Manchuria." Jonathan Goforth （古約翰）, a missionary from the Canadian Presbyterian Church, was a central figure in this "great revival." He came to China in 1888, mainly working in northern Henan. In the early 20th century, he began to pay attention to the Welsh Revival in Britain and read works by Charles G. Finney, the father of the American revival movement, expressing a desire for a similar spiritual revival in China.

In 1907, the Korean church experienced a significant revival, which attracted Goforth to the area for a three-week investigation. On his way back to Henan from Korea, he passed through Manchuria and shared his experiences at several missionary stations. Upon returning to Henan, he received invitations from the Scottish Presbyterian Church and the Irish Presbyterian Church in Northeast China, requesting him to lead revival meetings for ten days. In February 1908, Goforth departed, first leading meetings in Shenyang, where attendance reached 800 to 900 people daily. Starting from the second day, many repented for their sins, and tears flowed in prayer. Elders from the church acknowledged serious sins, filling the meetings with an atmosphere of crying and confession. In March, Goforth led a week-long revival in Liaoning, spurring local church revival where many confessed and sought God’s forgiveness. Not only the believers but even non-believers were moved to confess. The revival fire spread from Shenyang and Liaoning to various places in Manchuria, including Kuancheng, Haicheng, Niuzhuang, Ashihe, Guangning, Jinzhou, and Xinmin. After the revival, believers became zealous in evangelism, and non-believers joined in the ranks of those who confessed and repented, including former leaders of the Boxer movement who had killed Christians.

Having personally experienced the great revival in Manchuria, Goforth gained a deeper understanding of the principle that "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" and later decided to leave Henan to lead revival meetings throughout China. Wherever he went, especially in North China (in Taixi, Shanxi; Chengde, Hebei; and Yantai, Shandong), he also brought revival.

(8) Christianity and the Late Qing Revolutionary Movement（Shen Xinmin 2023: 122-135; Tao Feiya 2024: 133-135; Liang Shouhua 2007; Dunch 2001）

Reform and revolution were significant themes in China’s development during the 19th century. As the late Qing dynasty began a new decade, while the Qing court implemented new policies after the Boxer Rebellion, it still could not halt the rising revolutionary sentiments and actions. Aside from a few missionaries sympathetic to the revolution, most missionaries were clearly reluctant to involve themselves in this issue. The centenary missionary conference in 1907 passed a resolution encouraging the Qing government’s reform efforts and offered prayers for its success. Conversely, the resolution reiterated that missionaries held a moral and spiritual mission, with no political objectives, and reminded all missionaries to be wary of the church being exploited by any revolutionary means when Chinese nationalism awakened, so as not to provoke disloyal acts against the government due to the ignorance, confusion, or misguidance of Chinese believers. However, as the situation developed, missionaries inevitably began discussing the revolutionary circumstances in China, transitioning from initially labeling it as "rebellion" to positively recognizing the significance of "revolution" for China.

In contrast, during the late Qing revolutionary movement, many Christians could be seen at the forefront, such as Sun Yat-sen （孫中山）, Lu Haodong （陸皓東）, Qu Fengzhi （區鳳墔）, Yin Wenkai （尹文楷）, Wang Chonghui （王寵惠）, Zheng Shiliang （鄭士良）, Yang Xiangfu （楊襄甫）, Chen Shaobai （陳少白）, Guan Jingliang （關景良）, Zuo Doushan （左斗山）, and Shi Jianru （史堅如）, who were all involved in the revolution in Guangzhou and Hong Kong. Beyond South China, in the Hubei and Hunan regions, particularly in Wuhan, Christians like Huang Jiting （黃吉亭） and Liu Jing'an （劉靜庵） were also actively engaged in the revolution. Statistics indicate that during the Huizhou uprising in 1900, Christians constituted 30% of the participants. Among the 72 martyrs of the Huanghuagang incident in 1911, 24 were Christians. Undoubtedly, through their contact with Christianity, Chinese Christians' pursuit of Western culture and their understanding of the nation's future not only propelled them to engage in social and political changes but also led some to embrace the path of revolution. However, for the vast majority of Christians, their participation in the revolution was under their identity as Christians, and they were not necessarily "Christian revolutionaries." What they sought to achieve aligned more with a democratic republic embodying Christian values rather than a "Christian Republic."

Key Theological Issues

(1) The Deep and Shallow Version of the New Testament Combined 1907 (You Sida (尤思德) 2002: 232-235, 285-2296; You Sida (尤思德) 2003: 72-77, 79-80)

At the second missionary conference in China in 1890, it was decided to establish three translation committees: Deep Version, Shallow Version, and Mandarin. At the centennial mission conference in 1907, the three committees submitted reports and discussed whether to merge the Deep and Shallow Version committees. Gibson John (汲約翰) was the chair of the Shallow Version committee, and he supported the merger proposal, noting that the Chinese language had undergone changes in recent years and he foresaw the Shallow Version becoming the style of the present era. Some committee members argued that the Deep Version, being closer to classical Chinese, was inevitably influenced by the vocabulary commonly used in Confucianism, thus distorting the truth of the Bible.

D. Z. Sheffield (謝衛樓) was the chair of the Deep Version committee, and he also supported the merger proposal due to the lack of communication between the two committees. However, he offered harsh criticism of the Shallow Version, arguing that its attempt to emphasize the original text's meaning strayed beyond what could be tolerated in Chinese. For instance, in the use of function words, the Shallow Version frequently employed "且" to translate the Greek conjunction "καί," even though it was unnecessary according to the conventions of classical Chinese.

At the 1907 conference, Gibson John responded, stating the necessity of providing a translation based on the New Testament Greek, acknowledging that this would include many elements that are not Chinese (i.e., classical Chinese). He pointed out, “As Christian thought permeates the mindset of Chinese people, it will also permeate and refine their language.” Similarly, the Shallow Version opposed Deep Version translators “indulging in the elegance of citing classical texts or using terms from ancient literature,” arguing instead for the use of terms commonly found in ancient texts and the simplest classical idioms to express biblical thoughts, ensuring that even readers with limited knowledge of ancient texts could understand it, creating a version that is immediately comprehensible.

It is evident that although the chairs of both the Deep and Shallow Version committees agreed on the merger, significant differences remained regarding the style and translation principles following the merger. Since both versions of the New Testament had already been completed (the Shallow Version in 1900 and published in 1904; the Deep Version published in 1906), with the Old Testament yet to begin, the conference decided to merge the New Testament of the Deep and Shallow Versions and to only translate one version of the Old Testament. However, the new version of the New Testament would not be immediately merged, but would consolidate three years later. Due to the substantial differences in translation principles between the two versions, it was fundamentally difficult to integrate them.

The new committee for the merged version was led by D. Z. Sheffield, mostly comprised of the original Deep Version translators, who continued the translation of the Old Testament. Ultimately, the Old Testament of the merged version was completed in 1915 and published in 1919. At that time, the primary debate centered on the issues between the literary-form and the Mandarin translations.

(2) Discussion on the Issue of Ancestor Worship at the Centennial Mission Conference 1907 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1997: 31-40)

In 1907, the missionaries in China convened the centennial mission conference. Given the importance and controversy surrounding the issue of ancestor worship, the topic was submitted for further discussion at the conference, with a dedicated committee presenting a research report hoping to “strive to find solutions to the problem, remove all obstacles, while ensuring the purity of the church.” The report was presented by committee chair James Jackson (翟雅各, 美以美会). In the lengthy 30-page report, Jackson adopted a relatively open attitude toward ancestor worship, even admitting that missionaries had made past mistakes by neglecting the differences expressed in Chinese classics and the practices of ordinary people related to ancestor worship. Furthermore, he pointed out that even if ancestor worship is a flawed system, there are still certain truths contained within it. Missionaries should compassionately seek these truths to avoid uprooting the weeds at the expense of the wheat.

Subsequently, in his report, James Jackson (翟雅各) conducted a detailed analysis of ancestor worship. He believed that missionaries had misunderstood the term "worship," as the Chinese word "拜" is highly flexible, not limited to religious contexts but also applicable to showing respect to individuals. Regarding the issue of "prostration," this is a customary and respectful practice among Eastern people. Therefore, missionaries should abandon the biases and concepts held by Westerners. Moreover, the Chinese word "祭" differs from the English term "sacrifice." Jackson also fully acknowledged the role of ancestor worship in Chinese culture, admitting that its essence includes filial piety, and the superstitions exhibited in today's populace do not diminish this fact. Additionally, another strong motivation for ancestor worship is the maintenance of family integrity.

Clearly, Jackson deliberately avoided the sensitive question of whether ancestor worship is idolatry. He mentioned twice that regardless of whether ancestor worship constitutes idolatry, it undoubtedly opposes the worship of the one true God, taking a place in people's hearts and lives that should be occupied entirely by God. His ultimate objection to ancestor worship rests on the notion that it replaces the worship of God in the minds of Chinese people, rather than standing entirely against idolatry.

Under the premise of both affirmation and opposition, he specifically proposed five "constructive rather than destructive" suggestions as a reference for the Chinese church facing practical issues: (1) widely utilize memorial days to express believers' remembrance of deceased loved ones, incorporating elements such as hymns, Bible reading, and prayer; (2) strengthen the teaching of respect for parents in church schools; (3) convert funeral costs into charitable contributions as a memorial for the deceased; (4) how to handle ancestor tablets; (5) establish suitable funeral rituals for the Chinese church. The fourth point reflects a breakthrough by missionaries in establishing constructive proposals, as Jackson did not believe it was justifiable to require Chinese people to abandon or discard their ancestral tablets, but rather advocated for encouraging the elimination of superstitious beliefs associated with the tablets and suggested considering the replacement of ancestor tablets with similar commemorative tablets used in Christianity.

Jackson's report aimed to modify the previous absolute denial of ancestor worship policies by the Chinese church without making compromises or concessions. As he explained during the proposal, this was based on a position of "reconciliation" and "harmony" (an irenical & mediating position), acknowledging under the light of God's truth that ancestor worship can express the true nature of humanity; at the same time, he condemned certain characteristics that distort this nature and pointed out how this nature could be truly and fully reflected in Christianity. It can be said that Jackson's attitude remained opposed to ancestor worship among Chinese believers, but he argued that missionaries should not adopt a high-handed approach of resistance and prohibition, but rather patiently conduct ongoing "conscience education" for Chinese believers.

However, even such a stance raised concerns among some attending missionaries, who feared that the report would promote tolerance and compromise regarding ancestor worship. Some missionaries expressed strong dissatisfaction with the many affirmations of the spirit of ancestor worship in the report. For example, H. C. DuBose (杜步西, Southern Presbyterian Church) stated, "The report was presented in what he considers the most dangerous manner," reiterating that "ancestor worship is worship of human beings," and solely on this point, it violated the first two commandments. Clark (克拉克) from the China Inland Mission explicitly described ancestor worship as "paganism."

Of course, there were also many missionaries who supported Jackson's constructive stance. For instance, J. Wherry (惠志德, Northern Presbyterian Church) supported Jackson's appeal for the conference not to enact any mandatory legislation to define what Chinese believers should or should not do. Instead, missionaries should adopt a compassionate attitude and provide "conscience education" to new converts. J. C. Gibson (汲約翰) emphasized that addressing ancestor worship with a sympathetic approach is justifiable. If missionaries clearly oppose ancestor worship, it will only lead to greater resentment among Chinese people towards Christianity. However, regarding the issue of "conscience education," some missionaries expressed concerns about whether failing to explicitly require converts to comply with the prohibition on ancestor worship implies that believers might be permitted to continue practicing it before gradually being educated. Does this not violate the bottom line that Christianity is incompatible with ancestor worship?

Overall, from the reports and discussions on ancestor worship at the centennial mission conference, it is clear that Western missionaries did not alter their consistent opposition to ancestor worship. They firmly believe that ancestor worship and the noble spirit of Christianity remain incompatible. Their most significant change was in recognizing the sentiments of honoring and remembering the deceased; however, they reaffirmed that such activities cannot be tolerated within the Christian church. While being willing to consider the traditional Chinese respect for filial piety, they established some "constructive" alternatives, hoping to demonstrate through action that Christianity does not oppose filial piety but instead embodies it more beautifully than ancestor worship. Nonetheless, Jackson's suggestions for transforming ancestor tablets, touched upon in the report, did not receive further clarification in the proposal; instead, he reiterated the encouragement of Chinese believers to express their remembrance of the deceased through various means, reflecting the lack of consensus even within the committee. Interestingly, some missionaries expressed unease about the proposal to erect commemorative monuments, suggesting the addition of "useful" figures in front of the monuments. What constitutes a "useful" monument? This clearly relates to concerns among some missionaries about the possibility that Chinese people might ascribe superstitious meanings to the monument, believing it to be a dwelling place for the souls of their ancestors.

(3) Protestant Mission Consensus, 1907 (Yao Xiyi (姚西伊) 1997: 186-189)

According to Chinese Christian historian Yao Xiyi (姚西伊), the 1907 centennial mission conference reflected the "Protestant Mission Consensus." For example, in a letter to the home church, the propagation of the gospel remained the core mission of missions, but the responsibilities of education, healthcare, and charitable work were also acknowledged. However, the positioning of these works in the letter still centered around the purpose of evangelism. Therefore, it maintained a subtle balance between soul salvation and social transformation. The resolution regarding "the Church in China" affirmed the Bible (the Old and New Testaments) as the highest standard for faith and practice, recognized that the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed adequately express the fundamental doctrines of Christianity, while not adopting any creed as a basis for church unity. Regarding the attitude towards Chinese culture, the conference passed a letter to the Qing government, which acknowledged the consistency between the Christian faith and Chinese culture, while also pointing out the distinctions between the two, particularly in relation to the divine-human relationship and human responsibilities towards God. It is evident that missionaries recognize the correctness of non-Christian elements, but still hold Christianity as the highest revelation of God. However, Yao also noted that there were considerable debates between conservative and open-minded missionaries on individual issues such as ancestor worship and social work during the conference. Subsequently, the theological disputes led to the dissolution of the aforementioned missionary consensus.

Anti-Christian Leaders

(1) Zhao Duoshan（Zhang Shouchang 張守常1997：3-13；Chen Fangzhong 陳方中2008）

Zhao Sanduo (趙三多, 1841-1902) was a martial arts master from Shandong's Xiangwei County, known for his expertise in Plum Blossom Fist. In 1898, the Li Yuantun Incident occurred in Shandong due to the arrival of Catholicism in Li Yuantun, leading to the formation of a new social group consisting of clergy and parishioners that challenged the existing "community elites." The source of local disputes was the communal property of the Jade Emperor Temple, which had to be divided due to the presence of church members. Local officials sided with the church, and the local populace could not accept the parishioners, leading to deeply rooted anti-Christian sentiments. At that time, a group known as the "Eighteen Masters," led by Yan Shuqin, initiated anti-Christian incidents. The authorities dispatched troops to quell the unrest, and Yan and others sought assistance from Zhao Sanduo, who was respected as a master. Consequently, Zhao participated in the anti-Christian struggle and became a leading figure. The name "Yihequan" (義和團), meaning "Righteous and Harmonious Fists," was actually coined by Zhao Sanduo. In 1899, Zhao gathered with masters from other counties at the Zhengding Dabeisi and united various anti-Christian factions. During the meeting, Zhao proposed the slogan "God-Assisted Yihe Fist," which later evolved into the widespread "God-Assisted Fist" manifest during the Yihequan movement:

God-Assisted Fist, Yihequan, only because the devils disturb the Central Plain. Advise the faithful to rely on God, not to believe in divinity, and to forget their ancestors. Men engage in debauchery, women commit adultery; all children of devils are born from both. If you do not believe, observe closely; the devils' eyes are all blue. There is no rain from heaven, and the earth is parched; all because the churches block the skies. The gods are angry, the immortals are resentful, and together they descend to spread the doctrine. It is neither evil nor White Lotus; recite incantations, use true mantras, elevate yellow tablets, and burn incense to invite all gods from every cave. The immortals emerge from their caves, the gods descend from the mountains, attaching themselves to humans to transmit the fist. Military arts, all mastered; to defeat the devils is no difficult task. Dismantle railroads, pull down telegraph poles, urgently destroy railroad cars. The great powers make our hearts tremble; Britain, America, Germany, and Russia will all vanish. Foreign devils, utterly exterminated; the Great Qing will unify and pacify the mountains.

(2) Liu Shirui（Di Deman 狄德滿2011：259-308；Chen Fangzhong 陳方中2008）

Liu Shirui (劉士瑞, ?-1896), was a leader of the Dadaohui (大刀會, Large Saber Society), who passed the imperial examination in his youth and later took up "Golden Bell Cover," establishing the Dadaohui with the aim of "protecting one's family." In the 1890s, he became involved in anti-Christian activities in southwestern Shandong. In 1896, Dadaohui disciple Pang Sanjie, after facing unfavorable competition for newly cultivated land in the river with Catholic parishioners, led his followers to attack Catholics at the border of Feng County in Shandong and Dangshan County in Jiangsu, which subsequently spread into the territory of Caozhou and resulted in multiple incidents of violence against the church. At that time, local officials treated the Dadaohui as bandits and suppressed them, forcing the society underground. In 1896, over 30 Dadaohui leaders, including Liu Shidu, were beheaded by the authorities. After Liu's death, the Dadaohui continued to operate in various places.

In the early hours of November 1, 1897, two missionaries, Xu Nengfang (能方濟) and Han Li (韓理), were murdered at the residence of another missionary, Xue Tianzi (薛田資), an incident known as the "Juye Incident.” The Society of the Divine Word claimed that the assailants were Dadaohui members, but Di Deman's research indicates that this was an exaggerated accusation made by the Society to gain more benefits.

(3) Zhu Hongdeng（Chen Fangzhong 陳方中1998）

Zhu Hongdeng (朱紅燈, ?-1899), also known as Zhu Xiaozai (朱小子), was a leader of the Shenshuai (神拳, God Fist). In 1899, "God Fist" emerged in Pingyuan County, Shandong; it can be seen as a simplified version of the Dadaohui (大刀會, Large Saber Society). This required years of practice, while the Dadaohui's complex rituals were supplanted by shamanistic spirit possession. After this spirit possession, those claiming the ability to wield knives and guns formed groups that demonstrated martial arts. From the latter half of 1899 to the spring of 1900, such groups appeared widely in the rural areas of Shandong and Hebei, with each group containing anywhere from dozens to a couple of hundred members. The uprising led by Shenshuai was known as the "Pingyuan Incident," which coincided with a series of violent anti-Christian events in neighboring provinces, such as Jingzhou, Gucheng, and Wuyi in Zhili. The Zhu Family River area was a major base for the church members and became the location with the highest number of Christian casualties during the height of the Yihequan movement in June and July of 1900. On December 24, 1899, Zhu was captured and executed by the Shandong governor, Yu Xian (毓賢).

Anti-Christian Organizations

(1) Yihe Tuan (義和團) (Esherick 1987; Tiedemann 2011)

“Yihe Tuan” (義和團) refers to the armed anti-foreign and anti-Christian organizations in northern China in 1900. The Yihe Tuan in Shandong primarily included local militia groups such as Yihequan (義和拳), Dadahui (大刀會), and Shénquán (神拳), with the purpose of defending their communities and maintaining order, while also incorporating elements of folk religious beliefs that emphasized spirit possession and invulnerability to weapons. Western scholar Joseph W. Esherick (周錫瑞) and German scholar R. G. Tiedemann (狄德滿) attempt to explain why the Yihe Tuan emerged in late 19th century Shandong in this particular form. They focus on the social, economic, and political conditions of the region where the Yihe Tuan rose, interpreting the movement's causes through these interactions. Furthermore, the rise of these martial societies towards anti-foreign and anti-Christian sentiments is closely related to the influence of foreign missionary forces (especially after Germany's seizure of Kiautschou Bay) and the dire socio-economic background of the time: on one hand, continuous natural disasters and the devastation of rural economies, combined with disbanding soldiers leading to an influx of vagrants into the martial societies; on the other hand, the prolonged development of Catholicism leading to conflicts with popular beliefs, and the government's mishandling of religious cases exacerbated anti-Christian sentiments among the populace. The slogans “Support Qing and Eliminate Foreign Influence” (扶清滅洋) and “Revive Qing and Eliminate Foreign Influence” (興清滅洋) not only reflect the intense anti-foreign and anti-Christian emotions of the martial artists but also garnered support from certain conservative factions within the Qing government, further amplifying the issues caused by the Yihe Tuan movement.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

(1) Gengzi Jiao Nan (庚子教難) 1900 (Huang Shengpei (黃鍚培) 2010; Tao Feiya (陶飛亞) 2024: 129-130; Xiong Huang Huiling (熊黃惠玲) 2008; Zhang Yufa (張玉法) 1987: 223-237)

The Boxer Rebellion erupted in 1900, but there were 89 anti-Christian incidents throughout the province between 1895 and 1900, affecting 96 counties. Particularly from 1897 to 1899, there were several serious church incidents, such as the Juye Church Incident in 1897, the Yizhou Church Incident in 1898, and the Liyuantun Incident in Guanquan County in 1898. Anti-foreign and anti-Christian sentiments surged, and the conflict between the people and the church reached a critical point. The laissez-faire policies of various governors of Shandong (Li Bingheng (李秉衡), Zhang Paimai (張派梅), and Yu Xian (毓賢)) in just a few years allowed for increasing expansion, with their activities spreading from Shandong and Zhili to Beijing.

At this time, Empress Dowager Cixi (慈禧太后) was very displeased with Western sympathies towards reform since the Hundred Days' Reform and the support of Kang Youwei (康有為) and Liang Qichao (梁啟超). Furthermore, ministers such as Ronglu (榮祿), Yikuang (奕劻), and Gangyi (剛毅) were oblivious to the situation. Later, Yu Xian praised the Boxers for their loyalty and bravery in Beijing, which led Cixi to see them as a useful grassroots force to instill fear in foreigners. In late April 1900, the Boxers entered Beijing; in mid-May, they publicly posted proclamations, sabotaged railways and telegraph lines, and injured foreigners, prompting various foreign ministers to mobilize troops into Beijing and the Tianjin concession. In June, Boxer members set up altars everywhere, hanging banners proclaiming "Authorized Boxer Training," "Righteous and Harmonious Fists," and "Assist the Qing and Destroy the Foreign, Act on Behalf of Heaven." Ronglu even formally welcomed the Boxer leaders to the Tianjin governorship. In response, countries increased their military presence. Starting June 12, the Boxers indiscriminately burned churches, houses of Christians, and foreign businesses in Beijing, resulting in over 300 Christian fatalities. The telegraph office and customs in Tianjin were also looted. At this time, the foreign troops had already captured the Dagu Forts. Cixi and conservative officials viewed the Boxers as a means to resist foreigners, declaring that "war is unavoidable." On June 21, 1900, the Qing court officially declared war on foreign powers, denouncing their brutal and arrogant actions.

Following the declaration of war, the Boxers rampaged through Zhili, causing widespread destruction and killing foreigners and Christians while spreading to Fengtian, Shanxi, Henan, Hunan, and Zhejiang. After Empress Dowager Cixi issued the declaration of war, governors such as Li Hongzhang (李鴻章) of the Two Guangdong, Liu Kunyi (劉坤一) of the Two Jiang, Zhang Zhidong (張之洞) of Huguang, Xu Yinglun (許應騤) of Fujian and Zhejiang, Yuan Shikai (袁世凱) of Shandong, Wang Zhichun (王之春) of Anhui, and De Shou (德壽) of Guangdong defied the orders of "engaging in war with foreign powers and supporting the Boxers," establishing "Southeast Mutual Protection Agreements" with foreign powers.

As Beijing was besieged by the Boxers, the Eight-Nation Alliance (Britain, France, Germany, Russia, the United States, Japan, Italy, and Austria-Hungary) jointly mobilized troops, invading Beijing in August 1900, leading to the collapse of the Boxer and Qing resistance. After the war, China signed the Boxer Protocol with the foreign powers, which included severe terms such as paying large sums in reparations, dismantling coastal defense fortifications, and allowing foreign troops to station in Beijing.

During the Boxer Rebellion, foreign missionaries and Chinese Christians became targets, marking one of the most brutal "Christian persecutions" in late Qing history. It is estimated that nearly 30,000 Chinese Christians were killed during the uprising, along with 241 missionaries. Additionally, a list of 189 individuals who were killed by the Boxers was compiled, including 137 adults, 75 women, and 52 children of missionaries. Regarding mission societies, the China Inland Mission had the highest martyr count (69), followed by the American Presbyterian Church (35) and the Congregational Church (18). Among the regions affected most severely was Shanxi, where it is reported that as many as 15,000 to 20,000 Chinese believers were killed, and of the 91 missionaries from the Shanxi Inland Mission, 36 escaped, 38 were killed, and 17 were missing, with almost all missionaries from the American Presbyterian and British Baptist and Congregational churches being killed.

(2) Late Qing Conflicts Between the State and Christianity (教案) 1901-1909

After the Boxer Rebellion, scholars have found that from 1901 to 1911, a total of 334 civil-religious conflicts occurred, which was a significant decrease compared to the number before the Boxer Rebellion. This reduction was due to the Qing government's increased protection of churches following the Boxer Protocol and their stricter measures against local officials who failed to protect them. Additionally, after the uprising, the Qing government severely prohibited the formation of secret societies, resulting in a decrease in anti-Christian incidents related to such groups (Zhao Shuhao (趙樹好) 2014: 65-66).

Based on various scholarly studies, the causes of civil-religious conflicts during the late Qing period shared similarities with those before the Boxer Rebellion. For instance, Su Ping analyzed 344 anti-Christian cases based on the rumors surrounding them, including claims of "live bloodletting," "poisoning," "disguised officials spreading rumors," and "allegations of churches harboring munitions," which were all related to cases from 1901 to 1902. The spreaders of these rumors included both officials and commoners (Su Ping (蘇萍) 2001: 40).

There are also studies focusing on Zhejiang Province, highlighting the reasons for anti-Christian incidents during the late Qing era, which included land disputes (the Kuaiji Church Incident in 1904, the Huzhou Church Incident, the Shaoxing Church Incident in 1905, the Cixi Church Incident, the Tiantai Church Incident, and the Huzhou Church Incident in 1907), the overbearing behavior of Catholic believers (the Yanzhou Church Incident from 1903 to 1906, the Anji Church Incident in 1905, the Taizhou Church Incident in 1906, the Shanyin Church Incident, and the Xianju Church Incident), and church members' refusal to donate to god worship and monk funds (the Zhenhai Church Incident in 1906). These factors have been longstanding issues that have incited civil-religious conflicts (Zhou Donghua (周東華) 2013: 353-366).

Scholar Lü Shiqiang (呂實強) studying late Qing religious incidents believes that the decrease in such incidents after 1900 was due to local gentry beginning to recognize the importance of Western laws following the Boxer Rebellion, which consequently altered their attitudes toward Christianity (Lü Shiqiang (呂實強) 1985: 314-316).
