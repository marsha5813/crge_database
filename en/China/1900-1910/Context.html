China: Context (1900-1910)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. Qing Dynasty’s Imperial Edict Declaring War on Eight Nations, 1900 (Yang Dachun (楊大春) 2004: 42)

On June 21, 1900, the Qing court issued an edict declaring war on various countries. The text mentioned the Qing government’s policy towards Christianity since the Daoguang (道光) and Xianfeng (咸豐) eras, criticizing the Western powers and churches for their misdeeds in China and referring to those who burned churches as “righteous heroes”:

“For over two hundred years, my dynasty has shown great kindness and mercy, treating all foreigners who come to China as our ancestors would. Since the Daoguang and Xianfeng eras, we allowed them to engage in trade and requested them to propagate their faith, as the court believed in their encouragement of good deeds. Initially, they followed our rules within our borders; yet, in the past thirty years, taking advantage of our benevolence, they have grown increasingly arrogant, oppressing our nation, encroaching on our land, trampling our people, and extorting our property. Even when the court made slight concessions, they became increasingly heinous, bullying ordinary citizens and openly insulted our sanctity. Our people, filled with resentment, long for vengeance. Thus arose the righteous heroes burning churches and slaughtering Christians. The court still hesitates to provoke, maintaining its protective stance, fearing for our people. Therefore, we have repeatedly issued edicts prohibiting such acts, defending the embassies and caring for the Christians. The recent incidents involving the Boxer and Christians were explained as a resolution of old grievances. The court has treated foreigners with gentle respect to the utmost, yet they do not show gratitude, but rather, they threaten; just yesterday, a notice from the British demanded our withdrawal from the Dagu Forts to their custody, or they would attack us. Such menacing threats aim to instill terror within the capital. In our usual relations with neighbors, I have never been disrespectful toward them, yet they, claiming to be a civilized nation, behave recklessly, relying solely on their military strength and weaponry, thus fostering this inevitable rupture! For thirty years, I have treated my people as my descendants, and they revere me as a heavenly ruler; moreover, the compassionate and sacred revival of the cosmos bears witness to the deep-seated grace that penetrates our very bones. With our ancestors as our support and deities ensuring our sincerity, all are filled with loyalty and indignation, a spirit unseen in the ages. I now weep before our ancestral temple, bravely vowing to rally my troops. Rather than seek mere survival at the cost of eternal shame, should we not instead wage a grand campaign and settle this conflict decisively?”

  1. The Boxer Protocol and Christianity, 1901 (Li Chuanbin (李傳斌) 2011: 108-111)

After the Boxer Rebellion, the Qing government began negotiating peace with various nations. Officials from the Qing Dynasty (such as Wang Zhichun (王之春), Governor of Anhui; Liu Kunyi (劉坤一), Governor-General of the Jiangsu, Jiangxi, and Anhui regions; and Pei Weiran (裴維獻), vice prefect of Fengtian) believed that they could establish specific clauses regarding missionary activities in the negotiations, especially limiting them through “commercial interests” or requiring missionaries not to interfere in legal matters. When the Ministers of Military Affairs discussed the draft of the peace negotiations, they raised concerns about Article 10, which concerned punishing foreign officials, suggesting that due to the disharmony between the people and Christians, it could affect Chinese pastors, necessitating a careful examination of the circumstances and respective seriousness. They also proposed creating a separate protocol to address issues with churches. However, the plenipotentiaries, Yikuang (奕劻) and Li Hongzhang (李鴻章), believed that achieving terms to limit missionary activities at this stage of negotiation could be challenging.

In September 1901, China signed the Boxer Protocol with eleven countries, which did not address matters related to missionary activities. However, its punitive and reparative clauses targeted the casualties and destruction resulting from the anti-foreign sentiments during the Boxer Movement.

  1. Yuan Shikai’s “Simplified Regulations for Church Protection,” 1901 (Gan Han (甘韓) 1902)

In 1901, Yuan Shikai (袁世凱), the Governor of Shandong, issued the “Simplified Regulations for Church Protection” in Shandong, stipulating that local officials protect Christianity in designated areas. The regulations consisted of ten articles: (1) Local officials, militia members, gentry, village heads, and leaders must work together to protect clergy and churches, and anyone conspiring to harm clergy or burn and loot churches shall be executed on the spot; (2) If a local church or clergy is again subjected to severe incidents of burning and looting, local residents must jointly propose severe sanctions, and they will be held liable for the losses incurred; (3) Local persons are strictly forbidden from harboring individuals responsible for severe incidents of burning and looting against churches; (4) Local officials must earnestly protect churches and clergy; those who maintain peace for one year will receive recognition three times, while those who maintain peace for three years will be considered for rewards; (5) Proper arrangements and protection must be given to passing missionaries; (6) Civil and military local officials must protect churches and clergy within their jurisdictions; any severe incidents of burning and looting shall result in removal from office and severe punishment; (7) Severe punishment for robbers who burn churches and harm clergy; (8) Those who capture robbers responsible for burning churches and harming clergy, and bring them to the authorities, will receive rewards based on their merit; informants who lead to captures will also receive half of the bounty; (9) Gentry, village heads, and local leaders who diligently prevent and protect churches and clergy from harm, achieving a year of peace, will receive a reward; achieving three years of peace may warrant a medal of honor; (10) In cases of mistakenly purchasing a lost church, if verified, it must be reported to the authorities immediately to exempt from punishment; those who know but do not return the property shall be dealt with severely in accordance with the laws related to receiving stolen goods.(4) Regulations on Church Affairs in Guangdong, 1902 (Wang Tieya (王鐵崖) 1982: 147-148)

In 1902, the Governor-General of Guangdong and Guangxi convened with American and French consuls to establish the “Regulations on Church Affairs in Guangdong,” consisting of seven articles: (1) “Clergy shall not intervene under any pretext in non-church matters”; (2) In case of disputes among missionaries or between Christians and members of other religions, they should respectfully request the gentry to mediate; (3) If missionaries overstep by interfering in disputes among Christians or between Christians and non-Christians, “the supervising authorities must impose strict discipline on the concerned missionaries”; if there are complaints regarding missionaries’ misconduct, “the concerned missionary must listen with humility, investigate impartially, and verify the facts”; (4) Missionaries and Christians must use all legal means to stop violence among the same ethnic group. If a Christian disobeys a missionary’s guidance, they must be strictly disciplined; (5) For cases truly involving church affairs, evidence must be verified, and missionaries should report them to the county magistrate and local officials; if the magistrate’s judgment is deemed unfair, the case may be appealed to the consul; (6) If Christians or those pretending to be in the faith have disputes before joining the church, the consul will not intervene, “specifically to prevent true believers from being hindered”; (7) If bandits post signs saying “church” outside shops, missionaries must inform local officials and the consul for immediate and stern investigations.

  1. Ministry of Foreign Affairs’ “Follow-up Regulations on Church Cases in Shanxi,” 1902 (First Historical Archives of China, Fujian Normal University History Department 1998: 229-236)

In 1902, Shanxi Governor Cen Chunxuan proposed 12 articles regarding follow-up on church cases, which were then submitted to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and circulated to various churches. Each article first pointed out that the origins of church-related conflicts “cannot be solely attributed to missionaries, nor can they be wholly assigned to Chinese officials.” To eliminate contradictions, both sides bear responsibility. Therefore, it was recommended to “dissolve divisions between church communities,” treating civilians and Christians equally. Among the 12 articles, Articles 1 to 6 pertain to local officials’ responsibilities, Articles 7 to 9 pertain to missionaries’ duties, while Articles 10 to 12 outline shared responsibilities between both church and secular individuals.

Regarding local officials’ responsibilities: (1) Local officials should maintain contact with missionaries; “if mutual sincerity is established, business matters wouldn’t encounter barriers. Major issues can be made small, and minor issues can be eliminated”; (2) Local civil and military officials are responsible for protecting churches, missionaries, and Christians; (3) For passing missionaries, their passports should be promptly issued and protected; (4) Disputes should preferably be settled through direct negotiation; if correspondence is necessary, it should be carried out swiftly; (5) Local officials should keep records of churches and Christians, updating them regularly for protection; (6) Christians should be encouraged or punished for good or bad behavior alike. If mercenaries exploit religion to gain protection, they should be severely punished.

Regarding the responsibilities of missionaries: (7) Missionaries must not interfere in legal disputes among Christians; (8) Churches must not harbor guilty Christians; (9) If civilians have unresolved legal cases, they should be temporarily prohibited from joining the faith.

As to the shared responsibilities of external and internal individuals: (10) Local public affairs must be discussed jointly by both civilians and Christians, with no distinction between communities. However, civilians must not force Christians to take part in local religious celebrations and ceremonies; (11) Public expenses involving local beneficial matters must be equally borne by Christians. In terms of local religious celebrations, Christians are exempt from such expenses. However, Christians and their families, who do not contribute “theater money,” should not attend performances. If any wish to watch the performances, they must share the expense, “as performances, although due to religious ceremonies, are nonetheless entertainment and cannot be equated with worship”; (12) Students learning about the faith should assist with sacrificial ceremonies. While Christians only follow one belief, “upon entering Chinese studies, they should never disparage the teachings of the revered sages that originate outside.” Thus, those learning the faith should view “engaging in important matters as an honor.”

  1. Implementation of the New Education System, 1902, 1904 (Chen Jianhua (陳建華) 2006: 82-84)

After the Boxer Rebellion, Empress Dowager Cixi (慈禧) agreed to implement new policies. In December 1900, reform proposals were presented. In January 1901, a reform edict was proclaimed in the name of Guangxu (光緒). In July 1901, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs was established, followed by the Ministry of Commerce in September 1903, the abolition of the imperial examination system in 1905, and the establishment of the Ministry of Education in December. Earlier, in 1902, Zhang Baixi (張百熙) was tasked with drafting a new education system (Renyin Education System), and in 1904, he, along with Zhang Zhidong (張之洞) and Rongqing (榮慶), revised it into the “Guicheng Education System” for official implementation.

The purpose of the new education system was to “base principles on loyalty and filial piety, utilizing Chinese classical studies as the foundation to ensure that students’ hearts are pure, followed by integrating Western knowledge to refine their intellect and skills, aiming for them to become useful talents in the future.” Concurrently, the Confucian worship rituals were incorporated into the new education system; “during the opening and closing of the school and at the beginning of each month… students shall be led to pay respects before the shrine of the Most Honorable Teacher.” Annually, during the Empress Dowager’s and Emperor’s birthday celebrations and on Confucius’s birthday, students must also be led to the ceremony hall to pay their respects. Furthermore, the new education system stipulated that “foreign instructors must not speak of religion… If a hired Western teacher is of missionary origin, it must be stated in the contract that any teachings of scientific subjects must not involve religious discussions, and violations shall result in immediate dismissal,” and they must also “not interfere with the national constitution and any fundamental teachings that are obviously contrary.”In fact, the new education system mainly modeled after Japan, absorbing Western knowledge while rejecting Western teachings within the national educational framework.

  1. Ministry of Education’s Consultation to Provincial Governors, 1906 (Shen Xinmin (沈新民) 2023: 100-102; Zhu Youhuan (朱有瓛), Gao Shiliang (高時良), eds. 1993: 26)

In 1905, the Qing government abolished the imperial examination system and established the Ministry of Education. In 1906, the Educational Association of China, founded by missionaries, submitted a memorandum to the Ministry, expressing the willingness of church schools to accept government oversight and requesting equal status for their graduates with those from official schools, allowing them the opportunity to participate in various examinations and hold official positions. It was decided that missionaries in Beijing and Tianjin would form a follow-up committee to negotiate with the Ministry. If unsuccessful, the American chargé d’affaires, William W. Rockhill, would intervene. Due to the lack of progress in discussions between the Ministry and the Educational Association, Rockhill communicated with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The Ministry sought the opinion of the Ministry of Education, which then notified provincial governors in September 1906: “Education is the foundation for national prosperity; a country has its own citizens, and thus its own education. Not only do the behaviors and customs of the people differ, but the aims of education also cannot be uniformly imposed. Today, in revitalizing educational efforts, provinces must take responsibility for establishing schools; this is an urgent need. To foreign nationals seeking to establish schools within the country, it must be noted that there are no provisions allowing such; only existing schools may operate temporarily without needing formal registration, and in the future, should any foreign national apply to open schools, they likewise do not need registration, and no rewards will be granted to any students.” This was the first time the Ministry expressed the Qing government’s stance on church schools in a consultative form and communicated its execution to all provinces.

  1. Remarks by Qing Government Representatives at the Centenary Mission Conference, 1907 (Centenary Conference Committee 1907: 734-738; Zhao Bifang (趙碧芳) 2019: 140-141, 143-144)

At the Centenary Mission Conference in 1907, the Qing government invited Governor Yuan Dongfang (端方) of Jiangsu and Jiangxi to speak. On May 7, Yuan appointed Tang Yuanzhan (唐元湛) as his representative in Shanghai. Tang, one of the second cohort of Chinese students who studied in the United States, had accompanied five ministers on a political inspection tour abroad in 1905. Speaking in English, he pointed out that the efforts of missionaries over the past century sought to enhance the ethical standards of the Chinese people, facing numerous challenges and scrutiny during this time; however, driven by noble faith, they did not give up.

He mentioned the mutual contradictions between missionaries and China, stemming from differing views on the concept of “doing good.” He expressed hope for both sides to adhere to the principle of non-interference while acknowledging that no ethnicity or faith could monopolize the ideals or notions of goodness. Undoubtedly, missionaries believe that the tendency of humanity leans towards evil, yet he firmly believes that the overall trend of humanity is towards goodness. He recognized that differing views on religious and political issues exist, but these differences should not hinder everyone from exercising tolerance and compassion. While firmly believing in the superiority of their methods, it is essential to remember that others also have the right to believe their methods are the best, with the most important principle being the opposition to the use of force.

Here, Tang critiqued missionaries for relying too heavily on “the arm of flesh” instead of trusting “the arm of the Lord.” He quoted Jesus: “Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.” He argued that this clearly indicates enduring injustice silently aligns more closely with the spirit of Christianity than claiming treaty rights; suffering injustice aligns more with Christian ideals than punishing the wrongdoer. However, it is well known that the historical relationship between missionaries and the Chinese government has contradicted this principle, and the consequences of these missteps will negatively impact missionary efforts.

He reminded Westerners propagating their doctrine in China that they face a civilization older than their own. Everyone cherishes their racial and familial histories, but some outsiders trample upon what the Chinese value most and treat others with superiority. He suggested that missionaries should shed their sense of superiority to achieve their goals. They should also gain a deeper understanding of Confucian classics; the best approach would be for knowledgeable missionaries to impart spiritual insights to capable Chinese scholars, who could then teach those capable of making their own judgments and choosing their faith. He specifically used ancestor worship as an example, emphasizing that this respect and admiration is merely an expression of filial piety. Similarly, the respect that Chinese people have for Confucius stems from admiration for this wise sage.Everyone has the right to hold their own views and beliefs. Proper consideration of the religious sentiments of the general populace not only helps missionaries create and maintain good relationships with China but also allows them to assist China, while China can in turn help the missionaries. Thus, despite being divided into different sects and beliefs, whether Confucian or Christian, they can jointly strive with compassion. This compassion transcends all faiths and belongs to all beliefs, enabling beneficial contributions among all nations.

  1. Modification of Missionary Issues in Sino-Foreign Trade Agreements, 1902-1906

According to Article 11 of the Boxer Protocol, the Qing government agreed to modify trade treaties with various countries. In 1902, Britain was the first to propose modifications to the trade treaties, followed by further negotiations with the United States, Japan, Portugal, Germany, and Italy. Except for Japan, the negotiations with the other countries involved missionary issues. However, ultimately, China and Portugal did not sign a treaty due to disagreements (Li Chuanbin (李傳斌) 2011: 112, 117).

Sino-British Trade and Missionary Litigation Terms, 1902: In February 1902, China and Britain signed the “Trade and Missionary Litigation Terms,” which stipulates: (1) “Both Christianity and Catholicism are based on encouraging goodness; thus, there is nothing wrong with missionary work. All who teach and learn must be equally protected. Therefore, all who enter the interior for missionary work must report their names, organization names, and intended destinations to their respective consulates at the trade ports, so that permits can be issued by the local officials for their protection. Local officials must provide proper protection, and the consul must monitor them at all times. All who wish to convert shall face no restrictions; however, both converts and non-converts are considered Chinese citizens and must abide by Chinese laws and regulations, with missionaries not intervening in the local officials’ governance over the people, nor may local officials differentiate between Christians and non-Christians. Those wishing to acquire property in the interior for building houses, orphanages, and hospitals must state in the contracts that the property is for a particular church’s public use and cannot be registered solely in the names of missionaries or converts, and the church buildings must adhere to local architectural styles, with consent from neighboring gentry for harmonious coexistence, and local officials may inspect the premises at any time”; (2) “Although previous treaties stated that British subjects should be handled by British officials according to British laws, British merchants cannot use this treaty to exempt themselves from local authority or disregard Chinese laws. All Chinese laws that forbid actions also apply equally to British subjects, to maintain fairness. Furthermore, China is willing that all Sino-Western disputes are governed uniformly, so we propose to hire well-known lawyers from abroad who are familiar with Chinese laws to draft legal codes, establishing courts at trade ports to process all cases involving Chinese and British subjects according to the law pending China’s approval. If no officials familiar with new laws are available, they may hire British lawyers to conduct hearings, and it is permissible for Chinese citizens to file complaints either with local officials or at the established court” (Zhao Shuhau (趙樹好) 2014: 67).

Sino-American Trade Treaty, 1903: In August 1903, Clause 14 of the Sino-American Trade Treaty stipulates: “The purpose of Christianity is to encourage doing good; thus, those who wish to extend this to others must also do so. All honest and law-abiding missionaries must not suffer harassment due to their faith. Any Chinese who voluntarily wishes to follow Christ shall face no obstacles; however, both converts and non-converts are considered Chinese citizens and must comply with Chinese laws, respect officials, and coexist harmoniously. Those who may commit offenses before or after conversion shall not escape accountability for their actions. All Chinese are obligated to pay legally mandated taxes, and converts are no exception. However, taxation for purposes that conflict with Christian teachings, such as religious festivals, may not be levied on converts. Missionaries must not interfere with the authority of Chinese officials in governing the Chinese populace, and Chinese officials must not discriminate between converts and non-converts, enforcing laws impartially so that both groups can coexist peacefully. American churches are permitted to lease and eternally rent properties as church assets for missionary work, but only after local officials have verified and stamped the land contracts may missionaries proceed to construct suitable buildings for charitable purposes” (Zhao Shuhau (趙樹好) 2014: 68).

Sino-Portuguese Trade Treaty, 1904: In July 1904, Clause 17 of the Sino-Portuguese Trade Treaty stipulates: “China views the matter of mission work as requiring detailed consultation to avoid the resurgence of old grievances. Should China and countries with existing treaties send representatives to investigate this matter, they must strive to devise solutions. Portugal, having the responsibility to protect its Catholic churches in China, is especially willing to send representatives to jointly consult and work towards the goal of harmonious coexistence between the people and the faith.”All who adhere to their religious rules, whether Chinese or Portuguese citizens practicing their faith, are to encourage goodness and must not be subjected to harassment or mistreatment because of their beliefs. Both converts and non-converts are Chinese citizens and are expected to follow Chinese laws, respect officials, and live harmoniously together. Those who convert must still face legal accountability for any crimes committed before or after conversion. All Chinese must pay the stipulated taxes, and converts are not exempt from these obligations. However, levying taxes for purposes that conflict with Christian teachings, such as religious festivals, may not be imposed on converts. Missionaries must not interfere with the authority of Chinese officials in governing Chinese citizens, and Chinese officials must not discriminate between converts and non-converts; they must enforce laws fairly so that both groups can coexist peacefully. The Portuguese church is permitted to lease and hold properties for church use, but only after local officials have verified and stamped the land contracts may the missionaries construct suitable buildings to carry out charitable works (Zhao Shuhau (趙樹好) 2014: 68).

Sino-German Trade Treaty, 1905: In October 1905, Clause 14 of the Sino-German Trade Treaty stipulated: “There must be no obstruction to Germans and Chinese propagating their faith and practicing their religion. Their properties must be adequately protected, and German missionaries must not interfere with the authority of Chinese officials in governing Chinese citizens” (Zhao Shuhau (趙樹好) 2014: 68).

In June 1908, China signed a new Sino-Swedish (瑞典) Trade Treaty which contained clauses regarding Christianity that were similar to those in the Sino-American Trade Treaty (Zhao Shuhau (趙樹好) 2014: 68; Li Chuanbin (李傳斌) 2011: 117).

With the signing of treaties between China and Britain (also including treaties with the United States, Portugal, Germany, and Sweden), not only were the protective clauses for missionaries reiterated, but new provisions were added to prevent missionaries from interfering in legal proceedings and the governance of the Chinese populace by Chinese officials. Furthermore, it reaffirmed that Christians are Chinese citizens and should abide by Chinese laws. The concept of “harmonious coexistence between the people and the faith” reflects a desire to properly manage church affairs following the Boxer Rebellion to prevent future conflicts between the two.

  1. Proposal for “Harmonizing Civil and Religious Affairs” by the Sichuan Provincial Consultative Council, 1909 (Li Chuanbin (李傳斌) 2011: 120-121)

In 1906, the Qing court announced plans for constitutional reform, and in the following year established the Privy Council, leading to the formation of consultative councils in various provinces. By 1909, provincial consultative councils were gradually established.

During its first session in 1909, the Sichuan Provincial Consultative Council proposed a motion on “Harmonizing Civil and Religious Affairs,” asking council members to suggest methods for achieving “long-term peaceful coexistence between the people and the faith.” Opinions among the members could be categorized into proactive and reactive measures. The proactive approach focused on “revitalizing the nation’s strength and promoting education,” as “if the nation is weak, the people will lack wisdom,” resulting in “fear of religion,” which can lead to “hatred of religion.” With a strong nation and educated citizens, they could resolve issues surrounding consular jurisdiction, preventing foreign disrespect and ensuring that clergy do not conspire with outsiders. However, while proactive measures are foundational and long-term solutions, they cannot resolve immediate issues, leading to the need for passive emergency measures: (1) Local officials must judge civil and religious disputes “based on reason without showing favoritism to religion over the people.” They recommended drafting concise treaties and international laws to serve as guidelines; (2) Send distinguished local elites to advise against “discriminating against Christians”; (3) The Governor should notify all foreign consuls, requiring their missionaries to adhere to treaties and not interfere in legal disputes.

The response from the Governor of Sichuan to these proposed solutions was: “If the proactive measures can indeed be implemented, there would be no need for harmony; this is self-evident. As for the three passive measures, many have already been executed by the Governor’s office, such as issuing treaty guidelines and strictly regulating legal proceedings without distinguishing between civil and religious matters.”

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Review of the American Missionary Society’s Missionary Policy in China, 1900 (Brown 1901: 398-405)

On September 21, 1900, the Presbyterian Board of Foreign Missions (美國長老會海外差會) convened a meeting in New York with 17 Christian missionary societies from North America to discuss the missionary policy in China following the Boxer Rebellion (義和團). Eight Presbyterian missionaries who had experienced the violence were invited back to share their insights. The topics discussed included: restoration and reconstruction, compensation, the relationship between missionaries and government power, and cooperation among churches.

Regarding compensation, the society unanimously agreed that no excessive demands should be made on China. Therefore, claims should not include the suffering, deaths, or interruptions in work endured by missionaries (except in cases where the deaths of missionaries resulted in loss of economic support for their families) but should be limited to actual property damage and additional expenses incurred due to the violence. Furthermore, compensation claims should not be made by individual missionaries to the authorities, but rather by relevant committees of each missionary society. Additionally, the societies agreed that missionary organizations should not interfere in government policies toward China or make any political suggestions. When faced with future issues, it was advised that missionaries avoid unnecessary negotiations with local Chinese officials. The meeting generally hoped that, in resuming their work, the missionary societies could strengthen cooperation.

  1. Regulations for British Missionaries in Shandong, 1901 (Li Gangji 李剛己 1986: Volume 2, Part 1, 8-10)

Following the Boxer Rebellion, the British Baptist Missionary Society in Shandong formulated the “Regulations for British Missionaries in Shandong” to ease tensions between local communities and the church, hoping to foster harmony. The regulations comprise 25 articles, broadly covering the following aspects: (1) Clarification of missionary motives, stating no political or personal interests: “Missionaries come to China not as state delegates; all expenses are voluntarily donated by charitable individuals,” “Missionaries encourage people to pursue virtue, and in no way aim for worldly gain,” “Our mission’s charitable works, such as providing aid and medical services, adhere to the principle of loving others as oneself, with no intention to merely win hearts,” etc.; (2) Clarification of misunderstandings regarding church activities: “Being baptized in the church is like a cleansing bath, signifying a new beginning, not a return to old ways, with no ulterior motives,” “Seating in church is strictly defined, ensuring no confusion of genders,” “Unlike secretive cults, our church activities, such as worship, are open and transparent, with no hidden agenda, and no restrictions on observation”; (3) Respect for Chinese customs and culture: “Ancient sages advocated filial piety and goodness, which both church members and laypeople admire, and we urge educators to avoid slandering traditional teachings,” “Missionaries have traveled extensively in the West and, upon entering China, they adhere strictly to sacred teachings and comply with local customs,” “In the presence of officials and gentry, one should show respect and adhere to local customs, both in major and minor matters”; (4) Issues related to church members: “Chinese individuals who practice religion are still loyal citizens; disputes involving them due to civil matters should be managed by local officials, treating them equally with other citizens,” “In any legal disputes involving church members, if not initiated by religious matters, missionaries will not intervene,” “Those wishing to join the church must first be thoroughly investigated regarding any previous disputes before being allowed in. If they cause trouble after joining, they will be immediately dismissed”; (5) Relationship between the church and local society: “Activities such as welcoming gods, feasting, or building temples are unrelated to those practicing the religion,” “If bridges, roads, or gates are constructed, they should be carried out in accordance with public norms,” “Our society is very cautious in building churches, and if they truly obstruct the locals, we are willing to make accommodations,” “Missionaries often reside in local-style homes; if they do build structures, even if they have some Western characteristics, they will not be overly imposing and will not construct tall towers.” Lastly, it is emphasized: “The above items are encouraged to be established as regulations for practitioners, and those studying should adhere to them as guiding principles, with no room for suspicion. May officials and citizens understand and act justly, observing the principle of equality under the emperor’s rule, which aligns with the biblical teachings of maintaining one’s duties. The harmony between religion and community is our sincere hope.”

  1. Missionaries’ Discussion on the Protection Clauses of the Treaty, 1907 (Kevin 2007)

In 1907, during a centennial missionary conference, discussions regarding treaty protections took place. C. W. Mateer (狄考文) from the Northern Presbyterian Church reported that the attitude of the Chinese government and people towards missionaries had become positive and friendly compared to 40 years ago. However, the relationship between Christianity and the government, as well as the treaty issues, remained unresolved. In evaluating the protection clauses for Christianity in the treaty, Mateer recognized that Protestant missions in China were closely tied to the commercial treaties established between Western countries and China. Although Western nations applied various pressures to obtain commercial privileges, he believed that pressure was not used to gain religious tolerance and protection.The war and the resulting treaties are essentially between nations and commercial in nature, with religious tolerance for Christianity being a mere collateral event. He intentionally distinguishes the so-called “religious protection” clauses from Western political, military pressures and commercial interests. Furthermore, he affirms that the treaties indeed allowed for the free dissemination of Christianity. For self-protection, missionaries occasionally resorted to pressure tactics at different times, but very rarely purely for the protection of Chinese believers. The report maintains a certain vigilance regarding the injustices in the process of treaty formulation, but holds a positive attitude toward the protective clauses for missionaries within the treaties. Another missionary from the Northern Presbyterian Church, J. C. Garritt, even believed that these rights were granted by the Chinese government in accordance with God’s will.

Undoubtedly, there were differing opinions within the missionary community regarding the treaty issue, primarily reflected in two points: (1) the question of whether to retain or abolish the missionary clauses in the treaty and whether missionaries should continue to invoke these clauses or voluntarily relinquish them; (2) whether Chinese Christians should invoke treaty protections and whether missionaries should pressure Qing officials on behalf of persecuted Chinese believers. Regarding the first issue, Garritt pointed out two extreme positions among missionaries: one being the unrestricted use of treaty provisions and the other being a complete abandonment. Di Kaowen (狄考文) acknowledged that there has been a recent call suggesting that the treaty has become an obstacle to the spread of the Gospel in China, but he countered with two arguments. First, regarding practical effects, the treaty indeed provided legal protection for missionaries and Chinese believers. Without such protection, there could have been more bloodshed and persecution, and the church’s success would not have come as quickly. Whether to invoke the treaty should be determined by individuals based on their own circumstances. Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. The Intensification of Great Powers’ Competition in the Last Decade of the Qing Dynasty (Guo Tingyi 郭廷以 1983: 351-362)

The Boxer Rebellion in 1900 led to the Eight-Nation Alliance entering Beijing, ultimately resulting in the Qing court signing the Boxer Protocol. This event fully reflected the extreme ignorance and incompetence of the Qing court. At this time, Russia took the opportunity to seek to occupy Northeast China, and the Russian troops in Lüshunkou did not withdraw after the treaty. Russia’s ambition to occupy the three northeastern provinces prompted Britain and Japan to form an alliance in 1902, aimed at maintaining the territorial integrity of China and Korea and jointly protecting British and Japanese interests in China and Korea. If either Britain or Japan went to war with another country, the other would remain neutral. If another country assisted the enemy, Britain and Japan would respond together. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance was clearly intended to counter Russia’s expansion. In April 1902, the “Sino-Russian Transfer of Northeast Regions Treaty” was signed, in which Russia agreed to restore China’s rights in Northeast China and withdraw in three phases over 18 months. However, this act seemed to be merely a stalling tactic, as Russia did not actually withdraw and, instead, made new demands to China in April 1903, prompting protests from both Britain and Japan. During negotiations between Japan and Russia, Russia further demanded that both Korea and Manchuria belonged to it. The contradictions between the two sides intensified, and eventually, in 1904, Japan and Russia went to war, but the battlefield was east of the Liao River in China. Despite the significant disparity in strength, the Japanese forces ultimately achieved victory. In September 1905, Japan and Russia signed a treaty in Portsmouth, United States, recognizing Korea as belonging to Japan, and ceded Lüshunkou, Dalian, the South Manchuria Railway, and the southern part of Sakhalin to Japan. In 1907, Japan and Russia signed a secret agreement dividing southern and northern Manchuria into spheres of influence, whereby Japan recognized Russia’s special interests in Outer Mongolia, and Russia acknowledged Japan’s political relationship with Korea. Northeast China became a jointly divided sphere of influence for both Japan and Russia.

After the signing of the Boxer Protocol, China and foreign countries re-negotiated commercial agreements, further opening up to foreign investment. Between 1901 and 1910, 38 new treaty ports were opened, and the number of concessions increased. At the same time, various countries directly built and operated railways in China, with no railway in the late Qing period being under Chinese control. Additionally, foreign countries competed for mining rights in China, with foreign investments in mining during the late Qing period accounting for 80% of the total national amount.

  1. The New Policies of the Last Decade of the Qing Dynasty (Guo Tingyi 郭廷以 1983: 362-364, 386-393; Zhang Pengyuan 张朋园 2005)

Faced with the predicament after the Boxer Rebellion, Empress Dowager Cixi (慈禧) ordered ministers both inside and outside the court to supervise and implement reforms in December 1900. In January 1901, in the name of Guangxu (光绪), a formal edict for reform was issued. In terms of government structure, in April 1901, a Bureau of Political Affairs was established, and in July, the Grand Council was converted into the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. In September 1903, a Ministry of Commerce was established; in October 1905, a Department of Police was set up; and in December, a Ministry of Education and a Military Training Bureau were established. The areas covered by these reforms were actually similar to those of the Hundred Days’ Reform, but there were also several new initiatives, especially regarding the relationship between the Manchus and Han Chinese. In 1902, intermarriage between Manchus and Han Chinese was permitted, and women were encouraged to abandon the practice of foot binding. In 1904, it was stipulated that military officer positions could be held by Han Chinese, and new criminal codes were to be implemented for all Banner people. In 1905, measures were proposed to eliminate prejudices against Manchus and Han Chinese. Additionally, a significant reform was the abolition of the imperial examination system, which had been in place for over a thousand years, and the promotion of a new educational system starting in 1905. In 1906, regulations for girls’ schools (normal and primary schools) were defined.

In 1904, Zhang Zhidong (張之洞) petitioned for constitutional reforms, using Japan as a model. In July 1905, the Qing court sent five ministers abroad to investigate political conditions; three went to Japan, Britain, France, and Belgium, while the other two traveled to the United States, Germany, Italy, and Austria. Upon their return, the five ministers unanimously agreed on the necessity of constitutional reform. In September 1906, an edict was issued to emulate constitutional governance, starting with reforming the official system as a preparatory foundation for constitutionalism, with a timeline for implementation set for a few years later. At this time, public support organizations for constitutional governance were established one after another, such as the “National Constitutional Society” established by Kang Youwei (康有为). In December, a “Constitutional Preparation Society” was formed. In 1907, the “Constitutional Assembly” emerged. In 1908, the regulations for the Consultative Assembly and the election of members were defined, and in August, a draft constitution was issued, setting a national assembly to convene nine years later. In October 1909, consultative assemblies were established in various provinces. In January 1910, constitutional proponents petitioned for a national assembly to be convened within a year. However, the Qing court rejected this on the grounds that the people’s knowledge was not adequate. In June, constitutional proponents petitioned again, and in August, they gathered in Beijing, demanding the immediate opening of the national assembly and the formation of a responsible cabinet. In November, the Qing court announced that the preparation period for constitutional reform would be shortened from nine years to six years, with plans to establish a council in 1913 and to organize a cabinet and draft a constitution.

However, at this time, the Chinese revolutionary movement, although facing suppression from the Qing court, was flourishing. Between 1900 and 1908, there were seven uprisings, which, although failed, did not extinguish the revolutionary fire. Finally, in 1911, the Wuchang Uprising occurred, turning constitutional monarchy into an illusion.(3) The Revolutionary Movement and Nationalism (Guo Tingyi 郭廷以 1983: 381-382; Zhu Hanyuan 朱浤源 1993: 332-336)

In 1894, Sun Yat-sen (孫中山) established the Xingzhong Association in Honolulu, with the aim of “exterminating the Tartars, restoring China, and founding a federal government.” In 1905, when he founded the Tongmenghui in Tokyo, he proposed the revolutionary platform of “exterminating the Tartars, restoring China, founding the Republic, and equalizing land rights.” It is evident that from the Xingzhong Association to the Tongmenghui, Sun Yat-sen consistently upheld the principle of “exterminating the Tartars and restoring China,” reflecting his Han-centric “small nationalism” (as per Liang Qichao (梁啟超)). At that time, there was a debate between the revolutionaries and the constitutionalists, one point of contention being the exclusion of Manchus. The constitutionalists’ “New People’s Journal” contended that the Han people lacked the capability to establish their own nation and that in terms of political and legal matters, the Han and Manchus were equal, thus advocating the integration of various ethnic groups within the country to resist foreign enemies. They argued that insisting on the exclusion of Manchus would lead to a vengeful mentality and incite riots. The revolutionaries’ “Minbao,” on the other hand, asserted that exclusion was necessary to rid the country of the oppressive Manchu government, and that once the Manchu dynasty collapsed, various ethnic groups within the country could merge; thus, exclusion was a prerequisite for political revolution.

Scholars point out that during the late Qing revolutionary movement, Sun Yat-sen primarily leveraged anti-Manchu sentiment to promote the revolution, which can be seen as a strategy for overturning the Qing Dynasty. Therefore, narrow ethnic sentiments became a characteristic of Sun Yat-sen’s internal nationalism during this phase. In terms of foreign relations, while Sun Yat-sen criticized the great powers during the revolutionary movement, there wasn’t a strong anti-imperialist consciousness; rather, he sought support for China’s revolution from all countries and advocated for Sino-Japanese cooperation. However, with the establishment of the Republic and the achievement of the goal of overthrowing the Qing Dynasty, Sun Yat-sen’s nationalism underwent adjustments in the 1910s.
