China: Context (1900-1910)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. Qing Dynasty’s Edict Declaring War on Eight Nations 1900 (Yang Dachun 2004: 42)

On June 21, 1900, the Qing court issued an edict declaring war on various countries. The text mentioned the Qing government’s policy towards Christianity since the Daoguang and Xianfeng reigns, and condemned the evil acts of Western powers and churches in China, stating that those who burned churches in various places were “brave volunteers”:

“For over two hundred years, our dynasty has shown deep benevolence and grace; whenever foreigners come to China, our ancestors never failed to treat them with kindness. Starting from the Daoguang and Xianfeng years, we allowed them to engage in mutual trade and even to preach in our country, as the court believed that they advocated goodness and granted their requests. Initially, they operated within our boundaries and adhered to our restrictions; however, over the past thirty years, relying on our benevolence and a sense of complacency, they have become increasingly arrogant, oppressing our nation, encroaching upon our land, trampling on our people, and extorting our wealth. The court has made some accommodations, yet they have taken advantage of our kindness, becoming bolder by the day, committing acts of intimidation against commoners and disrespecting our sacredness on a larger scale. Our loyal citizens harbor grievances, yearning to retaliate. This is how the brave volunteers arise to burn churches and slaughter believers. The court still refuses to provoke, continuing to offer protection, fearing for our people. Hence, there have been repeated edicts issued to prohibit violent acts, to protect embassies, and to show compassion towards believers. Therefore, the recent instruction that both boxers and believers are our loyal subjects was originally meant to clarify local grievances. The court has been benevolent towards foreigners, to the utmost, yet they do not appreciate this; instead, they resort to intimidation, and just yesterday a letter from the British demanded that we withdraw from the Dagu Fort and cede it to their custody, threatening military action otherwise. Such threatening words are intended to provoke us and shake the capital. In our daily interactions with neighbors, I have never been disrespectful to them. They, who call themselves a civilized nation, act without courtesy, relying solely on their military might to create such a rupture! I have ruled for thirty years, treating the people as my own children, and the people revere me as their sovereign; moreover, with the benevolence of the sage emperor nurturing the universe, the grace bestowed penetrates to the marrow. Our ancestors are our support, the deities respond to our sincerity, and everyone is filled with righteous anger, a feeling unprecedented in history. Today, I weep as I report to the ancestral temple, vowing passionately to rally our forces. Is it better to stubbornly seek survival, bringing shame for eternity, or to boldly engage in battle and determine our fate?”

  1. The Boxer Protocol and Christianity 1901 (Li Chuanbin 2011: 108-111)

After the Boxer Rebellion, the Qing government began negotiations with various countries. Qing officials (such as Anhui Governor Wang Zhichun, Viceroy of Jiangsu and Zhejiang Liu Kunyi, and Deputy Prefect of Fengtian Pei Weian) believed it was possible to establish specific clauses for missionary work during negotiations, particularly to restrict it under “commercial interests” or require missionaries not to interfere in legal matters. When the ministers reviewed the provisions of the “Outline of Negotiation,” they specifically mentioned the punishment of officials who fostered anti-foreign sentiments, noting that discord between believers and non-believers could harm Chinese missionaries; thus, the situation needed to be investigated and addressed according to severity. They also suggested establishing specific provisions to address pastoral matters. However, the plenipotentiaries Yikuang and Li Hongzhang believed it would be difficult to achieve restrictions on missionaries in the current negotiations.

In September 1901, China signed the Boxer Protocol with eleven nations, which did not include any content related to missionary work. However, the clauses regarding punishment and compensation were established in response to the casualties and destruction caused by the anti-foreign and anti-Christian sentiments during the Boxer Movement.

  1. Yuan Shikai’s “Brief Regulations for Protection of Missionary Work” 1901 (Gan Han 1902)

In 1901, Shandong Governor Yuan Shikai promulgated the “Brief Regulations for Protection of Missionary Work” in Shandong, which mandated local officials to protect Christianity in the designated areas. The regulations consisted of ten articles: (1) All local officials, garrisons, local notables, heads of villages, and other community members must work together to protect missionaries and churches. Those who conspire to harm missionaries or burn, loot, or demolish churches will be executed on the spot; (2) If churches or missionaries in the area encounter serious incidents of burning and looting, local officials must bring forth the proposals to investigate and punish severely, and bear the losses incurred by the churches; (3) Local persons are strictly forbidden from harboring perpetrators of severe crimes against churches; (4) Local officials must seriously protect churches and missionaries; those who manage to maintain peace for a year will be credited with substantial merit three times, and those who achieve peace for three years will be awarded; (5) Proper care and protection must be given to passing missionaries; (6) Civil and military local officials must guard churches and missionaries within their jurisdictions; if severe incidents of burning and looting occur, they will be removed and severely punished; (7) Severe punishment for those who burn, loot churches, or harm missionaries; (8) Anyone who captures assailants responsible for burning and looting churches or harming missionaries and delivers them to the authorities will receive rewards, and those who inform of potential incidents leading to arrest will also receive half the compensation; (9) Notables, heads of villages, and community leaders who demonstrate serious prevention and protection of churches and missionaries with a peaceful year will be rewarded, and those with three years of peace will be considered for merit badges; (10) Any wrongful acquisition of churches by purchasing will be reported to the authorities immediately upon investigation, avoiding culpability. However, those who are aware but do not return will be punished strictly according to the law against receiving stolen goods.(4) “Regulations on Religious Affairs in Guangdong” 1902 (Wang Tieya 1982: 147-148)

In 1902, the Governor-General of Guangdong and Guangxi, in conjunction with American and French consuls, agreed on the “Regulations on Religious Affairs in Guangdong,” which comprised seven articles: (1) “Except for religious disputes, missionaries shall not intervene under any pretext”; (2) In the event of disputes between missionaries or between missionaries and adherents of other faiths, local notables should mediate to reach a settlement; (3) If missionaries improperly interfere in disputes among their own adherents or with non-believers, “the responsible missionary must be strictly restrained immediately”; should there be complaints regarding missionaries acting unprofessionally, “the responsible missionaries must listen humbly and conduct a fair inquiry”; (4) Missionaries and adherents must exert efforts to prevent clashes among their communities. If an adherent disobeys the instructions of their missionary, strict restraints must be imposed; (5) For cases that genuinely involve religious affairs, evidence must be confirmed, and missionaries must first report to county magistrates and local officials. If county magistrates are deemed unfair, cases can be appealed to the consul; (6) If adherents and imposters had disputes with others before joining the faith, consuls will have no regard for these cases, “specifically to prevent true seekers from being turned away”; (7) If any assailants post “Church” notices outside shops, missionaries must inform local officials and consuls to take severe action.

  1. Ministry of Foreign Affairs “Post-incident Regulations for Shanxi” 1902 (China First Historical Archives, Fujian Normal University History Department 1998: 229-236)

In 1902, Shanxi Governor Cen Chunxuan drafted twelve articles regarding the aftermath of religious incidents, which were submitted to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and subsequently communicated to various churches. Each article first stated that the causes of religious incidents “cannot be wholly attributed to missionaries nor to Chinese officials.” To resolve conflicts, both sides bear responsibility. Thus, it was suggested to “dismantle class distinctions between religious and non-religious persons,” treating all parties equitably. The twelve articles delineated responsibilities for local officials and missionaries differently, with articles one to six addressing local officials, articles seven to nine addressing missionaries, and articles ten to twelve outlining shared responsibilities for both religious and non-religious communities.

Regarding the duties of local officials: (1) Local officials should engage with missionaries, as “if mutual trust is established, matters can be handled without barriers. Major issues can be minimized, and minor matters can be eliminated”; (2) Local civil and military officials are responsible for protecting churches, missionaries, and adherents; (3) They should promptly clarify and ensure the protection of passing missionaries; (4) Incidents should ideally be resolved through negotiation; should letters be exchanged, they should be handled expeditiously; (5) Local officials should register churches and adherents, regularly updating records for their protection; (6) Adherents should face appropriate commendation or punishment for their actions. Strict punishment is required for ungenuine adherents who misuse their faith.

Regarding the responsibilities of missionaries: (7) Missionaries must not interfere in legal disputes among adherents; (8) Churches must not harbor wrongdoers among their adherents; (9) If a non-adherent’s legal case remains unresolved, their entry into the faith should be postponed.

As for the shared duties of religious and non-religious individuals: (10) Local public affairs should be collaboratively discussed, distinguishing between religious and non-religious communities is unnecessary. However, in matters of local rituals and festivals, non-adherents cannot compel adherents to participate in discussions; (11) Local public expenditures, which concern the welfare of the community, must equally involve adherents. Adherents are exempt from contributions for rituals and festivals, yet neither they nor their families may attend theater without payment. Those wishing to attend must pay for tickets, as enjoying theatrical performances, though associated with rituals, should not be equated with sacred observances; (12) Students learning the faith should assist in honoring shrine ceremonies. Adherents, though dedicated to one faith, “when entering Chinese academia, must void claims of superiority over ancient sages; therefore, those engaged in learning should find honor in diligence.”

  1. Implementation of the New Education System 1902, 1904 (Chen Jianhua 2006: 82-84)

After the Boxer Rebellion, Cixi agreed to implement new policies. In December 1900, a reform was proposed. In January 1901, reforms were issued in the name of Guangxu. In July 1901, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs was established, followed by the Ministry of Commerce in September 1903, and the abolition of the imperial examination system in 1905. Even earlier, in 1902, Zhang Baixi was commissioned to draft a new education system (the Renyin education system). By 1904, it was revised in collaboration with Zhang Zhidong and Rongqing to become the “Guiching education system,” which was formally implemented.

The aim of the new education system was to “base on loyalty and filial piety, using the study of Chinese classics as a foundation, so that students’ intentions may become pure; thereafter, Western learning would refine their knowledge and skills, aiming for future usefulness.” Simultaneously, the rituals honoring Confucius were incorporated into the new educational system, stating, “At the beginning and end of terms, and on the first day of each month… students must proceed to the altar of the Most Holy Ancestor to pay respect.” Furthermore, students were required to participate in ceremonies during the Empress Dowager’s and Emperor’s birthdays, as well as on Confucius’s birthday. Additionally, the new regulations specified, “foreign instructors must not reference religion… If a Western teacher is a missionary, it must be clearly stated in the contract that in teaching scientific subjects, they must not use religious language; violations will lead to immediate termination,” and they must not “interfere with constitutional affairs or engage in actions that contradict the doctrines of each faith.”In fact, the new education system primarily emulated the Japanese model, which absorbed Western learning while rejecting Western religion within the national education framework.

  1. Communications from the Ministry of Education to Provincial Governors 1906 (Shen Xinmin 2023: 100-102; Zhu Youhuan, Gao Shiliang eds. 1993: 26)

In 1905, the Qing government abolished the imperial examination system and established the Ministry of Education. In 1906, the “Educational Association of China,” founded by missionaries, submitted a memorandum to the ministry, expressing the willingness of church schools to accept government oversight and allow graduates from church schools to enjoy equal status with those from official schools, providing opportunities to participate in various examinations and hold official positions. At that time, it was decided to establish a follow-up committee of missionaries in Beijing and Tianjin to negotiate with the ministry. If no results were achieved, the American Minister to China, William W. Rockhill, would intervene. Due to the unsuccessful negotiations between the ministry and the Educational Association of China, Rockhill submitted a notice to the Qing Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The ministry consulted with the Ministry of Education, which in September 1906 informed provincial governors: “Education is the foundation of a powerful nation; every country has its own citizens and thus its own education. Not only do the customs and traditions of the people vary, but the educational purposes also differ significantly. Today, the revival of educational affairs is a pressing duty for each province; it is necessary to promptly establish schools to provide the populace with venues for learning. As for foreigners establishing schools in the interior, no such allowance is permitted according to the established regulations; aside from the schools already established being temporarily permitted, there is no need for further registries for future requests from foreigners to open schools in the interior, and students will not receive any rewards.” This was the first instance where the Ministry of Education formally expressed the Qing government’s stance toward church schools and ordered implementation across provinces.

  1. Speech by Qing Government Representatives at the Centenary Missionary Conference 1907 (Centenary Conference Committee 1907:734-738; Zhao Bifang 2019: 140-141, 143-144)

At the Centenary Missionary Conference in 1907, the Qing government’s Viceroy of Liangjiang, Duanfang, was invited to give a speech. On May 7, he sent his representative, Tong Yuanzhan (Y. C. Tong), a graduate of the second class of Chinese students sent to study in the United States, who had traveled abroad in 1905 with five ministers to investigate political matters. Speaking in English, he noted that for one hundred years, the efforts of missionaries aimed to elevate the ethical and moral standards of the Chinese people, encountering many challenges and examinations along the way, yet remaining steadfast due to their noble faith.

He mentioned the bilateral contradictions between missionaries and the Chinese regarding the concept of “doing good,” hoping both sides would adhere to the principle of non-interference and recognize that no single nation or faith can monopolize the ideals or concepts of doing good. Undoubtedly, while missionaries believed humanity was trending towards evil, he maintained a strong conviction that the overall trend of humanity is towards goodness. He acknowledged that differing views on religious and political matters exist, yet these differences should not impede the practice of tolerance and kindness. While firmly believing that their methods were the best, they should also remember that others have the right to believe that their own methods are equally valid, and it is crucial to oppose the use of force.

Here, Tong criticized missionaries for relying too heavily on “the arm of flesh” rather than trusting “the arm of the Lord.” He quoted Jesus, saying, “Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves,” believing this clearly indicates that silently enduring injustice is more in line with the Christian spirit than asserting rights established by treaties; seeking retribution is less Christian than enduring harm. However, it is well-known that the history between missionaries and the Chinese government contradicts this principle, and the consequences of these failures will have significantly adverse effects on missionary work and efforts.

He reminded Westerners promoting doctrine in China that they are confronting a civilization older than their own. Everyone treasures the history of their own ethnicity and family, yet some outsiders tread upon the ideals most cherished by the Chinese, treating others from a position of superiority. He suggested that missionaries should abandon their sense of superiority to achieve their goals. Furthermore, they should seek a deeper understanding of Confucian classics, where the best approach would involve knowledgeable missionaries imparting their spiritual beliefs to capable Chinese scholars, who in turn could teach those able to make independent judgments and choose their faith. He specifically cited ancestor worship as an example, emphasizing that this respect and admiration is merely an expression of filial piety. Similarly, the reverence that Chinese people hold for Confucius arises from respect for this wise sage.Everyone has the right to hold their own views and beliefs. Proper consideration of the religious sentiments of the entire populace not only helps to create and maintain good relations between missionaries and China but also enables missionaries to assist China, while China can also assist missionaries. In this way, regardless of whether one is Confucian or Christian, and even when divided into different sects and beliefs, they can work together with a heart of love. This love transcends all beliefs and belongs to all faiths, thus making some beneficial contributions among various ethnic groups.

  1. The Issue of Missionary Work in Sino-Foreign Commercial Treaty Revisions 1902-1906

According to Article 11 of the Boxer Protocol, the Qing government agreed to revise commercial treaties with various countries. In 1902, Britain was the first to propose treaty revisions, followed by negotiations between the Qing government and countries such as the United States, Japan, Portugal, Germany, and Italy. The issue of missionary work was involved in all negotiations except with Japan. However, ultimately, no treaty was signed between China and Portugal due to differences (Li Chuanbin 2011: 112, 117).

“Sino-British Commercial Terms for Missionary Litigation” 1902: In February 1902, the Sino-British Commercial Terms for Missionary Litigation were signed, stipulating: (1) “Both Christianity and Catholicism fundamentally advocate doing good, and missionary work is permissible. Those who wish to teach and learn should be equally protected. Therefore, all missionaries entering the interior must report their names, society names, and destinations to their country’s consulate at the trading ports, so that the local magistrate can issue a protection certificate before they proceed. Local officials should protect them accordingly, and consuls should supervise them. As for those wishing to join the faith, there are no prohibitions; however, both believers and non-believers are citizens of China and must comply with Chinese laws and regulations. Missionaries must not interfere with local officials’ governance over the populace, and local officials must not distinguish between believers and non-believers. Those wishing to acquire property in the interior for building houses, nurseries, or medical facilities must specify that such property is for the use of the church, and cannot explicitly state the names of the missionaries or believers. Furthermore, church properties should follow local architectural styles and be agreed upon with neighboring notables to ensure mutual harmony, and local officials should be allowed to inspect the premises often”; (2) “Even though the signed treaty states that British citizens will be managed according to British law by British officials, British merchants cannot invoke this treaty to escape local government management, nor to disregard Chinese laws. All prohibitions applicable to Chinese citizens must also apply to British citizens, to ensure fairness. Furthermore, China is also agreeable to the proposal that, in cases of disputes between Chinese and Westerners, everything shall be handled uniformly throughout, hence plans to hire renowned lawyers familiar with Chinese law from abroad to compile legal statutes, establishing a public court at trading ports, so that after receiving Chinese approval, all cases involving Chinese and British citizens shall be handled according to law in that public court. If China has yet to appoint officials familiar with these new laws to preside over cases, or wishes to employ British lawyers to hear cases, and allowances for Chinese citizens to appeal to local officials or to this public court shall be granted” (Zhao Shuhua 2014: 67).

“Sino-American Commercial Treaty” 1903: In August 1903, Article 14 of the Sino-American Commercial Treaty stipulated: “The aim of Christianity is originally to advocate doing good, and all who wish to apply it to themselves should do likewise unto others. Those missionaries who abide by the law and proceed with training and teaching should not be subjected to bullying due to their faith. All those who abide by the teachings, regardless of being Chinese or American citizens, must abide by Chinese laws, respect officials, and coexist amicably. Should any adherents commit a crime before or after converting, they shall not be exempt from prosecution merely because they have converted. All citizens must pay all prescribed taxes, and those who have converted may not be exempted. However, no levies that oppose Christianity in the name of deity rituals or festivals may be collected from converts. Missionaries must not interfere with the governance of Chinese officials over the populace, and Chinese officials must not discriminate against either converts or non-converts, ensuring that both groups live their lives under equitable governance. American churches are permitted to rent and lease land for church property to facilitate missionary work. Upon local officials verifying the proper documentation and sealing it, missionaries may construct appropriate buildings to conduct good works” (Zhao Shuhua 2014: 68).

“Sino-Portuguese Commercial Treaty” 1904: In July 1904, Article 17 of the Sino-Portuguese Commercial Treaty stipulated: “China views missionary issues as requiring detailed deliberation to prevent past grievances from causing future conflicts. Should China and treaty-signing nations send representatives to investigate this matter, they must work diligently to devise a solution. Portugal, being responsible for the protection of its Catholic churches in China, is particularly willing to send personnel to collaboratively explore and negotiate, aiming for lasting peace between the citizens and the faith.”All who adhere to religious teachings, whether Chinese or Portuguese, should not suffer bullying or oppression due to their faith, as both converts and non-converts are citizens of China and are expected to observe Chinese laws, respect officials, and coexist harmoniously. Any adherent who commits a crime before or after converting shall not be exempt from prosecution simply because of their conversion. All Chinese citizens are obliged to pay all prescribed taxes, and converts are also not exempt from such payments. However, no levies that contradict Christianity, such as those for religious ceremonies or festivals, may be imposed on converts. Missionaries must not interfere with the governance of Chinese officials over the populace, and Chinese officials must not discriminate between converts and non-converts, ensuring that all citizens live peacefully according to the law. Portuguese churches are permitted to rent and lease land as church property for missionary work, and after local officials verify and seal the land contracts, missionaries may construct suitable buildings to carry out good works (Zhao Shuhua 2014: 68).

“Sino-German Commercial Treaty” 1905: In October 1905, Article 14 of the Sino-German Commercial Treaty stipulated: “Neither Germans nor Chinese shall be obstructed in their efforts to preach or practice their faith, and their properties must be adequately protected. German missionaries shall not interfere with Chinese officials’ governance over the populace” (Zhao Shuhua 2014: 68).

In June 1908, China and Sweden signed a new “Sino-Swedish Commercial Treaty,” with the Christian clauses being the same as those in the Sino-American Commercial Treaty (Zhao Shuhua 2014: 68; Li Chuanbin 2011: 117).

The signed treaties with Britain (including those with the United States, Portugal, Germany, and Sweden) not only retained the protective clauses for missionary activities but also added stipulations that missionaries must not interfere in litigation or local governance by Chinese officials. Moreover, they reiterated that adherents are citizens of China and should abide by Chinese laws. The concept of “harmonious coexistence between religious and non-religious citizens” reflects the hope to appropriately manage religious affairs and prevent future conflicts following the Boxer Rebellion.

  1. “Proposal for Harmonizing Civil and Religious Affairs” by the Sichuan Provincial Consultative Council 1909 (Li Chuanbin 2011: 120-121)

In 1906, the Qing court announced the “Preparatory Constitution,” and the following year, the central government established a Political Council, with provincial consultative councils being organized subsequently. By 1909, various provincial consultative councils began to form.

During its first session in 1909, the Sichuan Provincial Consultative Council proposed a “Proposal for Harmonizing Civil and Religious Affairs,” requesting members to present methods for achieving “long-term peace between civil and religious communities.” The opinions of the members at the time could be categorized into two approaches: proactive and reactive. The proactive approach focused on “revitalizing national strength and promoting education,” as “a weak nation leads to an unwise populace,” fostering “fear of religion” that could escalate into “hatred of religion.” Strengthening the nation and educating the populace could resolve issues surrounding consular jurisdiction and prevent foreign disrespect, thus ensuring that religious educators do not rely on foreigners for influence. This way, harmony between civil and religious communities could be attained. However, the proactive methods were long-term solutions and could not address immediate needs. Therefore, there were also reactive emergency measures: (1) Local officials were required to make judgments based on reason at the onset of civil-religious disputes and should not show favoritism towards either side. It was suggested to compile concise diplomatic treaties and international laws to serve as references; (2) Appointing knowledgeable local notables to advise against “discrimination against adherents”; (3) The provincial government should notify foreign consuls to inform their respective missionaries to adhere to the statutes and refrain from interfering in legal disputes.

The response from the Governor of Sichuan to these proposals was: “If the proactive measures can truly be implemented, there would be no need for civil-religious harmony; this goes without saying. The three reactive measures are largely actions that my office has already taken, such as issuing legal treaties and strictly ensuring that civil and religious matters are treated equally.”

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Review of American Missionary Policy in China 1900 (Brown 1901: 398-405)

On September 21, 1900, the American Presbyterian Board of Foreign Mission convened a meeting in New York with 17 Christian missions from North America to collectively discuss missionary policy in China after the Boxer Rebellion. Eight elders who had experienced the Boxer Uprising were invited to share their experiences. Topics discussed included: reconstruction and recovery, reparations, the relationship between missionaries and state power, and cooperation among churches.

Regarding the issue of reparations, the mission boards unanimously agreed not to make excessive demands on China. Therefore, claims should not include the suffering, loss of life, or interruptions in service experienced by missionaries (with the exception of cases where the economic support of families was lost due to the death of missionaries), but should only cover actual property damage and additional expenses incurred due to the Boxer Uprising. Furthermore, it was agreed that individual missionaries should not present claims to the authorities, but this responsibility should lie with the relevant committees of the mission boards. Additionally, the mission boards agreed that they should not attempt to interfere with the government’s policy towards China or make any political suggestions. When facing problems in the future, they should avoid engaging local Chinese officials unless absolutely necessary. The meeting aimed to strengthen cooperation among the mission boards during the restoration of work.

  1. Regulation for British Missionaries in Shandong 1901 (Li Gangji 1986: Vol. 2, Part 1, pp. 8-10)

After the Boxer Rebellion, the British Baptist Mission in Shandong drafted the “Regulation for British Missionaries in Shandong,” hoping to ease tensions between the populace and the church, and to promote mutual coexistence. The regulations consist of 25 articles and broadly cover the following aspects: (1) Clarifying the motivation for missionary work, which does not involve politics or personal interests: “Missionaries come to China, not as representatives of the state, and all expenses are covered by donations from the faithful,” “Missionaries encourage learning the faith, not for worldly gain,” “Our church engages in charitable acts such as providing relief and medical aid, guided by the principle of loving others as oneself, without the intention of garnering support”; (2) Addressing misunderstandings about church activities: “Joining the faith and being baptized is like taking a bath to renew oneself, indicating a commitment to change and leaving behind old ways, nothing more,” “Seating arrangements in church for men and women are clearly defined, and there is no mixing; church matters are to be handled according to regulations,” “The White Lotus and other sects have very secretive practices, while in our church, public worship and other activities are transparent, with no ambiguous behavior, and we do not prohibit observation”; (3) Declaring respect for Chinese customs and culture: “The teachings of ancient sages advocating filial piety and kindness are respected by both church ministers and the populace, and we advise instructors not to slander ancient teachings, nor to disrespect sages,” “In all places, missionaries have traveled and carried out their ministry; upon entering the central plains, they are to abide by sacred teachings and respect Chinese customs diligently”; (4) Issues regarding church members: “Chinese believers are still children of the nation, and should there be disputes involving taxes or other local matters, these shall be handled by local officials just like any ordinary citizen,” “If any individual has a dispute, the missionaries will not intervene unless the dispute arises directly from church matters,” “Those wishing to join the faith must first be thoroughly checked for any prior disputes and must be clear about their situation before being allowed to join, and if they cause troubles post-baptism, they will be expelled immediately”; (5) The relationship between the church and local society: “Welcoming deities, establishing temples, and other similar activities are not related to the practitioners of the faith,” “For projects like constructing bridges, roads, and public facilities, they should be handled equitably among the populace”; “When our church builds a chapel, we pay close attention to whether it poses any conflicts with the locals and will make adjustments to avoid negative impacts,” “Missionaries in the interior often live in Chinese houses; if constructing, even if slightly Western in style, they will not be built excessively tall.” It concludes: “The aforementioned regulations are to be established as principles for practitioners of the faith; those learning should adhere to them as guiding inscriptions, leaving no room for doubt or discrimination. We hope officials and citizens will understand, addressing matters rationally, maintaining the principle of equality before the emperor above, and not violating the biblical teachings of everyone performing their respective roles below. The harmony between the populace and the church, and goodwill toward one another, is what we fervently anticipate.”

  1. Discussion on Missionary Protection Clauses 1907 (Kevin 2007)

During the centennial missionary conference in 1907, discussions were held regarding the issue of protection under treaties. C. W. Mateer of the Northern Presbyterian Church observed in his report that compared to 40 years prior, the attitude of the Chinese government and people towards missionaries had become positive and friendly. However, the relationship between Christianity and the government, along with treaty issues, remains a problem that has yet to be resolved in China. Regarding how to view and evaluate the protective clauses for Christianity within the treaties, Mateer acknowledged that Protestant missionary work in China has run parallel to the process of Western countries establishing commercial treaties with China. Though Western countries employed various pressures to secure commercial privileges, he believed that religious tolerance and protection were obtained without using such pressures.The nature of war and the ensuing treaties is fundamentally one of inter-state and commercial relations, with the tolerance of Christianity being merely a collateral event. He intended to distinguish the so-called “religious protection” clauses from Western political, military pressure and commercial interests. Furthermore, he affirmed that the treaties indeed allowed for the free spread of Christianity. For reasons of self-protection, missionaries had resorted to pressure tactics at various times, but rarely purely for the protection of Chinese believers. The report demonstrated a certain awareness of the injustices in the treaty process but maintained a positive attitude towards the protective clauses for missionaries within the treaties. Another missionary from the Northern Presbyterian Church, J. C. Garritt, even suggested that these rights were obtained from the Chinese government according to God’s will.

Undoubtedly, there were divergent opinions within the missionary community regarding the treaty issues, primarily reflected in: (1) the question of whether the missionary clauses in the treaties should be retained or discarded, and whether missionaries should continue to invoke these clauses or voluntarily relinquish them entirely; (2) whether Chinese Christians should invoke treaty protections and whether missionaries should pressure Qing officials on behalf of persecuted Chinese believers. Regarding the first question, Garritt pointed out that there were two extreme positions among missionaries: one was to use the treaty provisions indiscriminately, while the other was to abandon them entirely. Dicowen acknowledged that in recent years, there had been calls suggesting that the treaties had become an obstacle to the spread of the Gospel in China, but he presented two points for discussion. First, in terms of practical outcomes, the treaties did indeed provide legal protection for missionaries and Chinese believers. Without such protection, there would likely have been more bloodshed and persecution, and the church’s success would not have come so swiftly. Whether to invoke the treaties should be left to individual believers based on their circumstances. Second, while Jesus taught non-resistance, this was primarily directed at personal vendettas and did not intend to eliminate the functions of government.

The second question was more contentious, as the missionaries’ practice of invoking treaties to protect Chinese believers led to missionaries monopolizing lawsuits and situations where believers abused privileges. Therefore, some missionaries called for this privilege to apply only to foreigners. Dicowen opposed this, stating it contradicted the brotherly love among Christians. Unless missionaries were willing to forgo treaty protections themselves, they should extend a helping hand to their suffering brethren. At the same time, the right of Chinese believers to practice their faith was also recognized and protected by the Chinese government. However, the report repeatedly emphasized that missionaries should only provide assistance in cases of religious persecution and remain vigilant against the dangers of individuals exploiting this for personal gain. The report also acknowledged that seeking treaty protection could mislead the Chinese church into relying on human power rather than God’s power, thus being only a last resort. It further pointed out that the Catholic Church used treaty privileges much more frequently than Protestant missionaries. However, the Chinese government and people often conflated them with an extreme unfairness towards Protestant missionaries, thus recommending strengthening public relations to clarify misunderstandings. Ultimately, the report stated that whether to invoke treaties should be based on whether doing so best advances the cause of Christ, allowing individual Christians to make their choices without imposing unified criteria. Overall, the tone of Dicowen’s report was one of cautious, conditional affirmation and cautious use of treaty protections.

However, there were missionaries at the meeting who expressed differing opinions. C. Bolwig of the Danish Lutheran Mission directly pointed out that treaties were, in fact, “a hated yoke imposed on the government.” He expressed great reservations about including Chinese believers within the scope of treaty protections. In practical terms, it is difficult to distinguish what qualifies as a case of religious persecution; as long as the door to treaty protection is wide open, rash interference is unavoidable. In addition to intervening in lawsuits, missionaries could also pray and provide financial relief to comfort and assist persecuted Chinese believers. Bolwig ultimately provided his own mission’s policy of refusing to represent Chinese believers in lawsuits as evidence that such practices did not affect the relationship between missionaries and believers, nor did they bring about any other disastrous consequences. D. E. Hoste of the China Inland Mission also pointed out that, in fact, missionaries faced the danger of getting involved in non-religious persecution cases regardless of how careful they were, and he suggested that missionaries cease such practices, which aligned with the policies established by Hudson Taylor for the China Inland Mission.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. The Intensification of Great Power Rivalry in the Late Qing Dynasty (Guo Tingyi 1983: 351-362)

The Boxer Movement in 1900 triggered the Eight-Nation Alliance to enter Beijing, ultimately leading the Qing court to sign the Boxer Protocol. This event fully reflects the extreme ignorance and incompetence of the Qing court. At this time, Russia took the opportunity to seize the Northeast, and after the treaty, Russian troops did not withdraw from Lüshun. Russia’s ambition to occupy the three provinces of Northeast China prompted Britain and Japan to form an alliance in 1902, aimed at maintaining the territorial integrity of China and Korea and jointly safeguarding British and Japanese interests in China and Korea. If either Britain or Japan were to go to war with another country, the other would remain neutral. If any other country aided the enemy, Britain and Japan would respond jointly. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance was clearly intended to counteract Russian expansion. In April 1902, the “Sino-Russian Treaty on Northeast China” was signed, wherein Russia agreed to restore China’s rights in the Northeast and withdraw in three phases over 18 months. However, this move seemed to be a delaying tactic, as Russia did not actually retreat but instead made new demands on China in April 1903, with Britain and Japan protesting to Russia simultaneously. During Japan’s negotiations with Russia, Russia further claimed that Korea and Manchuria were under its control. As contradictions intensified, Japan and Russia went to war in 1904, but the battlefield was in Eastern China. Despite the disparity in strength between Russia and Japan, the Japanese forces ultimately achieved victory. In September 1905, the Treaty of Portsmouth was signed between Japan and Russia in the United States, recognizing Korea as Japan’s possession and ceding Lüshun, Dalian, the South Manchuria Railway, and southern Sakhalin to Japan. In 1907, Japan and Russia signed a secret agreement that divided Southern and Northern Manchuria into spheres of influence for both countries, with Japan recognizing Russia’s special interests in Outer Mongolia, while Russia acknowledged Japan’s political relationship with Korea. The Northeast became a jointly divided sphere of influence for Japan and Russia.

After the Boxer Protocol, new commercial treaties were established between China and foreign powers, further opening up the country and facilitating foreign investment. Between 1901 and 1910, 38 additional treaty ports were opened, and the number of concessions increased. At the same time, various countries built and managed railways directly in China, with all railway projects during the Qing period being controlled by foreigners. In addition, various countries competed for mining rights in China, with foreign mining investment during the Qing period accounting for 80% of the total investment in the nation.

  1. The Reforms of the Late Qing Dynasty (Guo Tingyi 1983: 362-364, 386-393; Zhang Pengyuan 2005)

In the face of the post-Boxer crisis, Empress Dowager Cixi ordered internal and external ministers in December 1900 to supervise and implement reforms. In January 1901, under the name of Guangxu, a reform edict was formally issued. In terms of government structure, in April 1901, the Office of Government Affairs was established, and in July, the Grand Council was changed to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. In September 1903, the Ministry of Commerce was established, followed by the establishment of the Police Department in October 1905 and the Ministry of Education and Military Training Office in December. As for the content, the range of reforms was similar to the hundred days’ reform, but there were also several new initiatives, particularly concerning the relationship between Manchu and Han. In 1902, intermarriage between Manchus and Hans was permitted, and Han women were encouraged to abandon the practice of foot binding. In 1904, regulations were established allowing Han people to hold military positions, and all Banner people were to use new criminal codes. In 1905, measures were proposed to eliminate the prejudices between Manchus and Hans. Furthermore, a significant reform was the abolition of the imperial examination system that had been in place for over a thousand years, implementing a new education system. In 1906, regulations for girls’ schools were set (normal and primary schools).

In 1904, Zhang Zhidong proposed a constitutional monarchy, using Japan as a model. In July 1905, the Qing court sent five ministers abroad to investigate politics; three went to Japan, England, France, and Belgium, while the other two visited the United States, Germany, Italy, and Austria. Upon their return, the five ministers unanimously agreed on the necessity of establishing a constitution. In September 1906, a decree was issued to implement constitutional governance, starting with government reform as a preparatory foundation for the eventual establishment of a constitution, to be finalized in a few years. At this time, civic organizations supporting constitutionalism began to emerge, such as the “National Constitutional Association” established by Kang Youwei. In December, the “Preparatory Constitutional Society” was formed. By 1907, there was the “Constitutional Association.” In 1908, the framework for an Advisory Board and a member election system was established; in August, an outline of the constitution was issued, stating that a national assembly would be convened in nine years. In October 1909, advisory boards were established in various provinces. In January 1910, constitutionalists petitioned for the convening of a national assembly within a year. However, the Qing court refused, citing the lack of popular knowledge. In June, constitutionalists petitioned again and held a meeting in Beijing in August, demanding the prompt opening of the national assembly and the formation of a cabinet responsible to it. In November, the Qing court announced that the preparatory period for constitutionalism would be reduced from nine years to six years, and a legislative chamber would be established in 1913, along with the organization of a cabinet and the formulation of a constitution.

However, despite facing the Qing court’s suppression, the revolutionary movement in China was in full swing. Between 1900 and 1908, there were seven uprisings, which, although failed, did not extinguish the fire of revolution. Ultimately, the Wuchang Uprising in 1911 made the constitutional monarchy an illusion.(3) The Revolutionary Movement and Nationalism (Guo Tingyi 1983: 381-382; Zhu Hanyuan 1993: 332-336)

In 1894, Sun Yat-sen established the Xingzhonghui in Honolulu, with the aim of “expelling the Tartars, restoring China, and founding a united government.” In 1905, when he founded the Tongmenghui in Tokyo, he proposed the revolutionary program of “expelling the Tartars, restoring China, establishing a republic, and equalizing land rights.” It is clear that from the Xingzhonghui to the Tongmenghui, Sun Yat-sen consistently upheld the goal of “expelling the Tartars and restoring China,” reflecting his ethnocentric “small nationalism” (in the words of Liang Qichao). At that time, the debate between the revolutionary faction and the constitutional faction included the issue of expelling the Manchus. The constitutional faction’s “New People’s Journal” argued that the Han people still lacked the capacity to establish a country and that, in terms of political law, Han and Manchu people were equal; thus, they should integrate the various ethnic groups in the country to resist foreign enemies. They argued that insisting on expelling the Manchus would lead to revengeism and unrest. The revolutionary faction’s “Minbao” contended that expelling the Manchus was aimed at the corrupt Manchu government, asserting that once the Manchus fell, the various ethnic groups within the country could merge, and expelling the Manchus was a precondition for political revolution.

Scholars point out that during the late Qing revolutionary movement, Sun Yat-sen primarily used anti-Manchu sentiment to promote the revolution, which can be seen as a strategy to overthrow the Qing Dynasty. Therefore, the narrow racial sentiment became a characteristic of Sun Yat-sen’s internal nationalism during this stage. As for the external aspect, during the revolutionary movement, Sun Yat-sen’s nationalism criticized the great powers but did not carry a strong anti-imperialist consciousness; instead, it demanded support from the international community for China’s revolution and advocated for Sino-Japanese cooperation. However, with the establishment of the Republic of China and the achievement of the goal of overthrowing the Qing Dynasty, Sun Yat-sen’s nationalism underwent adjustments in the 1910s.
