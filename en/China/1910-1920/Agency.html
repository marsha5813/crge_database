China: Agency (1910-1920)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Cheng Ching-Yi (誠靜怡, 1881-1939), courtesy name Jingyi, was of Manchu descent and was born in Beijing. His father, Cheng Haiting (誠海亭), was a missionary with the London Missionary Society. In 1897, he graduated from the Anglo-Chinese Institute in Beijing and continued his studies at a seminary established by the London Missionary Society in Tianjin, graduating in May 1900. In 1901, Cheng Ching-Yi assisted missionary clerk George Owen (文書田) in translating the New Testament into Mandarin. In 1902, upon Owen’s return to England due to his wife’s health, he invited Cheng to continue assisting with the translation work. Cheng arrived in England in July 1903 and completed the translation work by 1908. As early as September 1906, Cheng entered the Bible Training Institute in Glasgow, Scotland, graduating in 1908. After returning to China, he served at the Dongcheng Shuangqigan Church in Beijing (later relocated to Mishida Street) and was ordained as a pastor in October 1910, succeeding the departing missionary and becoming the first Chinese pastor of the church.

In June 1910, the World Missionary Conference was held in Edinburgh, Scotland, and Cheng Ching-Yi was one of three Chinese church representatives attending the conference, where he gave a speech advocating for the establishment of a multi-denominational, fully self-supporting, unified Chinese church, leaving a lasting impression on the attendees. At the end of the conference, he was elected as a member of the Continuing Committee.

In 1913, John R. Mott (穆德), the founder and chairman of the World Student Christian Federation, visited China. This visit facilitated the establishment of the Chinese Continuation Committee, where Cheng Ching-Yi served as the secretary. Later, Cheng, along with Shi Meiyu (石美玉) and Yu Rizhang (余日章), initiated the establishment of the China National Evangelical Society, with Cheng serving as the president. In 1919, the Continuation Committee launched the “China for Christ Movement” (中華歸主運動), and Cheng Ching-Yi was appointed as the general secretary.

In 1922, the National Christian Conference was convened, with Cheng serving as the general secretary. During this period, it was decided to reorganize the Chinese Continuation Committee into the National Christian Council of China (中華全國基督教協進會). In 1924, Cheng was appointed as the general secretary of the Council. In 1927, the National Chinese Christian Association was established in Shanghai, and Cheng Ching-Yi was elected as its first president. In 1928, the International Missionary Council convened a World Missionary Conference in Jerusalem, where Cheng Ching-Yi served as one of the 20 representatives of the Chinese church and was elected as a vice-chairman of the conference. In 1933, he was appointed as the general secretary of the National Chinese Christian Association, resigning from the position of general secretary of the Council. In 1938, he again participated in the Third World Missionary Conference held in Madras, India.

To honor his contributions to the global church unity movement, Cheng was awarded the following honorary degrees: an honorary Doctor of Theology degree from Knox College in Toronto, Canada (1916); an honorary Doctor of Jurisprudence degree from The College of Wooster in Ohio, USA (1923); and an honorary Doctor of Theology degree from St. John’s University in Shanghai (1929).

On November 15, 1939, Cheng Ching-Yi passed away at the Renji Hospital in Shanghai due to uremia, at the age of 58. Observing his life, he can be said to be one of the most important Christian leaders in China in the 20th century, not only being a major promoter of the Chinese church unity movement but also representing China in the global church unity movement.

  1. An Ruci (安汝慈, 1876-1949, Ruth Paxson) was born in Manchester, Iowa, USA. She graduated from the State University of Iowa and then attended the Moody Bible Institute in Chicago.She served as an officer in the YWCA of Iowa and later became a traveling officer for the Student Volunteer Movement. In 1911, An Ruci (安汝慈) was sent to China as a missionary by the YWCA, working as a secretary for the Student Department of the Chinese Christian Women’s Association.

In 1914, the Chinese Continuation Committee organized evangelistic meetings for women in nine locations: Tianjin, Baoding, Changsha, Suzhou, Hangzhou, Fuzhou, Guangzhou, Shanghai, and Nanjing, inviting several female missionaries as speakers, with An Ruci being one of the main speakers. Her effective evangelistic abilities gained widespread recognition, allowing her to dedicate part of her time to preaching specifically to women.

In 1914 and 1915, she published articles titled “Intercession and Evangelism” and “The Promotion of Intercession” in the Chinese Recorder, emphasizing that the foundation and results of evangelism are rooted in prayer, advocating that both believers and missionaries should participate in intercessory work. In 1918, the YWCA published an English and Chinese translated version of her book “Prayer and Evangelism.”

An Ruci actively participated in revival evangelism, becoming a prominent female revivalist in China in the early 20th century. She was later invited to the United Kingdom to lead the Keswick Convention and served as the honorary general secretary of the National Evangelical Society of China. Her thoughts were greatly influenced by the revival and holiness movements, advocating for the pursuit of the Fullness of Christ, becoming a role model for many Chinese female evangelists.

In order to nurture missionaries from various organizations in China and help them deepen their spiritual lives, she established a place of retreat in North China specifically for missionaries. She also offered three courses in biblical correspondence, using materials translated from the Biola Bible Institute of LA. In 1931, she left China for the United States, transferring the courses to the newly established Shanghai Jiangwan Bible Prayer Center (renamed the National Theological Institute in 1938), where she was also a founding board member.

In 1929, An Ruci was invited to serve as a guest speaker on “Revival Preaching” at the Canton-Hong Kong Spiritual Conference, delivering her sermon titled “The Highest Life,” making her the first female speaker at the event. In 1930, the Canton Christian Association published her book “The Highest Life.” In 1938, they published another of her works, “Rivers of Living Water.”

She spent her later years living in Massachusetts, USA, and passed away in 1949 at the age of 73.

  1. Yu Rizhanzhang (余日章, David Z. T. Yui, 1882-1936) was born into a Christian family in Wuchang, Hubei. His father, Yu Wenqing (余文卿), was a pastor in the Chinese Anglican Church. During his childhood, he attended both a missionary school and a church primary school. At the age of 13, he entered Boone School, an Anglican institution. In 1900, due to the Boxer Rebellion, Yu Wenqing moved the family to Shanghai for safety, and Yu Rizhanzhang transferred to St. John’s College in Shanghai. After the turmoil, his father returned to Wuchang, but Yu Rizhanzhang continued his studies at St. John’s. He graduated from St. John’s in 1902 and successfully entered the university department (正館) of the school, graduating with excellent results in 1905. After graduation, he taught at Wuchang Wenhua College. In 1908, he went to the United States to pursue a master’s degree in education at Harvard University, during which he and his classmates established the Chinese Student Christian Association in the United States. He planned to serve as a “traveling officer” for the association after graduation, but upon receiving news of his brother Yu Rixuan’s (余日宣) serious illness, he resigned and returned to China.

Upon his return in 1910, Bishop Logan Roots of the Anglican Church hired him as the principal of the Guanghua University Affiliated High School, but after the outbreak of the Xinhai Revolution, classes were temporarily suspended. Yu Rizhanzhang was then invited by friends in the revolutionary faction to assist with diplomatic work for the military government of Li Yuanhong (黎元洪).In August 1912, the Minister of Education Cai Yuanpei (蔡元培) convened the first National Education Conference in Beijing, and Yu Rizhanzhang (余日章) was invited to attend as an education expert. After the conference, he remained in Beijing to serve as an editor for the English Peking Gazette (《北京日報》) for about four months. In January 1913, at the invitation of Wang Zhengting (王正廷), then vice general secretary of the National Young Men’s Christian Association (renamed the National Association in 1915), he took on the role of director of the Lecture Department, marking the beginning of his service in the association.

After assuming the role of director of the Lecture Department, Yu expanded its scope to include agricultural and forestry, health, and graphic sciences, hoping to enlighten audiences through scientific lectures and Christian influence. He strongly believed that the weakness of the nation was due to the lack of educational development. Under his planning, the Lecture Department held hundreds of lectures annually, reaching audiences of hundreds of thousands.

In 1915, when the general secretary of the association, Fletcher S. Brockman (巴樂滿), returned to America, Wang Zhengting took over his position. The vacancy for vice general secretary was then filled by Yu. Shortly afterward, Wang resigned due to his duties as vice president of the Senate, and Yu acted in his stead. In 1917, Yu Rizhanzhang was formally appointed as the general secretary and would lead the association for the rest of his life.

As the general secretary of the National Association, Yu dedicated himself to promoting movements such as civic education, personal salvation, and public citizenship education. The civic education movement originated from the Chinese Young Men’s Christian Association during the European War and focused on literacy education for Chinese laborers in North America. To address China’s illiteracy problem, Yu invited Yan Yangchu (晏陽初) and Fu Ruoyu (傅若愚) to establish a section for civic education in the education department of the association. From 1922 onwards, they began pilot literacy education programs in various locations, paving the way for the future civic education movement in China.

The concept of personal salvation, advocated by the YMCA, was further developed by Yu Rizhanzhang. In 1921, he served as a national representative of the National Foreign Affairs Support Committee, traveling to the United States with Jiang Menglin (蔣夢麟) to supervise negotiations regarding the Beijing government’s involvement in the Washington Conference. Afterward, he articulated the idea of “personal salvation” at the YMCA, stating that only with a complete national character could a strong nation exist. In 1923, at the national conference, he called for “personal salvation” to drive the “civic education movement,” hoping to cultivate public responsibility toward family and society while equipping citizens to supervise the government.

During the anti-Christian movement, the YMCA faced various attacks and accusations, and Yu actively responded to the May 30 Incident, advocating for an investigation into the truth and demanding justice and compensation, as well as increased representation for Chinese members on the board of the Municipal Council. He also unequivocally supported the abolition of unequal treaties, the implementation of tariff autonomy, and the abolishment of consular jurisdiction. At the same time, he asserted that missionaries should no longer rely on political clauses for their protection. Regarding the relationship between the Chinese church and missionaries, Yu acknowledged the former contributions of missionaries while candidly pointing out their issues. He hoped that Chinese Christians could assume greater responsibility and leadership, working equally with Western partners in church activities. He actively supported the localization of the YMCA, and by 1926, most city associations had Chinese general secretaries.

In addition to his work with the YMCA, Yu Rizhanzhang was also elected as the vice president of the Chinese Continuation Committee in 1917. When the National Christian Council of China was established in 1922, he was elected as its first president, overseeing operations until 1928.

In May 1923, due to heart problems, Yu took a six-month leave from the national association. By the end of the year, his health prompted him to resign, but the board was unable to find a suitable successor and suggested he take a vacation in the United States for recovery. He left for the U.S. in March 1924 and returned in January 1925. In 1930, due to stress and a recurrence of heart issues, the association granted him another six-month leave, with the work temporarily handled by deputy general secretary Liang Xiaochu (梁小初).

After the “September 18 Incident” in 1931, Yu grew increasingly concerned about the situation. After the “January 28 Incident,” with Shanghai threatened by warfare, he traveled to the Philippines for a month of rest. He returned via Hong Kong and Guangzhou to Shanghai, then went to Qingdao for the summer. In June of that year, after attending the sixth national conference of the YMCA in Beijing, he temporarily stayed in Nanjing and Hankou before returning to Shanghai. At this point, feeling more stable health-wise, he decided to travel to the United States, hoping to help Americans understand the situation in China. He departed in September 1932, arriving in San Francisco in October, and subsequently traveled to Chicago, Washington, and New York. In January 1933, he was hospitalized due to a cerebral hemorrhage and spent time recovering in the U.S. until September 1933, when he returned to Shanghai. After returning, he submitted his resignation, but the board hoped he would retain his position as general secretary. In December 1934, Yu insisted on resigning, stating he could no longer fulfill the duties of the association. Unable to retain him, the board decided to grant him the title of General Secretary Emeritus in January 1935, with Liang Xiaochu succeeding him.On January 22, 1936, Yu Rizhanzhang (余日章) passed away at the age of 54.

  1. Wei Baoluo (魏保羅, 1877-1919), originally named Wei Enbo (魏恩波), was born in Baoding Prefecture, Zhili, and was unable to attend school in his youth. In 1895, he went to Beijing to learn the paper business and later opened “Enxin Yong Cloth Store” in Dongchahu Hutong outside Chongwenmen in Beijing. Around 1902, influenced by a believer named Chen Xinsan (陳新三), he began to encounter Christianity at the London Missionary Society church (formerly Dong Liushu Jing Church) in Chongwenmen. He was baptized by British missionary Samuel Evans Meech (宓治文) at the Shuangqigan London Missionary Society. Shortly thereafter, Wei was influenced by the ideas of church autonomy and initiated a self-supporting conference at the church in Dongchahu, inviting various guild leaders to attend, selling his property to contribute to the church, and actively fundraising to support the establishment of self-governing churches.

In 1908, he was expelled from the church for committing adultery. Several years later, he heard a voice saying, “The Lord still needs you; they do not want you, but the Lord wants you, just like the prodigal son.” Thus, Wei started to preach spontaneously throughout Beijing. However, he still could not escape the grasp of sin.

In 1915, Arthur Clifford Selmon (施列民), a pastor from the Shanghai Seventh-day Adventist Church, and Su Dianqing (蘇殿卿) came to Beijing, where Wei discussed the doctrine of the Sabbath with them, subsequently accepting the teaching of observing Saturday as the Sabbath. In 1916, Wei was introduced to the Pentecostal movement that had come from the United States. That year, he was seriously ill with chest pain and had been coughing for over five months; traditional medicine showed no improvement. In August, at his cloth store, he met Elder Xin Shengmin of the Faith Church, who advised him to stop taking medication and prayed for him by anointing him with oil, resulting in his recovery. Wei Enbo and his wife were subsequently baptized by the Faith Church (presumably by immersion, as the London Missionary Society practiced sprinkling), marking the start of his connection with the Pentecostal movement. Through Xin, he met missionary Bernt Berntsen (賁德新), from whom Wei learned many Pentecostal doctrines that significantly influenced the later establishment of the True Jesus Church.

In March 1917, Wei claimed to have been led by the Holy Spirit to the Dahong River outside Yongdingmen, where he was baptized facing downwards, and simultaneously stated that God commanded him to perform a 39-day fast. During this period, he reported seeing visions of Jesus, Moses, and Elijah, as well as the twelve apostles, and claimed to hear Jesus instructing him to “correct the church,” revealing the “erroneous” aspects of contemporary churches to him. After receiving this revelation, he began to part ways with the Faith Church and to practice his earlier enthusiasm for church autonomy. That same year, he promoted a self-governing movement under the name “Universal Reformation Church,” later naming his organization the True Jesus Church, meaning the “true” church of Jesus.

Initially, there was no fixed or formal church name, with variations such as “Jesus Church,” “Reformation New Protestant True Jesus Church,” “Reformation Jesus Church,” and “Reformation New Church.” Regardless, its clear orientation was to transform the “false” churches into the “true” Jesus Church.

From September 1917 onward, Wei Baoluo traveled numerous times to Hebei, Tianjin, and Shandong to preach, documenting his experiences in his journal titled “The True Testimony of the Holy Spirit” (also known as “The True Testimony Book”).

In 1918, Zhang Lingsheng and Zhang Banaba, who had earlier been in contact with Berntsen and the Pentecostal movement, united with Wei Baoluo. In February 1919, they published the “Universal Reformation Church Report” in Beijing.

After the passing of Wei Baoluo in 1919, his son Wei Yisa (魏以撒) along with Zhang Lingsheng, Liang Qinming, and Gao Daling managed the church affairs.

  1. Ding Li-Mei (丁立美, 1871-1936) was born in Jiaozhou, Shandong, into a Christian family. In his youth, he attended a private school in his hometown and received education at Tengchow College from 1883 to 1893. After graduation, he initially served as a superintendent at a church school and then returned to Tengchow College as a teacher from 1895 to 1896. Between 1896 and 1898, he received apprenticeship-style theological training in Yancheng and Weixian. After graduating, he served as a pastor in Yexian at the Presbyterian Church. During the Boxer Rebellion in 1900, due to the anti-Christian attitude of the Shandong governor Yu Xian (毓賢), he was temporarily arrested by the authorities. After Yuan Shikai (袁世凱) took office, with assistance from Watson M. Hayes of Tengchow College, he was released after over 40 days in prison.After his release, Ding Li-Mei (丁立美) became even more enthusiastic about church service, preaching in the Rudong area (Yuan Zhuang, Shigou, Qingdao). In March 1909, he was invited to return to Guangwen Academy to lead a revival meeting, encouraging students to commit to work for the Lord, resulting in nearly a hundred students dedicating themselves to preaching. Later, he was invited by the YMCA to preach in Tianjin, Beijing, and Tongzhou, inspiring over 200 students to dedicate themselves to preaching.

In April 1909, Ding initiated the “Student Commitment Evangelism Movement” at the “North China Youth Third Summer Conference” held at Hehe University in Tongzhou. In May 1910, with the support of the National YMCA, it was formally named the “Chinese Student Volunteer Evangelistic Team” (later renamed the “Xuexing Commitment Evangelistic Team”), with Ding appointed as the circuit secretary. Under Ding’s leadership, by 1925, 93 branches had been established nationwide. In the statistical report of 1924, 145 members had become missionaries. During this period, Ding traveled extensively throughout the country and even went to Japan and Korea, earning the reputation of the “Moody of China.”

In 1918, the Yunnan branch of the Chinese National Evangelical Association was established, planning to develop evangelistic work in Yunnan. In 1919, Ding participated in their survey team and during a year-long investigation in Yunnan, he held numerous revival and evangelistic meetings.

In 1923, at the invitation of Hayes (赫士), he taught the Bible at North China Theological College in Teng County, Shandong. In September 1932, he assisted in the founding of Tianjin Bible College with the Tongshenghui and served as vice president. While engaging in theological education, he continued to lead revival meetings. In early 1936, Ding went to Qingdao to plan the Lingguang Institute, aimed at nurturing the spiritual lives of Christians. He passed away on September 22, 1936, due to illness and was buried in Tianjin.

  1. Hayes (赫士, Watson M. Hayes, 1857-1944) was born in Pennsylvania, USA. After graduating from Allegheny College, he attended Western Seminary in Pittsburgh. He was a staunch conservative Presbyterian believer. On August 15, 1882, Hayes was ordained as a pastor and, in November of the same year, sent by the Presbyterian Church of the United States of America (PCUSA) to Shandong, China, where he taught astronomy, geology, finance, and mathematics at Wenhua Institute, founded by Calvin Mateer (狄考文). In 1896, Hayes succeeded Mateer as the head of the institute and established Shandong’s first newspaper, the Shandong Times (《山東時報》). To meet educational needs, he translated and authored several modern scientific publications, including log tables, “Acoustics: An Introduction” (1898), “Thermodynamics: An Introduction” (1897), and “Optics: An Introduction” (1898), paving the way for modern natural sciences in China.

In 1900, with the outbreak of the Boxer Rebellion, the Wenhua Institute was forced to suspend operations. In 1901, after the Qing government issued edicts for reforms and encouraged universities in each province, Hayes, at the invitation of the then Shandong governor Yuan Shikai (袁世凱), led a team from Wenhua Institute to Jinan to participate in the establishment of Shandong University, where he served as the chief instructor. He implemented a Sunday holiday policy at Shandong University, which was pioneering for government-run schools at the time. In 1901, Empress Dowager Cixi awarded Yuan Shikai and Hayes for their efforts and commissioned Hayes to draft a national educational plan, policies, and various regulations (including a Sunday holiday similar to practices in other countries). Later, Zhou Bolin (張伯熙), Zhang Zhidong (張之洞), and Rong Qing (榮慶) revised the new rules, renaming provincial universities as higher learning institutions and changing Sunday holidays to correspond with the birthdays of the Empress Dowager, Emperor, and Empress, additionally requiring students to attend ceremonies honoring Confucius on the first and fifteenth days of each month. This caused Hayes discontent, as he insisted on “kneeling only to the true God” and he left the school in protest.

In 1902, the Wenhua Institute relocated to Weixian and merged with the British Baptist Society schools in Shandong to form three colleges: a College of Arts, a College of Theology, and a College of Medicine. After resigning from the chief instructor position, he began teaching at the theological college. In 1909, it was officially named Shandong Christian University (Qi Lu University). In 1917, it moved to a new campus in Jinan.

In 1916, the president of Qi Lu University’s theological college, J. Percy Bruce (卜道成), a missionary from the British Baptist Society, was appointed as the president of the university.However, this was met with opposition from the Shandong District of the Presbyterian Church in the United States, leading to the resignation of several board members and faculty from the church. The directors of the College of Arts and the College of Medicine, along with Hayes, the acting president of the Theological College, unanimously requested President Bu to resign. Meanwhile, the more conservative theological stance of the Presbyterian Church in the United States was dissatisfied with the more open theological ideas of the faculty from other denominations at the theological college. The Shandong Presbyterian Church believed that Hayes could understand and represent their views, which further deteriorated the relationship between Hayes and President Bu. In 1919, the board finally demanded the resignations of the president, the dean of the College of Arts, and Hayes; 18 Presbyterian theological students decided to withdraw from their studies, and the Shandong Presbyterian Church also decided to withdraw from the theological college.

In November 1919, the Jinan District decided that “the Presbyterian Church must establish its own seminary in Shandong that respects the Bible,” inviting Hayes and Albert Dodd (道雅伯), both missionaries from the Presbyterian Church in the United States, to teach. In December, Chinese pastors and missionaries from four districts established a provisional board of directors. The seminary was named “Shandong Theological Seminary” (later renamed “North China Theological Seminary”), with Hayes serving as the president. In 1922, the North China Theological Seminary moved to Teng County, Shandong, becoming the most influential fundamentalist theological seminary in northern China. By 1927, the seminary had 186 students. Hayes was extremely dissatisfied with the liberalism and social gospel tendencies in missionary work, believing that an excessive emphasis on social reform and service, while neglecting evangelism, was very dangerous. A publication from the 1930s referred to Hayes stating, “Dr. Hayes is the strongest exponent of fundamentalist theology in China to date. He is aligned doctrinally with Patton and Machen from Princeton, and he does for the conservative church in China what those American scholars do in the United States.”

In terms of theological literature, Hayes published “Lectures on the Life of Jesus” as early as 1912. He later edited works including: “Commentary on Hebrews” (1925), “Commentary on Ephesians” (1925), “Systematic Theology” (1930), “Lectures on the Life of Jesus” (1932), “Church History” (1936), “Commentary on the Johannine Epistles” (1936), “Pastoral Theology,” “Ethics,” “Commentary on Romans” (1939), “Rules for Evangelism” (1939), “Harmonization of the Four Gospels,” and “Lectures on the Acts of the Apostles.”

After the outbreak of the Pacific War in 1941, the North China Theological Seminary was forced to suspend classes. In March 1942, Hayes and his wife were imprisoned in a detention camp in Weixian. Although an agreement had been reached between the U.S. and Japan regarding the exchange of prisoners of war, Hayes, by then elderly (87 years old) and suffering from heart disease and diabetes, chose to give the opportunity to younger individuals. On April 2, 1944, Hayes died in the Weixian concentration camp. He served in China for 62 years, making significant contributions, especially in theological education.

  1. Xu Qian (徐謙, Ch’ien Hs, 1871-1940), courtesy name Jilong (季龍), Christian name George, and later signed as Huangshan Qiaoke (黃山樵客) in his later years, was originally from a prominent family in She County, Anhui. He came from a scholarly background and in 1903, he ranked eighth among the second group of scholars in the imperial examination in Beijing, subsequently entering the Hanlin Academy to study translation. In 1907, he was appointed as an editor at the Hanlin Academy and held various positions, such as a consultant in the Ministry of Justice, chief of the Beijing Judicial Hall, and chief prosecutor of the Beijing Higher Court. In 1910, he traveled to the United States to attend the eighth International Prison Conference while investigating judicial systems in Europe and America. When he returned to China in 1911, the Wuchang Uprising had already occurred, and he resigned from all his official positions, advocating for the abdication of the Qing Emperor. In March 1912, along with Xu Shiying (許世英) and Xu Xun (徐巽), he established the National Progress Association in Beijing, advocating a “federal structure with a provincial spirit for China,” with Wu Tingfang as president and Wang Chonghui as vice president. In August 1912, the National Progress Association, the Chinese National Alliance, the United Republican Party, and the National Public Party merged to form the Kuomintang (Nationalist Party).

His relationship with Christianity can be traced back to the late Qing Dynasty when he came into contact with the Anglican Church in Beijing due to a family illness. However, at that time, he viewed Christianity merely as a religion that promoted goodness and considered Jesus as nothing more than a “saint” from the West. His perception of Christianity changed unexpectedly during his investigative trip to Europe and America. Upon returning to China, he began attending Anglican Church gatherings in Beijing, and his family was baptized in 1913, although he remained tentative.

After the failure of the “Second Revolution” in August 1913, Yuan Shikai (袁世凱) plotted to restore the monarchy, during which Xu had retreated from politics and was working as a lawyer in Shanghai.Although Xu Qian (徐謙) participated in various church gatherings in Shanghai, he still refused to accept baptism, famously stating, “I pray to God that if Yuan Shikai (袁世凱) dies, I will believe in Christianity.” In June 1916, following the failure of Yuan’s imperial attempt, he passed away. The day after Yuan’s death, Xu Qian was baptized.

With Yuan Shikai’s death, Li Yuanhong (黎元洪) succeeded him as President, and the National Assembly reconvened. At that time, the Confucian Church was actively promoting Confucianism as the national religion. In November 1916, Xu, serving as Deputy Minister of Justice in Duan Qirui’s (段祺瑞) cabinet, initiated the “Religious Freedom Association” and was elected as its president. Under Xu’s leadership, the association achieved a breakthrough by rallying support from various religious groups, including Catholicism, Orthodoxy, Islam, and Buddhism, to collectively advocate for religious freedom. In June 1917, after Li Yuanhong dissolved the National Assembly, Xu angrily resigned from his post and traveled to Guangzhou to participate in Sun Yat-sen’s (孫中山) constitutional protection movement, serving as the secretary-general of the Supreme Military Headquarters. The following September, Xu was appointed Minister of Justice by the government council. During Sun Yat-sen’s stay in Guangdong, Xu organized Bible study classes within the Supreme Military Headquarters to promote Christian teachings and advocated for the concept of Christian nationalism.

In July 1918, Xu traveled from Guangzhou to Shanghai to promote his ideas of Christian nationalism among colleagues at the Shanghai Christian Evangelization Association. After the May Fourth Movement in 1919, Xu became increasingly convinced that only Christianity could save the nation. In January 1920, the Shanghai Christian National Salvation Conference officially held its founding meeting at Moor Hall. Between 1920 and 1922, the conference actively sponsored speeches and issued declarations responding to the current situation.

In September 1922, Xu accepted an invitation from Wang Chonghui (王寵惠) to serve as the Minister of Justice in the Beijing government’s “Good Man’s Cabinet”. However, the cabinet was dissolved after just one month, leading Xu to withdraw from the political scene. In August 1923, he returned to Guangzhou to become the head of the Department of Literature at Lingnan University. In January 1924, with the Chinese Nationalist Party (Kuomintang) establishing its “Alliance with Russia, Accommodation with the Communists” policy, Sun Yat-sen invited Xu to join the reorganized Kuomintang, where he became active in Chinese politics again. Sun Yat-sen passed away in March 1925 in Beijing. In July, the National Government was established in Guangzhou, and Xu was elected as a government commissioner and concurrently served as the president of the Supreme Court and the administration of justice. In January 1926, he was elected as a member of the Central Executive Committee of the Kuomintang at its second national congress and served as the chairman of the Beijing branch. Xu Qian’s “leftist” stance within the Kuomintang caused increasing tension with Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石), although he did not align with the Communist Party. As the Kuomintang purged the party of leftists, Xu declared his withdrawal from the political scene in 1927.

It can be said that between 1924 and 1927, Xu Qian had set aside his Christian nationalism and was actively involved in revolutionary politics. At the same time, in the face of anti-imperialist sentiments targeting Christianity, Xu emphasized that, as a tool of imperialism in China, Christianity must be overthrown; on the other hand, he was convinced that a revolutionary Christianity was not in conflict with national revolution. He continued to seek a new relationship between Christianity and anti-imperialism, as well as the national revolution.

After announcing his withdrawal from political affairs, Xu Qian lived in seclusion in the French Concession of Shanghai. After the September 18 Incident in 1931, he began working for anti-Japanese and national salvation activities. In the winter of 1938, he went to Guangdong and Guangxi to inspect and assist in mobilization efforts, but due to health issues, he went to Hong Kong for treatment. With a large influx of refugees into Hong Kong, Xu decided to establish a relief center to care for and educate homeless children. In July 1940, as the situation in Hong Kong became dire, Xu faced a heart attack while planning for the safety of the children in the relief center. He ultimately passed away on September 26. Looking back over Xu Qian’s life, he not only participated actively as a revolutionary in modern China’s revolutionary movements but also worked diligently to integrate the future of the nation with religious faith as a Christian.

  1. Fu Nengren (富能仁, James O. Fraser, 1886-1938), was born in London, England, and was a missionary with the China Inland Mission, originally from the Methodist Church.

After graduating from the engineering department of the Royal Institute in London, he met Charles Thomas Studd (施達德), one of the “Cambridge Seven,” at a Christian camp in 1906. Deeply moved by the mission work of the China Inland Mission, he decided to join the organization.

Fu arrived in Shanghai in November 1908, where he received Chinese training at the Anqing Language School. In May 1909, he entered Yunnan via Hong Kong and Burma, eventually arriving at Tengyue, located in the southwest border area of Yunnan, where he established a new mission station. However, his initial missionary work was not smooth. By May 1910, while visiting a flower Lisu village in the northern part of the Nujiang region, he discovered that the local indigenous people were attentive listeners, which left a deep impression on him.

In 1912, Fu Nengren established a mission station in Tengyue.In early 1913, Fu Nengren (富能仁) visited Lisu villages with a new colleague, Carl G. Gowman, and received positive responses. In April of the same year, he baptized three Han individuals in Tengyue. In a missionary report from 1913, Fu noted that the lowland areas of Yunnan were primarily inhabited by Han people, while the mountainous regions were populated by various indigenous groups, including the Miao, Lisu, Yi, Dai, Blang, and Jingpo. Although missionaries had already begun evangelizing among the indigenous peoples in eastern Yunnan, northern Guizhou, and Sichuan Province, there was still no mission work in the western region of Yunnan, particularly around Tengyue, which was primarily home to the Jingpo, Lisu, and Dai people. He expressed his feelings, stating, “Most of the indigenous people we come into contact with are quite friendly to outsiders. They are simple, superstitious about various spirits, yet they do not know the Gospel. What a great pity! Unless the Christian church takes responsibility, when will the Gospel ever reach them? Various ethnic groups are densely populated throughout Yunnan, and from Marco Polo’s time until now, they remain bound in darkness.” He then analyzed the situation in Tengyue: the Dai people lived in the plains, governed by their own chieftains, and in both Myanmar and Laos, missionaries were already working among them, but none had yet come to Yunnan. The Jingpo people were distributed along the China-Myanmar border and were viewed as “savages,” with missionaries also engaged in work across the border in Myanmar. The Lisu people, on the other hand, were divided into Flower Lisu and Black Lisu, with the latter residing in deep valleys upstream of the Nujiang River.

From October to December 1915, Fu Nengren published an article titled “Some Observations on Mission Work in China” in the China Inland Mission periodical “China’s Millions” (“億萬華民”), analyzing the reasons why Han people found it hard to accept Christianity and the opportunities for evangelizing indigenous peoples. He noted that, overall, Chinese people showed little interest in the Gospel, while conversely, the ethnic groups in western Yunnan were fervently seeking it. He claimed that Chinese people struggled to understand true “altruism,” leading them to suspect that missionaries had ulterior motives. They believed that the Gospel was merely about encouraging goodness, making it extremely challenging to persuade them to abandon idolatry. Moreover, many Chinese viewed Christianity as a “foreign religion” and expected to gain some benefits from it. This article reflected his disappointment with missionary work among the Han, but he saw hope in the outreach to the Lisu.

In 1916, with the approval of CIM General Director Dixon E. Hoste (何斯德), Fu Nengren entered the Northwestern mountain areas to establish an evangelistic station for the approximately 12,000 Lisu people there. By 1917, he had led 800 individuals to faith and begun translating the Bible into Lisu. At that time, a Karen missionary named Ba Thaw (巴拖) from Myanmar was also evangelizing among the Lisu. He recorded the Lisu language using modified Latin letters, and Fu collaborated with him to create a written script for the Lisu people. The script they invented was called “Fraser Script” (富能仁文字) or “Old Lisu Script.” Together, they translated the Gospel of Mark into Lisu, and within three months, in addition to completing the Gospel of Mark, they finished the “Basic Faith Catechism,” hymnbooks, children’s prayer books, and a small dictionary.

In January 1920, he established new evangelistic works in three Lisu villages—Hsiangtah (向塔), Tahsiaoho (塔曉壕), Luhkenho (陸墾壕), and Hsinchaiho (新柴壕)—in the southern part of Tengyue’s Longling region. In 1921, Fu traveled further south from Longling to the China-Myanmar border, presenting the Gospel to the Lisu people along the Nujiang River, where he established 7 to 8 chapels.

In November 1922, Fu Nengren returned to England for a reporting trip and returned to Shanghai on September 12, 1924. At that time, Hoste arranged for him to serve as the principal of a China Inland Mission school in Lanzhou, Gansu. During the following two years, Fu not only managed the school but also trained believers and preachers. In early 1928, Fu was appointed as the supervisor of Yunnan Province, responsible for missionary work throughout the province. In his 1931 report, Fu noted that the number of baptized Christians in the China Inland Mission had reached over 8,000 by that year. In 1932, the mission opened 8 additional mission stations in Yunnan Province and added 12 new missionaries, bringing the total number of nearly 100 missionaries in the province, with half belonging to the China Inland Mission.

In January 1934, Fu and his family returned to England to report on their work and shared about their mission in Yunnan. During this time, he also wrote an article for “China’s Millions,” introducing the tribal groups in the border areas of Myanmar, noting that there were approximately 40,000 to 50,000 indigenous people from various tribes and that the China Inland Mission had begun work in those border districts.In March 1935, Fu Nengren (富能仁) returned to China and, by 1936, he proofread the newly translated Lisu New Testament, which was officially published in 1938.

On September 25, 1938, Fu Nengren passed away in Yongchang due to malaria.

  2. The Chinese Student Volunteer Movement for the Ministry, 1910 (Jiang Changchuan 江長川 1914: 43-44; Xu Yihua 徐以驊 2010: 204-222)

In the overseas missionary movement in Europe and America, the Student Volunteer Movement played an important role. In early 20th century China, a similar movement emerged calling on young students to dedicate themselves to the ministry, known as the “Chinese Student Volunteer Movement.” The initiator of this movement was Pastor Ding Limei (丁立美), who graduated from Shandong Dengzhou Wenhuiguan in 1900. In 1909, he was invited by Henry W. Luce (路思義), the vice principal of Guangwen School in Weixian, Shandong, to lead a three-day revival meeting, resulting in 104 students responding to the call to ministry.

In May 1910, officially supported by the Chinese Christian YMCA (which later became the National Association), the “Chinese Student Volunteer Evangelistic Corps” was established, affiliated with the student department of the YMCA. Ding Limei was appointed as the first marching director, promoting the student volunteer movement across schools nationwide. Its motto, “The Evangelization of Our Mother Country and the World in this Generation,” was inspired by the SVM’s motto, “The Evangelization of the World in this Generation.” In December 1912, during a national YMCA executive meeting held in Beijing, it was renamed the “Chinese Student Volunteer Movement for the Ministry.”

After the establishment of the corps, Ding Limei and other members began to evangelize across various regions, organizing branches or teams. By the end of 1916, 52 branches had been established in universities and colleges across the nation (including St. John’s University, Hujiang University, Soochow University, Zhejiang University, Jinling University, Fuzhou Yinghua College, and Guangzhou Peiying School), with 130 members feeling called to the ministry, 232 members entering seminary, and 11 members studying theology abroad. By 1921, the corps had recruited over 1,500 members and established more than 70 branches.In 1922, the Evangelistic Corps held its first national conference at Guling, Lushan. During the meeting, there were two differing interpretations regarding the scope of membership: one view suggested recruiting those committed to church work (education, healthcare, social services), while the other specifically referred to the work of pastors and evangelists. Since its establishment, the Corps had implemented a narrow interpretation of member recruitment policy, but this understanding began to be challenged in the 1920s, especially influenced by the “new concept of evangelism,” which argued that “to make the church the center of societal transformation and fundamentally seek the equitable development of the Chinese church, it is essential to expand the concept of evangelism.” After discussions, the Corps reaffirmed that members had the freedom to choose but still recognized pastoral work as the highest calling. In 1926, the Corps held its second national conference in Jinan, Shandong, discussing its mission under the new circumstances. Most participants advocated for evangelism not only as a means of conversion but also as a testimony. Previously, the emphasis was on “words over actions,” viewing healthcare, education, social services, and literature merely as ancillary to evangelism. However, the new situation called for an expanded scope of evangelism, even moving away from seeing evangelism as a profession, encouraging young students to commit to serving in various church endeavors. Thus, the aim became to turn the religion spoken about by the church into a practiced religion and to manifest religion in various Christian enterprises, leading to a name change for the Corps to “Chinese Christian Student Volunteer Testimony Corps,” along with new charter provisions.

In July 1927, the China Christian Young Men’s and Young Women’s Association and the Student Volunteer Testimony Corps jointly elected representatives to establish the “China Christian Student Movement Preparatory Committee” at Jinling Women’s University in Nanjing. After the transformation from an Evangelistic Corps to a Testimony Corps, it lost its original function, but the broad scope of the new mission made the work of the Testimony Corps lose its focus. With the development of the China Christian Student Movement, the Testimony Corps gradually faded away.

  1. China Sunday School Union, 1910 (Du Chunpu 都春圃 1914: 44-48; Chen Zhongling 陳中陵 2019)

In 1907, the Centennial Missionary Conference held in Shanghai decided to establish a Sunday school organization to develop a national Sunday school movement. Three years later, in 1910, the China Sunday School Union was officially founded in Shanghai, with its first general secretary being Du Chunpu (Elwood G. Tewksbury), a missionary from the American Congregational Church who came to China in 1890. Du emphasized the authority of the Bible alone in faith and believed that the purpose of the Union’s existence was to advocate for Bible study. Under this belief, the Union’s main work was divided into four departments: literature, education, evangelism, and promotion. The literature department was responsible for publishing Sunday school teaching materials, teacher training textbooks, Bible illustrations, and monthly publications; the education department organized various teacher training and correspondence courses; the evangelism department promoted personal evangelism both within and outside the church; and the promotion department conducted Bible study outreach activities.

The Sunday school curriculum materials published by the Union could be categorized as “unified curriculum” and “graded curriculum.” The “unified curriculum” was adopted by various Sunday school classes at all levels using the same curriculum goals and Bible passages, with only slight adaptations in teaching content and methods based on age. The “graded curriculum,” on the other hand, tailored materials to meet the needs of students in different grade levels. According to statistics, in the first four years of its operation, more than 90,000 children used this curriculum for Sunday school every Sunday, widely adopted by various denominational churches.

The Union was a part of the global Sunday school movement. Initially, the World Sunday School Association provided major financial support through its office in the UK.

In the 1920s, theological disputes between fundamentalists and liberals also impacted the Union’s work. Du did not reject new educational methods but refused to incorporate discussions on social ethics into the Union’s curriculum, a stance that drew criticism from liberal missionaries but garnered support from fundamentalist missionaries such as those from the China Inland Mission. In January 1930, the World Sunday School Association ceased part of its financial aid to the Union, including funding for Du’s salary. Although the Union lost significant foreign funding, it decided to continue its Sunday school work, with the committee supporting Du and requesting him to remain in his position. In fact, the association did not completely terminate its financial support for the Union, still covering expenses for Sunday school materials. However, at this point, the Union needed to increase its external fundraising efforts and seek financial support from conservative missionary organizations and church institutions.News of the situation reached overseas, and The Sunday School Times praised the Union for its “faithful and courageous actions.” Even the two Chinese missions of the Southern Presbyterian Church called on the Union to rectify the situation; otherwise, “we should urge our supporters to redirect their donations for Sunday school work in China directly to the China Sunday School Union in Shanghai.”

When the Pacific War broke out in 1941, British and American missionaries in China were detained in concentration camps, and Du Chunpu was among those who could not escape. He passed away after returning to the U.S. in 1945. In 1949, the China Sunday School Union’s committee sent Presbyterian missionary Dr. John Montgomery to establish branches in Taiwan and Hong Kong.

  1. Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA), 1912 (Zhang Zhiwei 張志偉 2010: 10-16)

The Young Men’s Christian Association originated in 19th-century England. After its establishment in London in 1844, it expanded to 16 cities in the UK by 1851. Soon, the YMCA movement spread to North America, where it experienced even more vigorous growth. In 1879, The International Committee of YMCA (the North American Association) was established, beginning the promotion of the YMCA movement worldwide.

The earliest YMCA activities in China began in 1885 in Fuzhou, founded by Western missionaries who had participated in YMCA activities. In 1889 and 1890, Luther D. Wishard, a traveling secretary from the North American Association, visited China twice and delivered a speech at a missionary conference in Shanghai in 1890. The conference passed a resolution to jointly request the North American Association to send representatives to China. In October 1895, the North American Association dispatched its first Western secretary, David W. Lyon, to China, starting his work in Tianjin. In 1896, John Mott, general secretary of the North American Association’s student department, visited China as a founder of the World Student Christian Federation, during which he and Lyon inspected church schools across the country, leading to the establishment of 22 new school YMCAs. In November 1896, representatives from these 22 school YMCAs attended the national representative conference and officially founded the “China College Christian Youth Association.”

In January 1900, the first urban YMCA was established in Shanghai, followed by similar associations in cities such as Hong Kong, Yantai, Guangzhou, Fuzhou, and Beijing. In May 1902, during its third national conference, the China College Christian Youth Association renamed itself the “Chinese-Korean Hong Kong YMCA.” In 1912, it changed its name to the “Young Men’s Christian Association of China,” and in 1915, it became the “National Association of Chinese Young Men’s Christian Association.” By 1920, the YMCA had established 30 city associations and 170 school associations in 17 provinces in China, with over 50,000 members, making it one of the most influential Christian organizations in early 20th-century China.

The YMCA aimed to cultivate youth through the “Threefold Education” of moral, intellectual, and physical development. After 1920, “physical” was added, becoming the “Fourfold Education.”

Since 1915, the position of general secretary was held by Chinese nationals, with the first Chinese general secretary being Wang Zhenting (王正廷), succeeded by Yu Rizhang (余日章) in 1917, who served until 1935, becoming the longest-serving general secretary.

  1. Chung Hua Sheng Kung Hui (SHSKH), 1912 (Wu Deshi 吳德施 1931: 13; Chung Hua Sheng Kung Hui 1949: (2) 2-7, 16)

In 1912, the Anglican Communion in China restructured, with each of the dioceses previously under the jurisdiction of the Anglican Churches of the United Kingdom, the United States, and Canada becoming part of the newly established Chung Hua Sheng Kung Hui, making it one of the first denominational traditions from Europe and America to achieve “unity.”

Since the 19th century, Anglican missions from various countries successively entered China. The American Church Mission opened its mission in Shanghai in 1844, which later extended to Jiangsu, Anhui, Jiangxi, Hubei, and Hunan provinces. The Church Missionary Society from Britain had already established itself in Hong Kong in 1842 with colonial government support and formed a diocese by 1849, later spreading to provinces like Zhejiang, Shandong, Zhili, and Guangdong. The Canadian Anglican Church primarily conducted its work in Henan.In 1897, bishops from various countries in the Anglican Communion in China held their first bishops’ conference, changing the previously disconnected situation. Subsequently, in 1909 and 1912, they convened the Bishops’, Clergy, and Laity Union meetings, establishing a climate for building a unified national Anglican organization. In April 1912, the General Assembly held in Shanghai officially passed the constitution and regulations for the Chung Hua Sheng Kung Hui, and the following day, the Anglican General Assembly was established, overseeing 11 dioceses and one missionary district. From that convention, it was stipulated that the General Assembly would hold meetings every three years. By the second national council in 1915, the missionary department of the Chung Hua Sheng Kung Hui was also established as the mission agency for the Anglican Church in China.

The 11 dioceses were: Hong Kong and Canton Diocese, Shanghai Diocese, Zhejiang Diocese, North China Diocese, West China Diocese, Hunan-Hubei Diocese, Shandong Diocese, Fujian Diocese, Guangxi-Hunan Diocese, and Henan Diocese, along with the Shaanxi Missionary District.

In 1918, the first Chinese was consecrated as a bishop, namely Shen Zaichen (沈載琛) of the Zhejiang Diocese. The first Chinese bishop was Zheng Hefu (鄭和甫) from the Henan Diocese, who became a bishop in 1931 after serving as a bishop coadjutor in 1929. In 1947, at the 10th General Assembly of the Chung Hua Sheng Kung Hui, Zheng Hefu became the first Chinese archbishop.

  1. China Continuation Committee, 1913 (Cheng Jingyi 誠靜怡, Luo Bingsheng 羅炳生 1922: 88-105)

In June 1910, the World Missionary Conference was held in Edinburgh, advocating for cooperation among different missions while also promoting collaboration between foreign missions and local churches, marking an important milestone in the “Ecumenical Movement.” To follow up on the outcomes of the conference, the Edinburgh Continuation Committee was established, chaired by John Mott. From October 1912 to May 1913, Mott visited major mission fields in Asia, inviting local church leaders to attend. He arrived in China in January 1913 and convened six regional meetings (in Guangdong, Shanghai, Jinan, Beijing, Hankou, and Shenyang) and one national meeting in Shanghai by March, hoping to communicate the vision of “unity.” At the end of the national meeting held in Shanghai, 120 representatives from Chinese and Western churches approved the establishment of the “China Continuation Committee,” with Cheng Jingyi and E. C. Lobenstine serving as Chinese and Western secretaries, respectively.

According to the Committee’s charter, it was an advisory and consultative organization without administrative or legislative power and without authority to constrain any church or mission. The members numbered between 40 and 60, with at least one-third being Chinese representatives (increasing to half by 1920). The purpose of the Continuation Committee was to facilitate communication between Chinese and Western churches and to promote cooperation among various missions and churches. Upon its establishment in 1913, it set up various special committees, including those for investigation and statistics, theological education, evangelism promotion, and clergy training. Later, due to work needs, additional committees were added for local churches, social services, Sunday school curriculum, promoting prayer, fellowship agreements, phonetic alphabet, missionary outreach, the Return of China movement, and evangelistic work. After the establishment of the Continuation Committee in 1913, it also took charge of editing the China Mission Year Book and the Annual of the Chinese Christian Church.

As a temporary organization, the China Continuation Committee completed its mission after the national Christian conference in 1922, and the newly established National Christian Council of China became the first united organization of Chinese Christianity.

  1. General Assembly of the National Presbyterian Church of China, 1914 (Jia Yuming 賈玉銘 1914: 22-25; Chen Zhiheng 陳智衡 2013: 83-88)

The first Presbyterian mission to China began in 1843 when the Presbyterian Church in the USA entered Guangzhou.Subsequently, various Presbyterian missions began to enter China, including the Reformed Church in America (1842), the Presbyterian Church of England (1847), the Scottish Presbyterian Church (1862), the Southern Presbyterian Church (1867), the Irish Presbyterian Church Mission (1869), and the Canadian Presbyterian Mission (1871), among a total of 12 different missions. As early as 1862, prompted by the Reformed Church in America and the Church of England, the self-governing Chung Hua Presbyterian Church was founded in southern Fujian. In 1890, a Presbyterian missionary proposed uniting all Presbyterian churches in China, but this proposal was not realized. In October 1901, representatives from 10 Presbyterian churches met in Shanghai and agreed to form a unified committee (Committee on Presbyterian Union).

In 1905, the Southern Presbyterian Church agreed to jointly establish the Chinese Presbyterian Church with the Northern Presbyterian Church. In April 1907, during the Centennial Mission Conference, the Federal Council of the Presbyterian Churches in China was formed. In 1914, the Federal Council unanimously decided to establish the “General Assembly of the Presbyterian Bodies in China.” In 1918, the General Assembly convened in Nanjing to discuss organizing a “Temporary General Assembly of the National Presbyterian Church,” with 41 representatives from six major assemblies in Kanto, North China, the Five Provinces (Central China and Jiangnan), southern Fujian, Lingdong, and western Guangdong attending the founding conference. During the meeting, representatives from the Congregational Church and the London Mission also participated, discussing each church’s issues. Later, the Presbyterian Church decided to expand its unity by incorporating with the London Mission and the Congregational Church to form the “Chinese Christian Church.”

However, some regional associations that disagreed with joining the Chinese Christian Church held a meeting in Qingdao in the fall of 1928 and recognized the need to retain the General Assembly of Presbyterians. In the fall of 1929, a meeting was held in Teng County, Shandong, officially establishing the “Presbyterian Church of Christ in China,” with members including the Weixian Association, Yizhou Association (formerly part of the Northern Presbyterian Church), Shijiang Association, Huaidong Association, Jiangbei Association (formerly part of the Southern Presbyterian Church), and the Taonan Association (formerly part of the Canadian Presbyterian Church).

  1. True Jesus Church, 1917 (Bays 1996: 311; Ye Xianqin 葉先秦, Cai Yanren 蔡彥仁 2020: 38-43; Melissa 2018: 57-186)

The True Jesus Church was founded by Wei Baolu (恩波, 1877-1919) in 1917 as an indigenous Christian denomination in China. Wei was originally a member of the London Mission in Beijing, later adopted the beliefs of the Seventh-day Adventist Church, and was subsequently baptized in the Apostolic Faith Church, seeking the fullness of the Holy Spirit. Shortly afterward, he met Bert Berntsen, leading him to accept Oneness Pentecostalism. In April 1917, after fasting for 39 days in the outskirts of Beijing, he claimed that Jesus appeared to him, and he heard a voice telling him to be baptized again in the large red river outside of Yongding Gate. After this baptism, he changed his name to Wei Baolu and named the church “The True Jesus Church of All Nations,” reflecting his desire for religious reform and hoping to “correct” the doctrines and practices of the entire Christian world, making it the “true” Jesus church. Influenced by both the Seventh-day Adventists and Oneness Pentecostals, he advocated for Sabbath observance and foot washing while combining the doctrine of speaking in tongues as a sign of Holy Spirit baptism with salvation, opposing traditional interpretations of the Trinity, and baptizing “in the name of Jesus” (rather than the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit).

In 1918, Wei Baolu united with Zhang Lingsheng and Zhang Barnabas in Shandong, and the church began to expand in southern China. After Wei Baolu’s passing in 1919, the leadership of the church was taken over by his son Wei Yisa, Zhang Barnabas, and Gao Daling, among others.In 1924, Zhang Barnabas began to distance himself from the Northern Church, claiming to possess the orthodox and legal succession of the True Jesus Church in the South. From 1924 to 1931, under Zhang Barnabas’s leadership, the Southern Church expanded the faith of the True Jesus Church to Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Southeast Asia. In 1931, the Northern and Southern churches united with a central station in Shanghai. Zhang Barnabas was removed from his position, but some congregations loyal to him continued to operate under the name “True Jesus Church,” contesting authority with the system led by Wei Yisa.

The core doctrines of the True Jesus Church include baptizing “in the name of Jesus” while facing downward, observing the Sabbath, seeking Spirit baptism, and using “speaking in tongues” as evidence. After the split between the North and South, Zhang Barnabas expanded upon Wei’s teachings to propose the “Five Great Doctrines” (Baptism, Foot Washing, Communion, Sabbath, and the Holy Spirit) and the “Ten Articles of Faith.” In contrast, Wei introduced the “Twelve Standards” in 1947.

By 1949, the True Jesus Church had 125,000 followers and over 1,200 churches.

  1. The Chinese Home Missionary Society, 1918 (Deng Ziliang 鄧梓良 1921: 69-74; Jiang Changchuan 江長川 1924: 42-43; Chen Hexiang 陳和相 1927: 45-47; Chen Hexiang 陳和相 1928: [肆] 10-16; Dong Jing’an 董景安 1930: [肆] 13-16)

The organization of the Chinese Home Missionary Society began in August 1918 when four Christian women—Hu Suzhen (胡素貞) from Hong Kong, Cai Sujuan (蔡蘇娟) from Nanjing, Shi Meiyu (石美玉) from Jiujiang, and Mrs. Song Faxiang (宋發祥) from Beijing—initiated missionary work towards Yunnan during a national leaders’ retreat at Lugu. They invited Cheng Jingyi, Yu Rizhang, and Chen Weiping to join, forming the Yunnan Mission Preparatory Committee, with Cheng as committee chair, Chen as secretary, and Yu as treasurer. Advisory committee members included Ruth Paxson, Ming Zengde, and Fan Liwen. In September, the preparatory committee selected 69 members from churches across various provinces to prepare, naming the organization “The Yunnan Department of the Chinese Home Missionary Society.”

Between March and April 1919, the first group of survey commissioners arrived in Yunnan to initiate their work, along with missionary and women’s schools as well as kindergartens. In November, the stationed external secretary, Chen Tiesheng (陳鐵生), arrived in Yunnan.

In December 1919, the China Continuation Committee initiated the “Return to China Movement” and proposed whether the Chinese Home Missionary Society could form a national missionary organization. In March 1920, the preparatory committee met in Shanghai and decided to accept the commission of the “Return to China Movement.” In July, the committee convened a general meeting at Guling and established a temporary charter, renaming “The Yunnan Department of the Chinese Home Missionary Society” to “Chinese Home Missionary Society,” with Cheng Jingyi as its first president.

Apart from the Yunnan Department, other departments were successively established: the Heilongjiang Department (1921) and the Mongolian Department (1923). However, the Mongolian Department remained only in the planning stages and did not concretely develop. The Home Missionary Society originally planned to hold a national missionary conference in Beijing in 1926, but due to the war, it was postponed to 1928 as a commemorative event for the society’s tenth anniversary. In 1928, the society’s work was still focused on Yunnan and Heilongjiang, with two dioceses (Lufeng and Gejiu) in Yunnan and eight dioceses (Dahongdong, Beian Town, Keshan County, Baiquan County, Hailun County, Zhengzho, Nenjiang County, and Hailar) in Heilongjiang. However, the national missionary conference could not be held as scheduled. In 1930, plans that had been prepared for many years regarding the Mongolian mission were decided to be implemented within three years. Meanwhile, the Yunnan Department grew to four dioceses, while the Heilongjiang Department expanded to 17 dioceses.

  1. North China Theological Seminary, 1919 (Yao 2003: 139-173; Zhao Yuebei 趙曰北 2015)

The North China Theological Seminary was founded in December 1919, arising from theological differences within the Shandong Qilu University Theological School. Qilu University (also known as Shandong Christian University) was jointly established by the Northern Presbyterian Church and the Baptist Missionary Society. In 1916, the president of the theological seminary, J. Percy Bruce (a British Baptist missionary), was appointed as the president of the university. However, this appointment faced opposition from the Northern Presbyterian Church’s Shandong Synod, leading to several members of the Northern Presbyterian Board and faculty resigning. At that time, the deans of the literature and medical schools, along with the acting dean of the seminary, Watson Hayes, unanimously called for President Bruce’s resignation. In 1919, the board ultimately requested President Bruce, the dean of the literature school, and Hayes to resign together.The issues surrounding J. Percy Bruce primarily involved the governance and management style of the theological seminary, but they also highlighted internal conflicts within the seminary itself. On one hand, the Shandong Presbyterial Assembly’s dissatisfaction with the influence of the English Baptist Church over foreign control of the theological school was growing. On the other hand, the more conservative theological position of the Northern Presbyterian Church was likewise discontent with the more liberal theological ideas of faculty members from other denominations within the seminary. The Shandong Presbyterian Church believed that Hayes could understand their perspectives and speak on their behalf, which further deteriorated the relationship between Hayes and President Bruce. After Hayes was forced to resign, 18 Presbyterian students decided to withdraw from the seminary, and the Shandong Presbyterian Church supported this decision, choosing to leave Qilu University Theological School. In November 1919, the Jinan District Assembly resolved to withdraw, citing that the internal conditions of the university hindered the spiritual growth of the students’ knowledge and that the teaching content from other denomination personnel was also unsatisfactory. To prevent students from receiving erroneous ideas, they decided to exit.

In November 1919, the Jinan District Assembly determined that “the Presbyterian Church must establish a respectful, biblically grounded theological seminary in Shandong,” inviting Hayes and Albert Dodd, both missionaries from the Northern Presbyterian Church, to teach. In December, Chinese pastors and missionaries from four district assemblies formed a temporary board of trustees. The seminary was named “Shandong Theological Seminary,” later renamed “North China Theological Seminary,” with Hayes serving as president. The temporary location was chosen in Weixian, and the nearby Southern Presbyterian Church’s Jiangbei missionary district, which had a similar theological stance, was also invited to cofound the seminary. In 1922, the seminary relocated to new facilities in Teng County, Shandong.

At the time of its founding in 1919, North China Theological Seminary had only 18 students, but the number grew rapidly, reaching 105 male and 24 female students by the 1925-26 academic year. By 1927, the number of students peaked with 135 males and 51 females, making it the theological seminary in China with the largest student population.

North China Theological Seminary upheld a fundamentalist theological stance from its inception, establishing that it would teach Christian fundamental doctrines aligned with God’s Word, emphasizing that the Bible was the only standard for faith and life, and striving to maintain conservative truths in theology, biblical studies, and hermeneutics. In 1924, Hayes and other missionaries issued a statement in the name of the seminary, declaring “unconditional support for the theological position of the Chinese Bible Society.” Due to its high regard for biblical authority, by the 1930s, the seminary decided that all students must read the entire Bible under the guidance of their instructors and pass exams.

In addition to missionaries, other Chinese individuals who taught at North China Theological Seminary included Ding Limei (丁立美) and Jia Yuming (賈玉銘), with Jia serving as vice president from 1934 to 1936. In 1937, Zhang Xuegong (張學恭) took over as president. The outbreak of the Pacific War in 1941 led to the internment of missionaries in concentration camps, and the seminary came under the “North China Christian Community,” which was supported by the Japanese. After the war, due to the circumstances of the Chinese Civil War, the seminary relocated from Teng County, Shandong, to a temporary site in Xuzhou, Jiangsu. In 1946, Zhang Xuegong founded Taixi Theological Seminary in Nanjing, with Jia Yuming assuming the presidency and Ding Yuzhang serving as vice president. In July 1948, the seminary moved to a newly constructed campus in Wuxi. In 1951, Ding Yuzhang became the president of the seminary.

In early 20th-century China, North China Theological Seminary became a bastion of fundamentalist faith. In 1952, it merged into the newly established Jinling Union Theological Seminary, with Ding Yuzhang serving as vice president of Jinling. Some of those who taught or graduated from North China Theological Seminary became the bearers of fundamentalist faith under the communist regime.

  1. China Mission Year Book (中國宣教年鑑) first published in 1910, edited by Canadian Presbyterian missionary D. MacGillivray (季理裴), and published by the Christian Literature Society for China (廣學會). In the preface, MacGillivray pointed out that since T. Richard (李提摩太) took the lead in publishing The China Mission Handbook: First Issue in 1896, there had been no second issue published for many years. In 1907, in response to the centenary of Christianity in China, he edited A Century of Protestant Missions in China (1807-1907), which detailed the work of various missionary societies in China. Since 1907, Christianity had been developing rapidly in China. Observing neighboring Japan, where Christian Movement in Japan published by the Cooperating Christian Missions of Japan had already reached its 8th issue in 1910, MacGillivray and another American Congregational missionary, Arthur H. Smith (明恩溥), felt a pressing need for a similar annual report to be published in China. With support from the Christian Literature Society in Shanghai, they began the coordination and editing work.

The first issue of the China Mission Year Book included a total of 30 articles. Besides a general survey and analysis of the background related to China, it covered various directions of missionary work, including evangelism to the elite, church education, the preaching work of various societies, urban missions, self-supporting and self-governing local churches, medical missions, theological education, Bible study movements, women’s work, revivals, joint ministries, literature work, Bible publication and translation, charitable work, industrial work, anti-smoking movements, youth organizations, overseas Chinese, and the development of the Orthodox Church and Catholic Church, among others. In addition to the main text, there were many appendices, making it a thorough and resource-rich document.

Since the publication of its first issue in 1910, the subsequent publication schedule was as follows: 1911 (2), 1912 (3), 1913 (4), 1914 (5), 1915 (6), 1916 (7), 1917 (8), 1918 (9), 1919 (10), 1923 (11), 1924 (12), 1925 (13), 1926 (14), 1928 (15), 1929 (16), 1931 (17), 1932-33 (18), 1934-35 (19), 1936-37 (20), 1938-39 (21). During the years 1920 to 1922, there were three consecutive years of missed issues, likely due to the editorial team’s full effort in compiling another statistical volume, Christian Occupation of China (《中華歸主》). In 1927, the situation was affected by political turmoil, and by 1930, the editors indicated that changes in China were happening rapidly, requiring more time for organization. The complete set of 21 volumes reflects the changes and developments in Christian endeavors over the years and shows how foreign and local church figures understood and responded to the changes in China and the Christian situation.

It is noteworthy that starting in 1926, the China Mission Year Book was renamed the China Christian Year Book (中國基督徒年鑑). The editor for that issue, Frank Rawlinson (樂靈生), pointed out in the preface that the change reflected the development of Christianity in China, which had shifted from being “mission-centric” to “China Christian-centric.”

In 1939, due to the Sino-Japanese War, a large number of missionaries evacuated, leading to the cessation of publication.(2) China Christian Year Book (中華基督教會年鑑) first published in 1914, after the establishment of the Zhonghua Continuation Committee (中華續行委辦會) in 1913. It is the first Chinese document that records the development of the church in China in an annual format. After the formation of the National Christian Council of China (中華全國基督教協進會) in 1922, the editing and publication were taken over by the Council.

The content of the Year Book mainly includes the background of the church and society in China, the development of various denominations, and different Christian endeavors such as unity and cooperation, evangelism, education, youth organizations, literature, medical work, and charity. Numerous charts and surveys have also been included in the appendices. Starting from the second issue, brief biographies of deceased Chinese believers began to appear.

With the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese War in 1937, the Year Book could no longer continue publication.

  1. Comity Agreement (睦誼協議), 1917 (China Continuation Committee 1917:119-124)

In April 1916, the Zhonghua Continuation Committee (中華續行委辦會) held its fourth annual meeting and established the “Comity Special Committee,” chaired by D. E. Hoste (何斯德) from the China Inland Mission. A report was scheduled for submission in the following year. In April 1917, at the fifth annual meeting of the Zhonghua Continuation Committee, the Statement on Comity was submitted. It included nine key points: (1) Arbitration and mediation: agreement to establish a mechanism within denominations or societies to handle disputes; (2) Regional agreements: agreement to consult local missionary organizations before commencing work in new areas, and to explore cooperation or unity with similar foreign bodies already operating there; (3) Transfer of personnel: agreement that before hiring staff (or theological students) working in other societies or churches, the original organization should be consulted; (4) Compensation of personnel: denominations should pay attention to salary levels to ensure they match changes in the economic environment; (5) Handling of personnel subjected to disciplinary actions: agreement that disciplinary decisions should be respected, and prior consultation with the original organization should occur before employing such individuals in new bodies; (6) Relations with members of other churches: agreement that spiritual assistance should be provided to believers from other churches when necessary, and those wishing to transfer should provide proof of their original membership; (7) Baptism and membership: agreement that standards for baptism and membership should be established within denominations, and churches should not baptize those who are currently being taught by evangelists from other churches; (8) Handling of disciplined members: agreement that disciplinary decisions should be respected, and for those transferring without proof, their conduct should be investigated; stricter caution should be taken concerning those being disciplined by their original churches; (9) Chinese representation in arbitration bodies: agreement that if church issues in China are involved, a Chinese representative should be included.

The statement was primarily based on texts approved by the National Missionary Council of India in 1915 and 1916, modified according to Chinese circumstances, and was reviewed and approved paragraph by paragraph by the attending representatives.

  1. United Missionary Conference of Kansu (甘肅省新教差會聯合會議), 1918 (Wu Jianli 2015:127-129)

In 1918, missionaries from the China Inland Mission (內地會), Christian & Missionary Alliance (宣道會), and the Scandinavian China Alliance Mission (北美瑞挪會) stationed in Gansu decided to organize a united conference aimed at promoting the organized and strategic dissemination of the Gospel in Gansu. The preparation committee included Mark Botham (濮馬克) and George Findlay Andrew (安獻今) from the China Inland Mission, with the former responsible for preparing a map of Gansu province and a report titled “Unoccupied Areas” (未得之地), which highlighted the need for the Gospel.From September 22 to 29, 52 missionaries gathered at the Borden Memorial Hospital in Lanzhou for the first joint conference, including 35 from the China Inland Mission (內地會), 14 from the Christian & Missionary Alliance (宣道會), and 3 from the Scandinavian China Alliance Mission (北美瑞挪會). The missionaries discussed cultural and language difficulties encountered in their missions, issues related to funding and ancestor worship, the influence of Tibetan Buddhism, and shared experiences. Among the many issues, the most focus was on how to evangelize to the Muslims in Gansu.

In 1918, Gansu Province had 13 primary schools and 4 secondary schools. The outcome of the conference was the establishment of a provincial church secondary school in Lanzhou, hoping to carry out evangelism through education. At that time, the unevangelized areas in the province included (1) eight counties south of Qinzhou; (2) a large area between Pingliang and Ningxia covering a total of 15 counties; (3) the long arm in the northwest of Liangzhou, including 5 counties of Ganzhou and Suzhou. Additionally, there were areas north of Qinzhou and in the northeast, including the Muslim stronghold of Zhangjiachuan, where no missionaries were stationed.

The joint conference strengthened communication and cooperation among the societies. A second joint conference was held in 1924.

  1. Moslem Work (穆民宣教), 1918 (Wu Jianli 2020:11-16, 120-124, 130-133)

Since the 1910 World Missionary Conference (愛丁堡宣教大會), Moslem missionary work has been widely discussed. However, as early as 1906, China Inland Mission (中國內地會) missionary Marshall Broomhall (海恩波) had begun to pay attention to this topic and started gathering information, publishing Islam in China: A Neglected Problem in 1910 for discussion at the Edinburgh Conference. In 1917, Samuel Zwemer (池維謀), editor of Moslem World, was invited to visit China, where he conducted research on Chinese Muslims in central and western China and gathered missionaries to promote the issues facing Moslem missions. At the Jigu Mountain Conference, the attendees agreed to establish a “Special Committee for Moslem Work” under the Zhonghua Continuation Committee. That same year, Donald MacGillivray (季理斐) from the Christian Literature Society also published Moslem Brethren Must See This (《回教同胞不可不看》).

In 1918, the special committee was officially established, led by G. H. Bondfield (文顯理). Under the committee’s appointment, China Inland Mission missionary Thomas Botham (濮馬可) visited 28 Muslim communities in East and Central China in 1921 and wrote a report, marking the first planned field investigation by missionaries in China. Additionally, the committee published works such as the Chinese Muslim Vocabulary (《中國穆斯林詞彙表》).

In 1922, after the National Christian Council of China was established, the Hui Committee ended in 1926. A group of missionaries concerned with Moslem work, including H. J. Hodgkin (麥樂義), Isaac Mason (梅益威), and Chaude L. Pickens (畢敬士), formed the Society of Friends of the Moslem in China in 1927, with its headquarters in Hankou, hoping to continue the work of the Hui Committee. In 1927, the society published The Quarterly Newsletter of the Society of Friends of Moslems in China (《中國穆民交際會季刊》), which became an important publication discussing Moslem missions in China. In 1936, it also published a Chinese quarterly titled Youmu (《友穆》).

  1. Christianity and the May Fourth Movement (基督教與五四運動), 1919 (Xing Fuzeng 2020:339-380)

The resolution regarding the Shandong issue at the Paris Peace Conference was the trigger for the May Fourth Movement. Various sectors in China closely followed the conference, and Christianity was no exception. As early as March 1919, the Shanghai Christian Union (上海基督教聯合會) and the Beijing Six Christian Associations (北京基督教六公會) decided to send Xu Qian (徐謙) and Wang Zhengtin (王正廷) as observation representatives to the Paris Peace Conference. Both were active Christian figures in the political sphere: Xu Qian served as the Minister of Justice in the Southern Government, while Wang Zhengtin was the Deputy Minister of Foreign Affairs for the Southern Government (and a member of the Chinese government delegation attending the conference).On the evening of March 30, 1919, the Shanghai Christian Missionary Society held a special peace prayer meeting at the Mör Hall to bid farewell to Xu Qian (徐謙). During the meeting, over ten believers from different denominations publicly made suggestions regarding domestic and foreign policy issues. In April, the Shanghai Christian Union (上海基督教聯合會) issued an open letter calling on believers nationwide to pay attention to the conference and pray for the nation. This shows that some Christian groups and believers at the time were closely monitoring the conference and its impact on China’s situation.

On April 22, faculty members at Jinling Theological Seminary (金陵神學院) learned of news regarding the unwillingness to return Qingdao and Jiaozhou to China, leading to the establishment of a National Salvation Prayer Group. They contacted the president of the Nanjing Christian Council for consultation. Subsequently, the seminary sent Zhang Yongxun (張永訓) and Zhou Jigao (周寄高) to Shanghai to coordinate with the Zhonghua Continuation Committee and decided to set June 1 as National Prayer Day.

When news of the Paris Peace Conference reached China, it shocked the nation and triggered protests by university students in Beijing on May 4. Christians across the country also mobilized. May 9 was the “National Humiliation Memorial Day” marked by the signing of the 21 demands; Christians in Nanjing joined other groups to petition the military and civilian authorities. On May 11, Christians in Shanghai held a prayer and peace meeting at Mör Hall, attended by a thousand people. A pastor proposed to send a telegram to the Beijing government, stating, “Christian citizens ask for a government of justice, and only petition to punish the errant bureaucrats,” and also resolved “to request our representatives in Europe not to sign any unjust treaty.” On May 22, the “Beijing Western Union Mission,” composed of European and American missionaries, also lodged complaints with the British and American governments. Moreover, Christians in places like Nanchang, Shanghai, Nanjing, Beijing, and Anqing formed National Salvation Groups.

On July 26, 26 local and foreign Christians in Shanghai (including Milton T. Stanffer (司德敷), Li Yaobang (李耀邦), Chen Tiesheng (陳鐵生), Chen Weiping (陳維屏), Ying Yuandao (應元道), Chen Chunsheng (陳春生), Gu Ziren (顧子仁), Hu Yigu (胡貽穀), and Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡) among others) published an open letter to the national students, expressing regret regarding some external criticisms of the students. “We hope that gentlemen will remember the humiliation suffered for the nation; though dishonor, it is also glory. For our compatriots enduring hardship, though suffering, it is also joy.” They simultaneously affirmed the students’ patriotic spirit, hoping “to ensure that the Chinese people remain equal among the nations and do not descend into the status of slaves or beasts of burden.”

  1. The China for Christ Movement (中華歸主運動), 1919 (Quan Shaowu 1921:44-49)

From December 16 to 20, 1919, the executive committee of the Zhonghua Continuation Committee convened a national conference with the theme “How can Christianity most effectively assist China?” Representatives from 117 churches nationwide discussed the issues of spirituality, evangelism, donations, leadership, social matters, publication, and organization regarding how to “obtain the clearest credit for Christianity in the renewal of individuals and the nation,” ultimately deciding to launch the “China for Christ Movement.” To promote the movement, a temporary organizing committee composed of 30 members was established, with an equal number of Chinese and Western members. The General Secretary of the National Association of Christian Youth in China, Yu Rizhang (余日章), served as the chairman. In 1920, at the annual meeting of the Continuation Committee, a report was submitted suggesting that the Chinese members be increased from one-third to half and to change the name; furthermore, it proposed that the Continuation Committee serve as the central authority for the China for Christ Movement, with Chinese individuals appointed as secretaries for the movement. After discussion, the Continuation Committee restructured its membership to include an equal number of Chinese and Western members, while the decision regarding name change was postponed until a national conference could be convened. As the central body for the movement, the Continuation Committee established a new committee, still chaired by Yu Rizhang, with an equal number of Chinese and Western members. The General Secretary of the China for Christ Movement was appointed to be Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡).

The purpose of the China for Christ Movement is (1) to deepen the Chinese church’s awareness of God; to expand the heart reliant on the Holy Spirit’s power; (2) to help Chinese believers realize that only Christ can meet China’s needs, leading them to willingly contribute themselves for the Lord’s service; (3) to unite and integrate all Chinese believers, guiding the nation’s people to submit to Christ. In terms of its nature, it is characterized as: (1) a spiritual movement; (2) an urgent movement; (3) a practical movement; (4) a movement of the Chinese people; (5) a united movement.

The main focus of work from 1920 to 1922 was to promote the China for Christ Movement from the grassroots level, launching joint evangelistic efforts in major cities such as Beijing and Nanjing. Additionally, summer volunteer schools for children were established to attract youngsters. A “Teaching the Ignorant Society” was also set up to align with the national phonetic alphabet movement.

After the National Christian Council of China was established in 1922, it published the monthly magazine “China for Christ,” but did not set up a related committee within its structure.

Key Theological Issues

  1. Christianity for National Salvation: The YMCA’s (Yóuhui – 青年会) Personality Salvation (Zhang Zhiwei (张志伟) 2010: 115-141; Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1997: 40-49)

From 1911 to 1918, Dr. Sherwood Eddy (艾迪), the North American YMCA (Yóuhui – 青年会) East Asia Touring Secretary, made four trips to China, leading to a tremendous wave of excitement across the nation with his sermons. Besides Dr. Eddy, John Mott (穆德) also visited China in 1913 to conduct evangelistic meetings, which similarly garnered widespread impact. For instance, in 1913, Eddy and Mott preached in 12 cities in China, attracting a total attendance of 78,000 people; after excluding repeat attendees, it is estimated that nearly 35,000 unique individuals attended. The following year (1914), during Eddy’s third visit to China, he held evangelistic meetings in 12 cities including Tianjin (天津), Beijing (北京), Baoding (保定), Changsha (长沙), Wuchang (武昌), Suzhou (苏州), Hangzhou (杭州), Fuzhou (福州), Hong Kong (香港), Guangzhou (广州), Shanghai (上海), and Nanjing (南京), drawing over 120,000 attendees; after excluding repeat attendees, around 35,000 were unique participants, with 90% being non-believers.

Dr. Eddy’s immense popularity can be closely linked to the topics he addressed. His speeches were “all practically related to the future of China,” with titles such as “Christianity is China’s Only Hope,” “Patriotic Christian Principles,” “Causes and Effects of National Decline,” “China’s Flaws,” “Secrets to Solving China’s Difficulties,” “Turning Points for China,” “China’s Urgent Needs,” and “China’s Hopes.” The Christian path he proposed is moral personality for national salvation. China’s weakness lies in a lack of character, while the foundation of Western strength is rooted in Christian character.

The development of the YMCA in the Republic of China (民国) period also significantly contributed to the idea of Christian national salvation. In November 1911, the National YMCA held its sixth conference in Shanghai, and one of the speech topics was “Christianity as the Foundation for a Strong Nation.” The YMCA’s mission, based on the principles of moral, intellectual, and physical education, aims to foster youth as morally upright citizens, thereby achieving the goal of national salvation through social reform.

The General Secretary of the National YMCA, Yu Rizhang (余日章), pointed out in 1918 that “to seek a wealthy and powerful nation, one cannot rely solely on various new laws, but must start fundamentally; promoting morality is essential.” The most important aspect is “receiving God’s love, Christ’s power, and allowing the Holy Spirit to fill within.” In April 1920, at the eighth national conference of the YMCA, he explicitly stated that “to strengthen the nation, citizens must cultivate fairness, compassion, brotherly love, peace, service, sacrifice, and determination; only then can success be achieved. The YMCA aims to nurture these virtues. To seek the way of national salvation, promoting the YMCA should be an urgent task.” Later, at the completion ceremony of the new YMCA headquarters, he inscribed the words “Personality Salvation” on the cover of the commemorative booklet. By 1923, when the YMCA held its ninth national conference, “Personality Salvation” became the main theme of the conference. In the report of the conference, Yu Rizhang confirmed that personality salvation was a tangible goal for the YMCA’s contribution to China in the future. Thus it can be said that the YMCA’s broad advocacy for “Personality Salvation” dates back to the 1920s, but its linkage between personality improvement and national salvation had already been established as early as the 1910s, with the related concepts woven throughout the YMCA’s mission.

After the May Fourth Movement (五四运动) in 1919, national salvation became a topic of fervent interest among students. In June of that year, the 12th Academic Summer Conference in North China was held at the Fo Shan Temple, specifically setting the overall theme as “Christianity for National Salvation” in response to the demands of the times. Initially, there were concerns that the student movement might overshadow it, with expected attendance around a hundred people. However, when the conference commenced, the number of attendees reached 270, and ultimately over 60 individuals expressed their aspirations in the “stand up and speak” session.

In addition to Beijing, the themes of various local summer conferences were also established based on the needs of the times, such as “Christianity and Public Opinion,” “The Attitude of Christianity Towards Political Reform,” and “Patriotism and Morality.” These themes attracted many students who were in a state of “confusion” (including non-Christians and students from official schools). In July, the Jiangsu-Zhejiang (江浙) Christian Summer Conference also carried a strong national salvation emphasis, with speakers mentioning the significant responsibility of Christians at this critical moment for China. The number of attendees exceeded 300.

At the June 1920 13th Academic Summer Conference in North China, it was decided to continue using “Christianity for National Salvation” as the theme, inviting speakers from both domestic and abroad to discuss various perspectives. For instance, Cheng Jingyi (诚静怡) spoke on “The Call for Leadership in the Chinese Church,” and Le Lingsheng (乐灵生) discussed “Jesus Christ as the Leader,” while Wang Zhiping (王治平) talked about “The Struggle for Personality.” The attendance exceeded 300, marking a significant increase compared to previous events.Xu Baoqian (徐宝谦) believes that it reflects students’ “willingness to study Christianity, hoping to gain methods and strength for national salvation from Christianity.” The number of participants in the national summer conferences that year also reached a record high, exceeding 1,600. It is evident that the sense of concern triggered by the May Fourth Movement (五四运动) has strengthened the narrative within the Chinese church about Christian personality-based national salvation.

  1. The Theory of Christian National Salvation and the Christian National Salvation Society (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1997: 76-113)

In addition to personality salvation, there was another different form of Christian nationalism in the 1910s, advocated by Xu Qian (徐谦), who established the Christian National Salvation Society in Shanghai to fully implement his ideas on national salvation through Christianity.

Xu Qian (1871-1940), courtesy name Jilong (季龙), and Christian name George (佐治), hailed from a prominent family in She County, Anhui (安徽). He was born into a scholarly family and actively participated in legal reforms during the late Qing Dynasty. After the Xinhai Revolution (辛亥), he supported the republican cause and followed Sun Yat-sen (孙中山) to engage in revolutionary activities. In 1916, after being baptized as a Christian, he began to think actively about how Christianity could assist China, particularly in advocating for personality salvation. However, he also realized that the political and social evils at the national level could not be resolved solely through personality improvement, as the state could compel individuals to commit crimes. He opposed the approach of “saving individuals before saving the nation,” believing that one cannot attain salvation without the nation. To fundamentally achieve national salvation, efforts in politics, law, and morality would be in vain; only “Christian nationalism” could provide a way to address the sins of the nation and the individual. According to his Christian nationalism, Xu Qian pointed out that “democratic republic” and breaking private property with the “Christian New Communist System” were precisely what China needed. To promote his national salvation ideas, Xu Qian founded the Christian National Salvation Society in January 1920 in Shanghai and established branches in various locations. From 1920 to 1921, the Society actively expressed opinions on various contemporary issues, including foreign affairs. In November 1922, Xu drafted 23 constitutional points based on Christian nationalism, stating that “everything proposed is rooted in fundamental beliefs, namely the truth of Christian national salvation, which should manifest as constitutional claims.”

However, due to Xu Qian’s political background, many in the church viewed his advocated Christian nationalism as a political maneuver utilizing Christian slogans, and his “Christian New Communism” was regarded as radical rhetoric. At the end of 1920, when Sun Yat-sen reorganized the military government and appointed Xu Qian as Minister of Justice, Xu moved south to Guangzhou (广州), and the Society’s activities shifted from Shanghai to Guangdong (粤).

In June 1922, following Chen Jiongming’s (陈炯明) rebellion in Guangzhou, Xu Qian was forced to return to Shanghai with Sun Yat-sen. He remained actively involved in politics until the summer of 1923, at which point the activities of the Christian National Salvation Society began to fade.

  1. Chinese Union Version Bible, 1919 (Zetzsche 1999; Zhao Weibin (赵维本) 1993: 32-45; Zhuang Rouyu (庄柔玉) 2000)

In 1890, at the second missionary conference in China, there was vigorous discussion on the issue of a unified Chinese Bible translation. Representatives from Bible societies in Britain, the United States, and Scotland proposed to translate the Union Version, which included standard translations of classical Chinese (Wen-li Version), simplified Wen-li (simple Wen-li Version), and Mandarin (官话). The conference resolved to establish three translation committees to produce translations in classical Chinese, simplified Wen-li, and Mandarin in order to achieve the goal of “One Bible, Three Translations.” The Revised Version published in 1885 served as the basis for the Union Version translation, with each Bible society determining its own methods for translating terms such as God, Holy Spirit, and Baptism into Chinese.

In 1900, the New Testament of the Simple Wen-li Union Version was published, and in 1904, but the Old Testament portion remained unaddressed. In 1906, the New Testament of the Deep Wen-li Union Version was published.

At the centennial missionary conference in 1907, a representative suggested merging the Simple Wen-li and Deep Wen-li translations into one, and discontinuing any further translation in classical Chinese. As Chinese society moved toward Westernization in the 20th century, writing tended to adopt a simpler style. Additionally, some representatives expressed concern that classical Chinese would use a lot of Confucian vocabulary and concepts, worrying that it might distort biblical truth. Ultimately, the conference decided to combine the translation teams for Simple and Deep Wen-li and publish a single version of the Wen-li Bible. The combined Wen-li translation of the Old and New Testaments was published in March 1919.Regarding the Mandarin version, the New Testament and the Old Testament of the Mandarin Union Version were completed in 1906 and 1919, respectively, and are collectively known as the Guoyu (国语) Union Version.

In 1913, the Nationalist government designated Beijing Mandarin as the “Guoyu” and developed a system of phonetic symbols to mark the pronunciation of Guoyu, establishing it as the national language. In the late 1910s, figures such as Hu Shi (胡适) and Chen Duxiu (陈独秀) advocated for a literary revolution, leading to the rise of the vernacular movement aimed at transforming written Chinese from classical to vernacular language. The publication of the Mandarin Union Version Bible became one of the earliest vernacular translations in China, significantly influencing the popularization and development of vernacular Chinese.

The publication of the Mandarin/Guoyu Union Version was greatly welcomed, replacing the Simple Wen-li Version and becoming widely used among Chinese churches. It was the first vernacular Bible to be commonly used throughout the country, establishing its authoritative status.

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Hu Shih (胡適, 1891-1962), from Songjiang, Jiangsu, was educated under the new educational system in Shanghai starting in 1905. In 1910, he received a scholarship for study abroad and went to the United States to attend Cornell University for agricultural studies. In 1911, influenced by the revolution, he abandoned agriculture for literature. In 1915, he entered the philosophy department at Columbia University, studying under the pragmatist philosopher John Dewey (杜威). After returning to China, he taught at Peking University. Hu Shih was a central figure in the New Culture Movement, advocating for the use of vernacular Chinese in an article published in 1917 in New Youth (新青年).

During his early years in the United States, Hu Shih attended a summer camp of the North American Chinese Christian Students’ Association, where he was exposed to Christianity. Although he maintained a favorable view of Christian ethics, he was critical of Christianity as a religious institution, even considering it superstitious and ignorant. Furthermore, he argued that religion must accept the baptism of scientific civilization to keep pace with the times.

On February 1919, he published an article titled “Immortality—My Religion” (不朽──我的宗教) in New Youth, criticizing the religious view of “the immortality of the soul and the final judgment after death” from a pragmatic perspective. He stated, “To cultivate virtue and do good solely for fear of judgment regarding heaven, hell, and the final days is purely selfish and cannot be regarded as true morality.” Thus, the question of “whether the soul is immortal” has no significant impact on real-life behavior; what matters is not this distinction in being good or bad.

Additionally, he critiqued the “Three Immortalities” (立德、立功、立言), which assess a person’s value based only on their character, achievements, and writings, ignoring whether the soul could exist after death. Although “Three Immortalities” is superior to “immortality of the soul,” it is limited to a minority who possess virtue, accomplishment, and literature. “What about the countless ordinary individuals who may not have hope for immortality?” This undoubtedly leads to the “immortal theory of the ‘few’.” Furthermore, “Three Immortalities” stresses solely the positive aspects; then, “what of the evil?” Moreover, the definitions of virtue, achievement, and speech remain vague.

Hu Shih proposed the theory of “social immortality,” contending that society is an organic entity whose life is interdependent. Therefore, the “small self” does not exist independently; it is influenced by all of society and the world and is causally connected with the past and future of the social world. The past “small self,” the present “small self,” and the future “small self” are intertwined and collectively form the “big self.” “The ‘small self’ dies, but the ‘big self’ is eternally immortal and indestructible. Although the ‘small self’ may perish, every action, merit, sin, speech, or deed—regardless of size, right or wrong, good or evil—will forever exist within that ‘big self’”—this is the “social immortality.” Hence, evil deeds and crimes also lead to “the results of evil that can never be erased nor repented.”

Hu Shih argued that the religious means of “teaching through divine authority,” religious authority, and idol worship have all become ineffective. Therefore, he defined his religious doctrine as “social immortality”: “This present ‘small self’ bears a profound responsibility towards the eternal ‘big self’ and its endless past; I also bear a significant responsibility towards the endless future of that eternal ‘big self.’ I must consistently consider how to use this present ‘small self’ to honor the infinite past of the ‘big self’ and avoid harming its infinite future.”

  1. Zhu Zhixin (朱執信, 1885-1920), originally from Zhejiang, was born in Panyu, Guangdong. In 1904, he studied in Japan on a government scholarship, enrolling at Tokyo Hosei University (東京法政大學). While in Japan, he met Sun Yat-sen (孫中山) and joined the Tongmenghui (同盟會) in 1905. He returned to China in 1907 and became involved in revolutionary activities. After the establishment of the Republic, he continued to follow Sun Yat-sen, participating in the Second Revolution in 1913. Following its failure, he fled to Japan again. In 1917, he participated in the constitutional protection movement and later founded the Republic Daily (民國日報) in Shanghai. He was killed in September 1920 during a conflict at Humen.

On December 25, 1919, the “Hangyu” supplement of the Republic Daily published a special issue against Christianity, including several articles opposing the religion, among which was Zhu Zhixin’s “What is Jesus?” (耶穌是什麼東西). Due to its length, it was continued on December 29, 30, and January 4 and 5, 1920. The editor Wei Xin (味辛) wrote in “Please See What Christianity Is” that this issue of “What is This?” was published to “expose the evils of Christianity.”The text directly points out that Christianity is “the vanguard of imperialist aggression against China,” asserting that “Christian believers act as the pioneers of aggression against China, intoxicating the minds of Chinese youth with the splendor of capitalist society, the pleasures of capitalist society, and the brilliance of the great figures of capitalist society.” Thus, this special issue was particularly edited.

In the article “What is Jesus?” (耶穌是什麼東西), Zhu Zhixin provided explanations from five perspectives: (1) The historical Jesus: “Jesus is merely a bastard.” (2) The Jesus of the Bible: The Jesus in the Bible speaks of equality, love, and treating others as oneself, but the Christian church is selfish, “obliterating the true benefits of Christianity.” Later, Christianity’s dealings with heretical sects were entirely “retaliatory and exclusionary.” (3) The Jesus of Protestants: The Protestants after the Reformation were also self-serving and cruel. (4) The Jesus of new idealist philosophers: The idealization of Jesus’s character merely places the spirit of new idealism into the shell of Christianity. (5) The Jesus of Tolstoy: He is just a tool of “non-resistance.”

Zhu’s conclusion is: “The historical Jesus is of no great significance; the Jesus of new idealism and the Jesus of Tolstoy are both temporarily utilized; therefore, our focus of discussion should naturally be on the Jesus character as described by old and new believers in the Bible for our judgment. We regret that we must conclude: Jesus is a hypocritical, narrow-minded, self-serving, wrathful, and revengeful idol.”

After Zhu Zhixin passed away in 1920, this article was repeatedly reprinted during the non-Christian movement, appearing in various anti-Christian publications, and became an important manifesto criticizing Christianity.

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. Kong Jiaohui (孔教会) 1912 (Huang Kewu (黃克武) 1984: 197-224; Wu Changxing (吳昶興) 2015: 92-97; Chen Xiyuan (陳熙遠) 2020: 147-148)

In October 1912, the Confucian church (孔教会) was established in Shanghai, organized by Chen Huanzhang (陳煥章), Shen Zengzhi (沈曾植), and others, with the goal of “promoting Confucianism and aiding society.” In the preface to the Confucian church, they cited the Doctrine of the Mean (中庸), stating that “the practice of the Dao is what education entails,” affirming that “education” and “Dao” can be interchangeable: “Confucian Dao” is the same as “Confucian education.” Therefore, he disagreed with many people at the time who viewed “Confucian Dao” merely as a theory or doctrine, asserting that this would “plunge China into a state without education.” He stressed, “If we do not recognize Confucianism as education, then although the Confucian Dao exists, it is merely an empty theoretical construct.”

In February 1913, the Confucian church magazine (孔教会杂志) was published, with 132 branches established nationwide. At that time, Yuan Shikai (袁世凯) appointed Chen as an advisor to the presidential office. In June, Yuan issued a “proclamation to revere Confucius,” stating that the Republic of China would “based on ancient principles, establish the rites of Confucius ceremonies in a blended manner with clear regulations to show respect.” In September, the Confucian church held a grand ceremony to honor Confucius at the Guozijian (国子监) in Beijing, attended by thousands.

In August 1913, a petition was presented to the National Assembly, requesting to establish Confucianism as the national religion. However, it was rejected by the constitutional drafting committee. In November, Kang Youwei (康有为) was elected as president, and Chen Huanzhang (陳煥章) was appointed as the general secretary. In 1916, after Yuan Shikai’s passing, the Confucian church once again petitioned the National Assembly to establish Confucianism as the national religion and to restore Confucius’ rites. At that time, Christians formed the “Religious Freedom Association” to oppose this. Ultimately, the national religion proposal was still rejected. In fact, those opposing the establishment of a state religion were not limited to Christians; figures from the New Culture Movement also criticized traditional culture, thus withholding support from the Confucian movement.

After the 1920s, the national religion movement became increasingly silent. By the time of the establishment of the Nanjing Nationalist Government, the Confucian church had virtually disbanded. In 1936, the central training department of the Kuomintang, along with the Ministry of Internal Affairs and the Ministry of Education, discussed reorganizing the Confucian church into a new association, suggesting renaming it “General Association of Confucian Studies” (孔学总会) or “General Association of Confucian Dao” (孔道总会), completely under the control of the Kuomintang.

  1. Shaonian Zhongguo Xuehui (少年中國學會) 1919 (Li Yongchun (李永春) 2005: 214-259)

The Young China Society (少年中國學會) was an influential organization formed during the May Fourth New Culture Movement. As early as June 1918, the founders, including Wang Guangqi (王光祈), Li Dazhao (李大釗), and Zhou Taixuan (周太玄), discussed and established a preparatory committee in Beijing. After a year of preparation, it was officially founded on July 1, 1919. The general assembly was located in Beijing, with branches in Nanjing and Chengdu, boasting approximately 120 members distributed nationwide. The society’s aim was to “embody the spirit of scientific inquiry for social action, to create a ‘Young China’.” Upon its establishment, it launched the monthly magazine “Young China” (少年中國), which ceased publication in May 1924.

Since its inception, “Young China” published some statements opposing Christianity. It criticized Chinese society’s submission to Western “churches” and pointed out that some in the country were superstitious about the “religious salvation theory,” believing that religion was the key to the strength of Europe and America, further claiming that the YMCA (Young Men’s Christian Association) was an “old force.” In 1921, “Young China” published three issues focusing on the “religious question,” featuring 29 articles discussing religion. Overall, these addressed the definitions and origins of religion, sources and contents of religious beliefs, religious experiences, and the benefits and drawbacks of religion. Zhang Qinshi (張欽士) from the Beijing Youth Association later noted that the religious thoughts from 1917 to 1921 primarily debated whether “modernizing China requires religion” from a “scientific perspective.” In general, “Young China” exhibited an “anti-religion” and “anti-Christian” stance, believing that religion had lost its value, asserting that even if it had some social functions, it could be replaced by other means.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Scientism Against Religion (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2006: 74-77)

During the New Culture Movement, discussions regarding religion primarily critiqued it from the perspective of scientism. Scientism treats science as the ultimate truth, denying the tenets of spiritual entities and the objectivity of values, believing that science can ensure future societies are in a perfect state.

In 1908, a Chinese believer using the pen name “Weixinzi” (微信子) addressed the influence of “pan-scientism” on churches in the newspaper Youth Daily (青年報), stating: “Today’s situation is drastically changing, the dissemination of science is entering our country’s academic world from both East and West. The attitude of scholars towards Christianity has shifted from blind hatred due to ignorance to a half-understood rejection… It is claimed that the doctrines presented by Christianity often lack substantial evidence that can be readily verified, relying solely on human belief, which is closely akin to a form of superstition.” Furthermore, Wang Aitang (王愛棠) from the Dongguan (東莞) Li Xian Society raised concerns about the “dangers” faced by churches during this “transitional era,” pointing out that “today’s enemies of the church are, surprisingly, the new doctrines of Western Confucianism.” The so-called “new doctrines” refer to evolutionary theory and atheism. These new theories characterized by scientism spread to China as the Chinese adopted Western learning, becoming widely popular among intellectuals of the time. Science was universally emphasized and pursued, and with science being the only inquiry accepted, this led to the deeply rooted influence of natural science’s value system among the Chinese people, resulting in the rejection of all irrational and anti-natural myths, including religion itself.

The late scholar Joseph R. Leverson (李文遜) summarized the Chinese opposition to Christianity by stating that in the 19th century, the Chinese opposed Christianity because it was considered anti-traditional, while by the 20th century, the opposition was due to its conflict with modern science (anti-modern). In the 19th century, Christianity was criticized for not being Confucian; however, by the 20th century, it was condemned for not being scientific. The wave of scientism extended into the 1920s, eventually merging with nationalism and giving rise to a large-scale anti-Christian movement.
