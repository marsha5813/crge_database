China: Context (1910-1920)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. The Constitutional Provisions Regarding Religion in the Early Years of the Republic of China (Cen Dezhuang 岑德彰 1933)

The “Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China” (中華民國臨時約法) is the first constitutional law established after the founding of the Republic of China. It was passed by the Provisional Senate, located in Nanjing, on March 8, 1912 (the first year of the Republic of China). Article Five states: “The people of the Republic of China are equal and shall not be distinguished by race, class, or religion.” Article Six includes “freedom of belief” among the various rights and freedoms enjoyed by the people.

In February 1912, Sun Yat-sen (孫中山) ceded the position of Provisional President to Yuan Shikai (袁世凱) to promote peace negotiations between the north and south and facilitate the abdication of the Qing dynasty. In March, Yuan Shikai assumed office as Provisional President in Beijing, and at that time, both the House of Representatives and the Senate began drafting constitutional work, holding sessions at the Temple of Heaven (天壇) near Yongding Gate. The draft of the “Constitution of the Republic of China” (中華民國憲法草案), also known as the “Temple of Heaven Constitution Draft” (天壇憲法草案), was completed in October. Article Four states: “The people of the Republic of China are equal in law without distinctions of race, class, or religion.” Article Eleven: “The people of the Republic of China have the freedom to practice religion and shall not be restricted except by law.”

In October 1913, after Yuan Shikai became the President, dissatisfied with the restrictions on presidential power in the “Draft Constitution of the Republic of China,” he drafted a new “Constitution of the Republic of China” (中華民國約法), also known as the “Yuan Constitution” (袁氏約約), which was published in April 1914. Article Four states: “The people of the Republic of China are equal in law without distinctions of race, class, or religion.” Article Five states regarding the freedom rights of the people: “The people have the freedom to practice religion within the bounds of the law.”

After Yuan Shikai’s death in 1916, Li Yuanhong (黎元洪) became President. However, the situation in China further deteriorated, falling into warlord fragmentation. It wasn’t until the establishment of the Nanjing National Government in 1927 that the work of drafting a new constitution was resumed.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. National Religion Movement and the Freedom of Belief 1913-1917 (Han Hua 韩华 2007; Wu Zongci 吳宗慈 1924: 38)

After the establishment of the Republic of China, although the Provisional Constitution included provisions advocating for “freedom of belief,” it still faced considerable challenges in the early years of the Republic. Undoubtedly, the most fatal challenge was the proposal to establish Confucianism (Kǒngjiào 孔教) as the national religion.

As early as the late Qing Dynasty, Kang Youwei 康有为 advocated for Confucianism to be set as the national religion, believing that its establishment could save China. After the founding of the Republic, he remained firmly convinced of this belief. In 1913, Kang published a draft constitution for the Republic of China in “The Unyielding Magazine” (Bùrěn zázhi 不忍杂志), which reiterated the opinion of “establishing Confucianism as the national religion.” Additionally, Chen Huanzhang 陳煥章, the initiator of the Shanghai Confucian Association, actively supported this idea.

Although Yuan Shikai 袁世凯, after taking office as president, stated his willingness to maintain a policy of religious freedom, he displayed considerable support for the Confucian movement. For example, in June 1913, he ordered the restoration of the Confucius (Kǒngzǐ 孔子) worship ceremonies. In July of the same year, the constitutional drafting committee was formed to prepare a constitution, at which point the Confucian Association immediately petitioned Yuan to establish Confucianism as the national religion while allowing freedom of belief. At this time, Christians in Beijing organized an Anti-National Religion Association, collaborating with representatives from Catholicism, Buddhism, Taoism, and Islam to advocate for religious freedom, opposing the establishment of Confucianism as the national religion, and conducting petition activities. When the National Assembly discussed the proposal to declare Confucianism as the national religion, it sparked many opinions. Members of Yuan’s party and a few progressive party legislators supported it (Chen Mingjian 陳銘鑑 proposed the “Proposal on Whether Confucianism Should Be Declared the National Religion in the Constitution,” Zhu Zhaoxian 朱兆莘 proposed “Establishing Confucianism as the Foundation of National Education,” and Huang Zanyuan 黃贊元 proposed “The Republic of China Taking Confucius’ Way as the Foundation of Civil Culture”). In contrast, members of the Kuomintang (Guomindang 國民黨) opposed it (He Wen 何雯, Xu Jingxin 徐鏡心, Wu Chaoshu 伍朝樞, Wang Pengnian 汪彭年, Lu Tianyou 盧天游, Gu Zhongxiu 谷鍾秀, etc.). After lengthy debates, a temporary compromise was reached by October; Article 19, Section 2 of the so-called “Temple of Heaven Constitution” emphasized only: “National education is based on Confucius’ teachings for self-cultivation.” Thus, the national religion movement ended in failure, but this clause left a basis for the later movements advocating for freedom of belief.

In 1916, after Yuan Shikai’s death, Li Yuanhong 黎元洪 succeeded him as president, and the National Assembly reopened. At that time, leaders of the Christian community in Beijing united various religions to call for the removal of Article 19, Section 2 from the constitutional draft to permanently preserve complete freedom of belief. However, this action met with strong opposition from the Confucian Association, which sought the support of provincial warlords to bolster the advocacy for Confucianism as the national religion.

Facing the resurgence of the national religion issue, the “Freedom of Belief Association” (Xìnjiào zìyóu huì 信教自由会) was officially established in Beijing in November 1916. Xu Qian 徐謙 was elected as the president of the Freedom of Belief Association. The actions of the Freedom of Belief Association aimed to publicize its purpose and gain public support through speeches, issuing “Petition Letter for Freedom of Belief News,” telegrams, writings, and other methods. On the other hand, they also directly petitioned President Li Yuanhong twice, as well as contacted members of both houses of the National Assembly to influence their decisions. At that time, Li Yuanhong remarked: “I regard the reasons for the petition from the Freedom of Belief Association as sufficient,” and promised to present the related opinions to the National Assembly. Meanwhile, Christians joined forces with Catholics, Eastern Orthodox Christians, Muslims, and Buddhists to strive for religious freedom together.

On May 14, 1917, the National Assembly resumed constitutional meetings, with supporters and opponents of the national religion still unable to reach an agreement, and several votes yielded no results. Ultimately, both sides compromised, repealing Article 19, Section 2 of the “Temple of Heaven Constitution,” which stated, “National education is based on Confucius’ teachings for self-cultivation,” and amending it to “People of the Republic of China have the freedom to revere Confucius and to practice religious beliefs, which shall not be restricted by law.” On May 15, the Freedom of Belief Association convened a national representative conference at the Beijing Youth Association, expressing deep satisfaction with the National Assembly’s decision, believing their goals had been achieved, and decided to hold a “Celebration Conference” on the 17th.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. The Political Landscape after the Founding of the Republic of China (Guo Tingyi 郭廷以 1983: 427-502; Young 1983: 208-255; Nathan 1983: 256-283; Sheridan 1983: 284-321)

After the Xinhai Revolution, the Qing Dynasty fell, and Sun Yat-sen (孫中山) assumed the position of provisional president in January 1912. However, because the revolutionary party could not control military power, Yuan Shikai (袁世凱), the leader of the Beiyang New Army, became the key figure in the political landscape. After reaching an agreement with the revolutionaries, Yuan Shikai forced the Qing to abdicate, becoming the first official president of the Republic of China. However, Yuan Shikai’s political ambitions exceeded republican ideals; he quickly weakened the power of the parliament and political parties, concentrating power in his own hands.

In 1913, Sun Yat-sen led the Kuomintang (國民黨) in launching the “Second Revolution” in an attempt to overthrow Yuan Shikai, but they failed due to the disparity in strength. Subsequently, Yuan Shikai dissolved the parliament and declared the restoration of the monarchy in 1915, self-styling as the “Emperor of the Chinese Empire.” However, the monarchy faced opposition across the country, including the national defense war initiated by figures like Cai E (蔡鍔) and Li Liejun (李烈鈞). Under internal and external pressures, Yuan Shikai abolished the monarchy in 1916 and soon passed away, ending his brief and authoritarian rule.

After Yuan Shikai’s death, the Beiyang system split into different factions, including the Zhili clique (直系), Anhui clique (皖系), and Fengtian clique (奉系), marking the beginning of the warlord era. The Beiyang warlords fought for control over the central government and regional power, leading to years of conflict and political instability: (1) The Zhili warlords, led by Feng Guozhang (馮國璋) and Cao Kun (曹錕), controlled Zhili and the North China region, advocating for the maintenance of the Beiyang power structure; (2) The Anhui warlords, led by Duan Qirui (段祺瑞), controlled Anhui and the middle reaches of the Yangtze River and competed with the Zhili clique; (3) The Fengtian warlords, led by Zhang Zuolin (張作霖), dominated the Northeast and gradually expanded their influence in North China.

In 1917, Anhui warlord Duan Qirui dismissed Li Yuanhong (黎元洪) and dissolved the parliament, leading Sun Yat-sen to initiate the Constitutional Protection Movement in Guangzhou, attempting to restore the Provisional Constitution. In 1920, Duan Qirui offended Zhili forces due to his close relations with Japan, resulting in a war for central control between the two sides. The Zhili ultimately defeated the Anhui faction, causing Duan Qirui to step down and significantly weakening the Anhui forces. Subsequently, conflicts broke out between the Zhili and Fengtian warlords over control of the Beijing government. The Zhili emerged victorious, suppressing the Fengtian forces, but the conflict between the two sides remained unresolved. While the Beiyang government nominally maintained central authority, in reality, internal factional disputes were frequent, and various regions strengthened their tendencies towards autonomy. Fiscal, military, and diplomatic powers became decentralized, and the central government nearly lost its ruling capabilities.

During the Beiyang government era, China’s internal divisions provided opportunities for foreign powers to expand their influence in the country. Japan was one of the most influential foreign powers during the Beiyang government era. Particularly during Yuan Shikai’s time, Japan forced China to make further concessions through the “Twenty-One Demands.” Following that, Japan continued to support the Anhui warlords and expanded its interests in China through economic control and military advisors, especially in Shandong and Manchuria. Britain primarily focused on its economic interests in China, particularly in the Yangtze River basin and the Shanghai concessions. Britain adopted a relatively flexible stance towards the Beiyang warlords, neither actively intervening nor overtly supporting any particular faction. The United States continued its “Open Door” policy, attempting to prevent Japan’s expansion. However, after World War I, the United States focused on domestic issues and had limited intervention in the Beiyang government. After the October Revolution, Soviet Russia exhibited a friendly diplomatic posture, claiming to abandon the unequal treaties from the Tsarist era and supporting revolutionary forces in China, such as the Kuomintang led by Sun Yat-sen, in an effort to expand its influence in China.

  1. The New Culture Movement and Complete Westernization (Schwartz 1983; Schwarcz 1986; Lin 1979; Li Zehou 李澤厚 1987: 11-15)

The New Culture Movement was an important ideological and cultural movement in China from the mid-1910s to the early 1920s, which began with the launch of the magazine “La Jeunesse” (新青年) in 1915 and further developed after Yuan Shikai’s death in 1916. The core of this movement was a comprehensive critique of traditional Chinese culture and a high regard for Western思想文化, particularly represented by the idea of “complete westernization,” aiming to find solutions to the social crises in China.

The 1911 Xinhai Revolution overthrew the Qing Dynasty, but instead of establishing a democratic China, it led to the failure of Yuan Shikai’s attempt to restore the monarchy and the warlord fragmentation. At the same time, the “Twenty-One Demands” highlighted Japan’s rising ambitions. Faced with internal and external crises, many intellectuals reflected on why political revolutions could not lead to national reconstruction and turned to consider issues of national identity. They believed that as long as traditional Confucian culture was not dismantled, the people would struggle to awaken, thus igniting a cultural revolution known as the “New Culture Movement.”In the New Culture Movement, the ideas of “complete westernization” or “totalistic antitraditionalism” were particularly prominent. The main advocates included Chen Duxiu (陳獨秀), Hu Shi (胡適), and Lu Xun (魯迅). They believed that for China to achieve modernization, it must entirely abandon traditional culture and fully embrace Western civilization.

In “La Jeunesse,” Chen Duxiu proposed “Mr. Democracy” and “Mr. Science” (i.e., democracy and science) as the two pillars for national salvation, calling for a cultural transformation of China. He sharply criticized traditional Confucianism, equating it with feudal despotism, and proposed the notion of “abolishing classical studies,” attempting to completely overthrow traditional ethics. Hu Shi, deeply influenced by American philosopher John Dewey’s pragmatism, advocated for “more experimentation and less empty talk” and argued for “re-evaluating Chinese culture by Western standards” on the level of ideology and culture.

He promoted the vernacular movement, believing that an accessible language was a necessary condition for popularizing new ideas, and in the literary revolution, he posited that “literature should be a tool for expressing thoughts.” Lu Xun regarded the ethical value system of Confucian culture as the root of “national shortcomings” in China. He criticized values emphasized by Confucianism, such as the “Three Principles and Five Virtues” and “filial piety and loyalty,” which bound individuals under the authority of family and monarch, stifling individuality and free thought. The notion of “eating people’s rites and teachings” became fundamental to the oppression of humanity by feudal culture.

Vera Schwarcz (舒衡哲) referred to the New Culture Movement as China’s Enlightenment, focusing on the long-term critique of China’s inherent traditions. Li Zehou (李澤厚) pointed out that the New Culture Movement after 1915 was a period where enlightenment and national salvation mutually promoted each other, with the goal of cultural transformation ultimately aimed at changing China’s predicament and freeing it from the threat of national survival, a phenomenon he called “the dual variations of enlightenment and national salvation.” Lin Yusheng (林毓生) indicated that the intellectuals of the New Culture Movement’s “antitraditionalism” had a “totalistic” aspect, but this attempt to “use thought and culture to solve (social and political) problems” paradoxically rooted in the deeply entrenched traditional Chinese culture’s “monistic and intellectualistic mode of thinking.”

  1. The Russian Revolution and China in 1917 (Pantsov 2000)

The 1917 Russian Revolution consisted of the February Revolution and the October Revolution, which not only overthrew the Tsarist regime but also established the world’s first socialist state, Soviet Russia. Initially, during World War I, Russia’s economy collapsed, social contradictions intensified, and Tsar Nicholas II’s (尼古拉二世) rule could not be maintained, leading to large-scale protests and strikes. Ultimately, the Tsar abdicated, ending the Romanov dynasty, and a provisional government formed by members of the Duma proclaimed the establishment of a democratic regime. At the same time, a group of workers and soldiers formed the Petrograd Soviet of Workers’ and Soldiers’ Deputies, becoming another powerful political force. The provisional government was dominated by liberals and the bourgeoisie, while the Soviet represented the interests of the working-class and peasantry.

Ultimately, the Bolshevik Party, led by Vladimir Lenin (列寧), carried out a coup against the provisional government of Russia, establishing the world’s first socialist state—Soviet Russia (later the USSR).

The October Revolution not only completely changed Russia’s political structure but also had a profound impact on global politics in the 20th century, especially in China. The October Revolution extended the practice of Marxism worldwide and inspired progressive intellectuals in China. Among them, Li Dazhao (李大釗) was one of the earliest Chinese thinkers to praise the October Revolution, stating in an article published in 1918 that Russia’s transformation marked “the beginning of a new era for the world.” The Communist International (共產國際), established in 1919 and also known as the Third International, was an international communist organization led by the Soviet Bolshevik Party, aimed at promoting proletarian revolution globally and establishing a worldwide communist system. In 1921, the Communist Party of China was established with the support of the Communist International. At the same time, the Soviet regime after the Russian Revolution took an anti-imperialist stance and also supported Sun Yat-sen’s (孫中山) Nationalist revolutionary movement.The cooperation between Soviet Russia and the Kuomintang (中國國民黨) laid the foundation for the first collaboration between the Nationalists and Communists in 1924.

  1. The Shandong Issue, the Paris Peace Conference, and the May Fourth Movement in 1919 (Chow 2013)

The May Fourth Movement, which erupted on May 4, 1919, was a profoundly influential student movement in modern Chinese history, sparked by the widespread dissatisfaction among the Chinese populace regarding the outcomes of the Paris Peace Conference. The Paris Peace Conference was an international meeting held after the conclusion of World War I, aimed at discussing post-war international order and peace treaties. During the conference, China, as one of the Allied Powers, had high hopes for the resolution of the Shandong issue, aspiring to reclaim sovereignty over Shandong, which had been occupied by Germany. The Shandong issue involved how to handle the rights and interests in China of Germany, a defeated nation. As a belligerent nation, China expected to regain relevant rights and interests. However, upon Japan’s entry into the war in 1914, it rapidly occupied Germany’s concessions and railway rights in Shandong and presented the “Twenty-One Demands” to China in 1915, further expanding its interests in China, including in Shandong, Manchuria, and Inner Mongolia. At that time, the Beiyang government, dominated by Yuan Shikai (袁世凱), sought to use Japanese support to declare himself emperor, and therefore dared not completely reject Japan’s demands, signing a revised treaty on May 9, 1915. Despite the strong domestic opposition to the “Twenty-One Demands,” these terms provided Japan with a pretext for legitimate control over Shandong in the international arena.

During the Paris Peace Conference, Britain and the United States leaned towards supporting Japan regarding the disposition of Germany’s rights in China, mainly due to their respective strategic considerations and diplomatic compromises. Britain and Japan secretly signed an agreement in 1917, promising to support Japan’s succession of Germany’s rights in Shandong as a condition for Japan’s continued support for the Allies.

At the Paris Peace Conference, the Chinese delegation actively advocated for the return of Shandong rights as a victorious nation, emphasizing the legitimacy of China’s participation in the war and the principle of national self-determination. However, Britain and France were firmly supportive of Japan, believing that Japan’s contribution during the war, particularly its role in the Pacific battlefield, warranted compensation. Britain further supported Japan’s demands based on the previous secret agreement.

U.S. President Woodrow Wilson (威爾遜) proposed the “Fourteen Points” for peace, which included the concept of self-determination and offered China a glimmer of hope. However, to ensure the United States’ leadership in Europe and the League of Nations, Wilson compromised with Japan. Ultimately, the Paris Peace Conference decided to transfer Germany’s interests in Shandong, including the Jiaozhou railway and the Qingdao concession, to Japan. This outcome completely ignored China’s legitimate claims and triggered intense dissatisfaction across various sectors in China. The Chinese delegation ultimately refused to sign the Treaty of Versailles (《凡爾賽條約》).

When the decisions of the Paris Peace Conference were communicated back to China at the end of April 1919, they provoked outrage among the Chinese populace. On May 4, students from Peking University and other institutions gathered at Tiananmen Square to stage massive protests. They shouted slogans such as “Fight for sovereignty externally, and remove traitors internally,” demanding the rejection of the Treaty of Versailles and strongly condemning the government’s compromise with Japan. They also targeted pro-Japanese politicians, such as Cao Ruolin (曹汝霖) and Zhang Zongxiang (章宗祥), even burning Cao Ruolin’s residence to express their dissatisfaction. The movement quickly spread from the student community to the entire nation. Workers, merchants, and other social classes joined in, forming a powerful patriotic movement. Strikes, class boycotts, and market closures followed, further pressuring the government to change its stance. The May Fourth Movement marked a significant awakening of Chinese nationalism and symbolized the united action of intellectuals and the general populace on critical political issues.

The success of the May Fourth Movement forced the Beiyang government to refuse to sign the Treaty of Versailles and prompted the politicization of the Chinese intellectual community in the 1920s. Faced with the suppression of truth by Western powers, many intellectuals were convinced that political means were necessary to save the country. The various patterns of movements during the May Fourth Movement directly influenced the establishment of the Communist Party of China and subsequent revolutionary movements. It can be said that the May Fourth Movement reflected the strong nationalist and salvific demands of China in the face of external oppression and internal corruption. Additionally, it represented a cross-class social mobilization, particularly giving rise to the modern student movement.
