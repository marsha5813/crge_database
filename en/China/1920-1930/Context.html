China: Context (1920-1930)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. Ministry of Education Training Directive No. 138, 1921 (Zhu Youhuan (朱有瓛) and Gao Shiliang (高時良) 1993: 783)

In April 1921, the Ministry of Education established regulations for private secondary schools with six stipulations: (1) “The name of the school should include the term ‘private’”; (2) “Secondary schools must operate in accordance with the Secondary School Act and its implementation rules…”; (3) “The subjects and curriculum standards for secondary schools must be followed. If changes are necessary, reasons must be stated and reported to the provincial education authorities for approval by the Ministry of Education. However, the National Language and National Geography may not be requested for changes”; (4) “The content of subjects and teaching methods must not include proselytizing nature”; (5) “Students, regardless of their religious beliefs, should be treated equally”; (6) “Those who violate the above provisions will not be allowed to register; if already registered, changes made midway may result in cancellation of registration.”

  1. Ministry of Education Announcement No. 16, 1925 (Zhu Youhuan (朱有瓛) and Gao Shiliang (高時良) 1993: 784)

In November 1925, the Education Report announced six regulations regarding schools established with donations from outsiders: (1) “Any school established with donations from outsiders…may apply for recognition in accordance with the procedures published by the Ministry of Education”; (2) “The school’s name must include the term ‘private’”; (3) “The principal of the school must be Chinese; if the principal is originally a foreigner, a Chinese must serve as vice-principal and represent the school during the application for recognition”; (4) “If the school has a board of directors, Chinese individuals must occupy more than half of the board seats”; (5) “The school’s purpose must not be the propagation of religion”; (6) “The curriculum of the school must adhere to the standards set by the Ministry and cannot include religious subjects as mandatory.”

  1. Zhejiang Provincial Education Department’s Order Prohibiting Religious Propaganda in Schools, 1925 (Zhu Youhuan (朱有瓛) and Gao Shiliang (高時良) 1993: 784-785)

On May 1, 1925, the Zhejiang Provincial Education Department issued an order stating that “teaching religion is absurd and indeed serves to obscure human intellect.” As schools are “places for cultivating talent,” they “should not promote religion, which hinders the advancement of education.” It specifically mandated “prohibition of religious propaganda within schools.”

  1. Regulations for the Establishment of Private Universities and Specialized Schools and Regulations for the Establishment of Private Secondary Schools and Elementary Schools, 1927 (Da Xue Yuan (大學院) 1928: 26-31)

On December 20, 1927, the “Regulations for the Establishment of Private Universities and Specialized Schools” and the “Regulations for the Establishment of Private Secondary Schools and Elementary Schools” were promulgated, dictating that the aforementioned schools must be submitted for approval to the Ministry of Education of the Republic of China through provincial education administrative bodies. The regulations stipulate that “the principal must be Chinese.”

  1. Regulations on Private Schools, 1928 (Zhu Youhuan (朱有瓛) and Gao Shiliang (高時良) 1993: 786)

In February 1928, the Ministry of Education announced the “Regulations on Private Schools”: (1) “Schools established by individuals or groups are classified as private schools; schools established by foreigners and churches are included”; (2) “Private schools must be under the supervision and guidance of education administrative agencies”; (6) “The principal of a private school must be Chinese”; (8) “Private schools may not include religious subjects as mandatory, nor conduct religious propaganda in courses”; “If there are religious rituals in private schools, students may not be compelled to participate.”

  1. Regulations on Private School Boards, 1928 (Zhu Youhuan (朱有瓛) and Gao Shiliang (高時良) 1993: 786)

In February 1928, the Ministry of Education published the “Regulations on Private School Boards,” in which Article 12 stipulates: “In special circumstances, foreign individuals may serve on the board of directors; however, their number must not exceed half, and the chairperson must be Chinese.”

  1. National Government’s Circular on Religious Freedom, 1928 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1997: 350-352, 361)

In January 1928, two Kuomintang Christian members, Zhang Zhijiang (張之江) (Commissioner of the National Government and Senator of the Military Senate) and another, Niu Yongjian (鈕永建) (Governor of Jiangsu Province), proposed to the Central Political Conference to implement religious freedom and abolish slogans against Christianity and all religions. In their proposal, they stated that Christianity is absolutely unrelated to imperialism, and its spirit contains rich “revolutionary significance,” guiding followers with concepts of equality, fraternity, and freedom, which influenced revolutions in Britain, America, and Germany. Many comrades of the Chinese revolution (including Sun Yat-sen and individuals from the Northwest Army) were also “inspired by this faith,” striving to save the country and people. Without the impact of Christianity, “how could the centuries-old feudal loyalty become second nature, suddenly advocating for nationalism and livelihood?” Moreover, Christianity “emphasizes reform” and also “focuses on alleviating the suffering of the lower classes.” Thus, Christianity is “incompatible with imperialism” and should not be recklessly undermined.Furthermore, they pointed out that the slogan to abolish Christianity is actually a result of the Communist Party’s hostility towards it. Now that the Kuomintang is conducting a party purge, it should also further eliminate Communist theories. The slogan against Christianity should be canceled as early as possible, and religious freedom should be implemented. Only then can one “comfort the sincere beliefs of the late Premier.”

This proposal was discussed in the 128th meeting of the Central Political Conference on February 15, 1928, and it was decided to refer it to the Central Party Headquarters for consideration at the Standing Committee meeting the following day. On February 26, the Kuomintang Central Executive Committee announced its review of the Zhang-Niu proposal, resulting in the conclusion that “the Central Party had already made a decision last year, and there is no need for further review.” It reiterated that in the meeting of May 13, 1927, the public “must not misunderstand the opposition to imperialism as using any force to oppress or violate the religious freedom of the people, both Chinese and foreign.”

  1. Regulations for Churches Renting Land and Buildings in Mainland China, 1928 (Zhao Guangjun (趙廣軍) 2014: 101)

In July 1928, the National Government promulgated the “Regulations for Churches Renting Land and Buildings in Mainland China,” allowing foreign churches to establish hospitals or schools in China, “as permitted by treaties between their country and China,” to rent land and construct or purchase buildings in the name of the church. The land and buildings “must comply with current or future laws and taxes in China.” Churches must report to local authorities for approval together with the property owners, and the amount and size of land and buildings rented or constructed must not exceed the church’s needs, nor may they be used for profit or commercial purposes; otherwise, local authorities shall not approve or may cancel the contract.

  1. Regulations on Private Schools, 1929 (Zhu Baochen (朱寶琛) 1930: [Wu] 96-97)

In September 1929, the Ministry of Education stipulated in the “Regulations on Private Schools”: “Private schools established by religious organizations shall not include religious subjects as mandatory, nor conduct religious propaganda in courses. If there are religious rituals in schools, students must not be forced or encouraged to participate, and no religious ceremonies may be held during school hours” (Article 5).

  1. Ministry of Education’s Measures for Religious Organizations Engaging in Educational Activities (Zhu Baochen (朱寶琛) 1930: [Wu] 98)

In April 1929, the Ministry of Education established procedures because religious organizations engage in educational activities, either “by donating to establish schools to cultivate talent,” or “to gather followers for the study and dissemination of their doctrines,” clarifying that these two purposes are different and can easily cause confusion. The regulations comprise four articles: (1) Any school established with donations under the name of a religious organization must comply with the regulations for private schools. If establishing various tutoring schools or community schools, it must be done according to the regulations determined by the Ministry; (2) Religious organizations wishing to spread their religion through institutions for enrollment “must not use the names of schools within the educational system”; (3) Religious organizations gathering to study doctrines or other academic matters “may proceed according to the procedures for academic associations”; (4) “Items 1 and 3 above must be reported to education administrative authorities for registration or record, while Item 2 is subject to local government jurisdiction and does not need to be reported.”

  1. Law on Supervising Charitable Organizations, 1929 (Zhu Baochen (朱寶琛) 1930: [Wu] 98-99)

In June 1929, the National Government promulgated the “Law on Supervising Charitable Organizations,” defining charitable organizations as “groups aimed at providing relief, disaster assistance, elder care, and other services to support the young and underprivileged.” It stipulated that “charitable organizations must not use their activities for religious propaganda, or engage in private profit-oriented enterprises.”

  1. Church Hospitals and Doctors Must Comply with Chinese Laws (Zhu Baochen (朱寶琛) 1930: [Wu] 96-97; Zhao Guangjun (趙廣軍) 2014: 101)

The National Government issued the “Midwives Regulations” (1929), “Provisional Regulations for Physicians” (1929), “Western Medicine Regulations” (1930), and “Licensing Measures for Foreign Physicians” (1931), to manage national health services. At that time, many missionaries engaged in medical practice to spread their faith in China, and these regulations placed restrictions on the qualifications of foreign physicians and required registration. In 1929, the central government believed that hospitals run by churches or foreigners in the provinces should be returned to their respective churches for management but required that doctors and nurses in church hospitals must comply with Chinese laws.

  1. Documents from the Kuomintang Central Department of Public Training Regarding the Formulation of Guidelines for the YMCA, 1929-1930 (China Second Historical Archives 1994: 1023-1026)

On October 28, 1929, the Kuomintang Central Department of Public Training proposed that various local youth organizations belong to a “people’s group,” whose nature is to use religion as a guise for cultural aggression.In recent years, “often using seemingly plausible yet misleading religious theories to distort or misinterpret our Premier’s teachings… allowing such propaganda to continue without intervention would significantly undermine our party’s objectives during the training governance period.” Therefore, “it is necessary to have local party branches participate in the guidance.” Regarding the guidance of the Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA): (1) A circular orders provincial and municipal party branches to instruct county and city party branches to send personnel to provide guidance whenever a local YMCA is established; (2) Local party branches must notify the YMCA of all meetings in advance, so that party representatives can attend to offer guidance or take the opportunity to promote party principles; (3) Any YMCA meeting must follow the ceremonial procedures for public organizations, including reading the Premier’s legacy instructions, displaying the party and national flags, and singing the party song.

On February 10, 1930, Chen Lifu (陳立夫), Secretary-General of the Kuomintang Central Secretariat, replied to the Central Training Department, noting that after the central committee’s 68th regular meeting, members Sun Ke (孫科), Wang Zhengtong (王正廷), and Kong Xiangxi (孔祥熙) had reviewed the matter and offered the following opinions: (1) The YMCA should accept “the guidance of our party”; (2) The YMCA belongs to a “religious and international group, which is fundamentally different from other public organizations that are guided by local party branches,” making the application of “direct guidance methods” to the YMCA “inappropriate and likely to cause various unforeseen disputes,” hence the necessity for separate regulations; (3) These regulations should be jointly established by the Central Training Department and the National YMCA before being submitted to the Central Committee for a decision.

In June 1930, Yu Wenxian (余文銜), a section chief of the Central Training Department, met with Yu Rizhang (余日章), the Secretary-General of the National YMCA Association. Yu agreed to the observance of a Premier Memorial Week by various local YMCAs and notified local party branches to participate in important meetings. Yu suggested that party branches could send representatives to attend YMCA meetings and provide guidance or speeches at member assemblies and other public gatherings, with YMCA activities also reported to local party branches. Yu Rizhang felt that there were too many meetings at various levels of the YMCA and that the party branches would find it burdensome, thus the emphasis should be on major meetings. The two sides could not reach a consensus. On August 19, the Public Training Department reported that discussions had yielded no results, and decided in the executive meeting that “arrangements would be made after military pacification.”

  1. Chinese Communist Party’s Christian Policy (Zhao Guangjun (趙廣軍) 2014: 114, 121-122)

In June 1923, at the Third National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), it was stated that the current task of the CCP was to “invalidate all unequal treaties imposed by imperialist powers in China… limiting the establishment of churches, schools, factories, and banks by foreign countries or individuals in China.” In January 1925, at the Fourth National Congress of the CCP, the resolution on the “Peasant Movement” indicated that “in areas affected by Christian and Catholic missionary forces, farmers should be especially encouraged to oppose the occupation of agricultural lands by churches and to resist collusion between churches and local bullies that oppresses the common people.”

In November 1927, the CCP’s party platform on land issues stated that “the Chinese Communist Party believed that to fundamentally reform China’s land system, it was essential to eradicate all the collapsed and chaotic relationships of the old society,” thus advocating that to address peasant issues and land matters, it was necessary to confiscate “the land held by temples and churches.”

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Chen Duxiu (陳獨秀). “Jidujiao yu Zhongguoren” (Christianity and the Chinese). Xin Qingnian (New Youth) February 1920 (Zhang Qinshi (張欽士) 1928: 37-51).

In February 1920, Chen Duxiu published an article titled “Christianity and the Chinese” in Xin Qingnian (新青年), pointing out that Christianity already had many followers in China, “occupying a portion of our spiritual life… impacting our practical life.” Therefore, “the issue of Christianity is a major problem that should be studied in Chinese society.”

First, Chen does not deny the “sins” of Christianity in oppressing scientists and free thinkers during the European Middle Ages, but this still does not negate that one of the “sources” of European culture is Christianity. The Christian “creation theory” and “doctrine of the Trinity” have already “bankrupted” in the face of natural sciences, yet as a “religion of love,” Christianity has not “fundamentally collapsed.” “The fundamental doctrine of Christianity is simply faith and love; everything else is merely branches and leaves.” Whether believers or opponents, no one can overlook this fundamental doctrine.

Next, he organized ten reasons why Christianity faced opposition in China: (1) There are more eaters of the doctrine than believers, leading to social contempt; (2) Governments use missionary efforts as a weapon of aggression, provoking resentment among the Chinese; (3) The Chinese respect the concept of sanctity and exclusion, rejecting Jesus; (4) The “officials’ infatuation” of the Chinese, who only value princes and officials, while the Bible records interactions with fishermen and the sick; (5) Officials who fawn on foreigners anger the people, while officials who respect sanctity anger the believers; (6) Proper missionaries advocate for the human rights of believers, inciting resentment from officials and jealousy from the people; deviant missionaries who shelter immoral individuals expand church influence and provoke public hatred; (7) The conflict between Christian doctrines and the Chinese practice of ancestor worship and other idolatries; (8) The vernacular translations of the Bible are not as elegant as the Four Books and Five Classics; (9) A lack of education among the Chinese leads to many baseless rumors; (10) The Catholic Church’s mysterious attitude incites rumors. Chen Duxiu pointed out that the main problem lies in the “mistakes” of the Chinese people, while the missionaries have “a couple of mistakes” that could be corrected as well. He once again called for the “Masters who respect sanctity and defend the path” to calmly study Christianity and not “recklessly oppose it.”

Regarding the issue of Christianity, Chen proposed the need for “profound awareness,” cultivating Jesus’ “great personality and passionate, profound emotions” within our blood, rescuing us from the degeneration of coldness, darkness, sullied conditions, and pits. He noted that the root of the Chinese people’s degeneration stems from a lack of super-material spiritual impetuses and emotions. The “faith” and “love” of Christianity directly address these shortcomings. In Chen Duxiu’s view, Jesus’ character represents a spirit of noble sacrifice, a formidable spirit of forgiveness, and an egalitarian spirit of fraternity, all sufficient to correct the deficiencies in the national character of the Chinese people.

Chen Duxiu reiterated the need to abandon the “creation theory,” “doctrine of the Trinity,” and various supernatural “old beliefs,” seeking a “new faith” that embodies “Jesus’ noble, great character, and passionate, profound emotions.” “Those ancient, unreliable legends, and associations should not be believed; even all modern trivial theologies and formal doctrines lack the importance of Jesus’ character and emotions.”

  1. Xing Fuzeng (邢福增). “Jidujiao ruhe hui ying xin sichao de taolun” (How Christianity Responds to New Thought Discussions). 2021 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增): 2020: 356-362).

From May 5 to 10, 1921, the Chinese Continuing Committee held its ninth annual meeting, where two days were specifically dedicated to discussing new thought, reflecting the significance placed on this matter by the conference. On the second day of the meeting, Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) (Chair of the Theology Department at Yanjing University, core member of “Life”), P. Frank Price (畢來思) (Systematic Theology Professor at Nanjing Theological Seminary), John L. Childs (柴約翰) (secretary of the Beijing YMCA), and Luo Yunyan (羅運炎) (editor of “Xinghua Bao”) each spoke from different angles about the relationship between new thought and Christianity.

Liu Tingfang’s speech, titled “The Responsibility of Christian Missionaries in the New Culture Movement,” pointed out that the influence of the new culture movement “has already spread nationwide,” and that “now both those who oppose it and those who support it have been influenced.” Christianity should regard the challenges as an opportunity for “self-verification” and “self-evaluation” provided by the times. Christianity must accept the baptism of the new culture movement and respond appropriately, helping Chinese intellectuals understand Christianity while also examining its own shortcomings to correct its “superstitious nature.” In this “movement for the reform of the Chinese Christian Church,” all missionaries must be “prophetically self-aware” and fully commit to the following tasks: (1) Cross-check their own faith; (2) Review the habits of the church; (3) Abandon dogmatic attitudes, preaching truth with a spirit of free discussion; (4) Promote “social gospel”; (5) Preach a positive “personal gospel.”He particularly hoped that the church’s “long-term ministers” would emphasize the “habit of research” and a “critical attitude” to address church issues. Clearly, Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) adopted an appreciative and accepting stance towards the new culture movement, viewing it as an opportunity for Christian reform.

Another speaker, Luo Yunyan (羅運炎), held similar views. He firmly believed that there “cannot be any absence of conflict” between Christian faith and new thought, yet they “depend on each other as elements contributing to the truth.” Christians, facing a “faithless world,” must provide a “reasonable explanation” when preaching to enable people “to move from doubt to research and from research to faith.” For this reason, Christians should “examine” the doctrines of new thought, accept its “test,” and then explain the Bible with reasonable methods to clarify the “truth” of the scriptures. This is the approach Luo advocated for “utilizing the new culture movement to assist in promoting the Kingdom of God.”

In contrast, the two foreign missionaries did not embrace new thought as fully as Liu and Luo. P. Frank Price (畢來思) reported on “The Present Intellectual Awakening and Its Bearing upon the Christian Church.” He was particularly concerned about whether responding would lead to “completely abandoning Christian doctrine in favor of non-Christian philosophers.” He firmly believed that Christianity must take on a “leading responsibility” to “guide, constrain, caution, encourage, and advise the new culture movement.” Following that, Price pointed out that the “opportunity” of the new culture movement is the “freedom of moral choice,” but it also carries the “danger” of “unlimited indulgence” and “hopeless skepticism.” However, “even with various dangers, we must welcome the movement for seeking freedom.” The key lies in how Christianity should respond. Price believed that the most important response was to “turn the enemies of Christianity into friends and disciples of Jesus Christ.” Convincing a leader of the new culture movement is worth more than a thousand debates.

John L. Childs (柴約翰) explicitly expressed dissatisfaction with new thought, deeming it “arrogant,” noting that the movement contains various assertions while lacking “formal organization.” At the same time, he stated that new thought has neither produced “new social theories,” “new political opinions,” nor “new religious insights,” but is merely an imported “foreign product.” Nevertheless, since Childs had more contact with the youth association and students, he could not deny that both Christian and non-Christian students, in church schools or national schools, were deeply influenced by the movement. Particularly, many Christian student organizations have already been impacted by new thought. Childs believed that the organization and faith of the church, as well as church leaders, would increasingly feel the “pressure” brought about by the movement.

While critiquing, Childs also argued that Christianity should “understand this movement.” He proposed not to overlook the common elements between Christianity and new thought, including the appeal for the transformation of Chinese society, breaking down old civilization, critically examining existing systems, advocating for scientific spirit, promoting self-government and freedom, and upholding the principle of service with fraternity and rational living. Based on this, Childs further believed that the nature of new thought is not only “not opposing Christianity,” but broadly aligns “with Christianity.” He emphasized that the opposition of new thought to Christianity is merely the result of critiquing tradition and old authority and power, reflecting that those who have freed themselves from old “power constraints” are not easily submissive to new authorities. Thus, new thought only “opposes certain coercive interpretations of Christianity,” rather than “the central doctrines of Christianity.” Christians should “welcome their rigorous scrutiny of Christianity,” allowing new thought to provide Christianity with “vital vitality” and the “energizing effect.”

From the speeches of the four individuals, it is evident that the two national Christians (Liu Tingfang and Luo Yunyan) had a more open attitude towards the new culture movement, advocating for Christianity to adapt to new thought and respond effectively. In contrast, the two missionaries (P. Frank Price and John L. Childs) held both critical and understanding stances, advocating to respond to the movement from a Christian perspective.

On the third day of the annual meeting, discussions continued on the issue of new thought, and five discussion directions were proposed: (1) What impact does new culture have on women? (2) What methods should we use to help Christian missionaries and believers to always understand the truth of the new culture movement, facilitate its progress, eliminate misunderstandings, and enhance mutual benefit? (3) What methods should we adopt to assist the new culture movement and all leaders affected by it, so that they can understand Christian doctrine?(4) Xin wenhua yundong, jili wo men qu shenme taidu, dui fu zhinan jiaozhu zhuzhijia de wenti? (What attitude should we take towards the issue of Chinese Christian leaders in the new culture movement?) (5) Wo men dui yu xin wenhua yundong, yingdang you shenme wenzi de gongxian, you duoshao wenzi de gongxian? (What written contributions should we make to the new culture movement, and how many written contributions?)

In summary, the discussions during the annual meeting of the Chinese Continuing Committee regarding new thought reflect that Christianity has recognized the challenge posed by the new culture movement cannot be avoided. However, upon careful observation and analysis, Christian individuals still have different attitudes; some advocate that Christianity should accept the scrutiny of new thought and adapt with innovative reforms, while others assert that Christianity needs to guide new thought.

  1. Yu Shaonian Zhongguo Xuehui de duihua (Dialogue with the Young China Society) (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2020: 355-356)

Starting in December 1920, the Young China Society held several lectures in Beijing and Nanjing regarding religious issues, featuring speakers such as Zhou Zuoren (周作人), Wang Xinggong (王星拱), Liang Shuming (梁漱溟), Li Yuying (李煜瀛), Tu Xiaoshi (屠孝實), and Lu Zhiwei (陸志韋). The content, along with other articles, was compiled into a special issue titled “Religious Issues,” which was published by the society’s magazine, Young China (少年中國), in three installments in 1921, showcasing the basic attitudes of the intellectual community toward religion. The topics covered included the definition and origin of religion, the functions and pros and cons of religion, and particularly whether New China needs religion.

In February 1922, the magazine Life (生命) invited more than a dozen intellectuals from the new culture movement to write about their impressions of Christianity. Eventually, responses were received from Zhou Zuoren, Zhang Dongsun (張東蓀), Gao Yihan (高一涵), Hu Shi (胡適), and Qian Xuantong (錢玄同). Life published these responses in a special issue titled “Attitudes of the Chinese Intellectual Community Toward Christianity” in the March issue and reprinted Chen Duxiu’s (陳獨秀) earlier article “Christianity and the Chinese.” The positions of these individuals clearly presented the attitude of the intellectual community towards Christianity. For example, Zhou Zuoren expressed that “while reading the Bible, he felt that the spirit of Christianity is very good.” The “spirit” he referred to was “love.” He also hoped that Christianity could “embrace” the spirit of science and not transform itself into “the Emperor of Heaven in a suit,” becoming a “hindrance to free thought.” Although Zhang Dongsun believed that religion was unnecessary, he nonetheless thought that Christianity had its merits compared to Confucianism, as it could “correct Chinese habits.” Gao Yihan recognized Christianity’s historical successes but maintained that science would ultimately replace religion.

Hu Shi, a central figure in the new culture movement, mentioned that religion consists of three components: moral teachings, theological theories, and superstition. Christianity must “abandon” the “superstition” it has held for two thousand years while also “discarding” the “theological principles” developed since the Middle Ages. As for moral teachings, “some parts can be preserved.” If Christianity could “remove” superstition and theology, its moral teachings would be “especially bright”; otherwise, morality itself would also be “dragged down with them.” At the same time, Hu called on the Chinese intellectual community to adopt a “tolerant” attitude towards Christianity based on the principle of religious freedom, recognizing and allowing freedom of preaching, as well as clarifying the distinctions between “blessing” and “curse,” and “brilliant” and “dross.” Indeed, during Hu’s early years studying in the United States, he participated in the summer camp of the North American Christian Association for Chinese Students, allowing him to engage with Christianity. Although he retained a favorable view of Christian ethics, he was critical of Christianity as a religious institution, even deeming it superstition and ignorance. He further argued that religion must undergo the baptism of scientific civilization to progress with the times. This belief also seemed to be a common recognition among the intellectual community regarding Christianity.

  1. Zhonghua jidujiao jiaoyu hui dongshi hui nianhui guanyu jiaohui xuexiao zhuzhu lianan de jueding an 1925 (Zhu Youhuan 朱有瓛, Gao Shiliang 高時良 1993: 786-787)

In April 1925, the annual meeting of the Board of the Chinese Christian Education Association passed the following resolutions regarding the registration and establishment of church schools: (1) “Christian schools should promptly register with local or central government; however, special functions of Christianity should not be restricted by registration”; (2) The significance of registering Christian schools includes: (a) The national regulations clearly state that elementary school graduates have the right to vote for local administrative officials or representatives; university graduates have the right to elect national representatives and take national exams for studying abroad. “If church schools sever ties with the government, they are essentially harming the future of their graduates”; (b) The relationship between registration and the church, church schools, and churches: “The church should not claim special rights based on unequal treaties, as this is not a long-term solution. All students receiving church education”naturally sympathize with and trust in the cause of church education, which is the church’s greatest support”. Graduates of church schools have the right to participate in political activities. The future of Christian schools and churches “lies entirely in the attitude of Chinese citizens.” By registering with the government, “students will enjoy civil rights and the benefits of being voters, taking national exams, and serving public society.”“Overall, the registration of church schools is of mutual benefit to church schools, churches, and Chinese students alike.”

  1. Youguan Sun Zhongshan zongjiao xinyang de zhenglun 1928 (Debate on Sun Yat-sen’s Religious Beliefs) (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1997: 350-361)

In 1928, a debate erupted between Christians and members of the Kuomintang regarding how to interpret Sun Yat-sen’s religious beliefs. On February 19, the Min Guo Ri Bao (民國日報) published an article by the non-Christian Yuan Yeyu (袁業裕) titled “A Study of the Attitude of the Three People’s Principles towards Christianity,” which acknowledged Sun Yat-sen as a Christian. However, he argued that when Sun “invented the Three People’s Principles, he abandoned religious faith in favor of belief in the principles he created.” He even cited a statement where Sun advocated for party members to replace religious beliefs with the Three People’s Principles as a basis for discussion: “The reason religion can influence people is that it has a doctrine that encourages belief. If ordinary people believe in the doctrine, they can… be willing to die for it. For this reason, missionaries often struggle for their faith, even at the cost of their lives. Therefore, the power of religion is even greater than that of politics. Our Kuomintang wants to revolutionize Chinese politics by implementing the Three People’s Principles and the Five Powers Constitution. Our principles are more practical than religious ones, as religious principles discuss future matters and matters beyond this world. Our political principles address current issues and those matters that affect humanity deeply. Religion seeks the happiness of the soul, while politics seeks the happiness of the physical body. Discussing the future of the soul is naturally closer to emptiness; talking about the present physical body is inherently concrete.” Yuan questioned why Sun Yat-sen emphasized that the Three People’s Principles “are more practical than religious principles.” Doesn’t this prove that the former is superior to the latter?

In response, Christian Zhang Shizhang (張仕章) wrote an article titled “Mr. Sun Yat-sen’s Religious Beliefs” on March 8 and 9 in “Jue Wu” (覺悟), meticulously extracting references to Christianity from Sun’s letters, notes, private conversations, speeches, political platform, and even his will to demonstrate that Sun Yat-sen had “strong faith in Christianity,” thereby nullifying the claim that “party and religion cannot coexist.”

In reply to Zhang Shizhang’s arguments, the non-Christian Zhang Zhenzhen (張振振) quickly wrote a response titled “The Whole Nature of Sun Yat-senism—Refuting ‘Mr. Sun Yat-sen’s Religious Beliefs’” questioning what methods should be used to understand “Sun Yat-senism.” He defined the scope of this ideology: “The emergence of an ideology does not fall from the sky; it is naturally connected to the historical background of the time. Mr. Sun’s historical context is the most suitable for the creation of ‘Sun Yat-senism.’ Therefore, in this historical background, Mr. Sun is a leader creating ‘Sun Yat-senism,’ and many others, such as Mr. Hu Hanmin and Mr. Zhu Zhixin, also made significant contributions to this ideology. Strictly speaking, ‘Sun Yat-senism’ is shaped by the zeitgeist, global trends, and the conditions of China… It cannot be misconstrued by an individual’s partial past actions. Thus, accepting the ‘wholeness’ of ‘Sun Yat-senism’ is an important premise for discussing the relationship between Sun Yat-sen and Christian faith that is beyond dispute. Otherwise, Communists could claim”Mr. Sun’s Marxist belief,” nationalists could claim “Mr. Sun’s national belief,” and anarchists could claim “Mr. Sun’s anarchist belief.” This way, the ‘wholeness’ of ‘Sun Yat-senism’ fragments apart. Fragmenting Mr. Sun’s beliefs into hundreds of versions is the same as distorting them!” Zhang Zhenzhen also ridiculed those who “split apart the essence of Christianity while fixating on ‘Mr. Sun Yat-sen’s religious beliefs’” using a “side-attack method.”

  1. Guomin geming chongji xia ge di jidujiao ge xin yundong (Christian Reform Movement under the Impact of the Nationalist Revolution) (Xie Jinhui 協進會 1928: 22-67)

The editorial team of the 1928 Yearbook of the Chinese Christian Church summarized the macro situation of the Chinese church in 1927 as follows: “As the sixteenth year of the Republic approaches, it is indeed a tumultuous epoch for China. The Dingzi Incident has dealt a blow to the Christian church, which is comparable to the aftermath of the Boxer Rebellion, but even more profound and serious. The current state of this blow includes the occupation and destruction of churches, schools, hospitals, and housing, the murder and expulsion of both Chinese and Western workers; work has consequently been halted. Externally, public sentiment is heated and irrational, using the banner of ‘nationalism’ to undermine the people’s external admiration; it considers Christianity as a form of foreign cultural invasion, hence aiming to overthrow both imperialists and the Christian church, thus closely linking the two with each other.”This issue of the Yearbook of the Chinese Christian Church not only reports on the impacts faced by churches across various regions but also features a new chapter titled “Various New Initiatives within the Church,” detailing the responses of churches in places such as Hangzhou, Wuhan, Harbin, and Hubei. It includes the “Declarations of the Reform Movement” from various churches: the Declaration of the Christian Reform Movement in Changsha, the Declaration of the Christian Reform Movement in Chengdu, the Chinese Christian Reform Declaration from Shuoxian, the Suzhou Christian Declaration, the Kaifeng Christian Declaration, the Zhenhai Chinese Christian Declaration, the Declaration of the Yunnan Chinese Christian Federation, the Declaration of the Hengyang Chinese Christian Federation, the Declaration of the Hangzhou Chinese Christian Federation, the Declaration of the Shanghai Chinese Christian Conference, the Declaration of the Liangshan Christian Church, the Declaration of the Shanxi Huofen Chinese Christian Church, the Declaration of the Xiangtan Christians’ Self-Organized Campaign, the Jiangxi Annual Conference Declaration of the Methodist Church, the 1927 Declaration of the Hunan and Hubei Conference of the Chinese Christian Church, and the Declaration of the Chongqing Chinese Christian Association, among others.

The editor noted: “In recent years, due to environmental pressures, the internal dynamics of the church have undergone changes. The tendencies of these changes reflect new attempts, and the future is difficult to predict. There are countless declarations regarding these new movements, and it is impossible to compile them all in one collection, so we have chosen to include only the more significant ones, knowing there are surely omissions; however, they are generally similar in nature, with only minor differences.” The phrase “generally similar” can be summarized as: (1) Supporting the national revolution; (2) Supporting the Kuomintang and the Three People’s Principles; (3) Opposing unequal treaties; (4) Opposing imperialism; (5) Supporting the self-reliance of Chinese Christianity; (6) Supporting the registration of church schools; (7) Affirming the patriotic and salvific spirit of Christianity.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. Cooperation with Russia and the First Nationalist-Communist Alliance 1924 (Guo Tingyi 郭廷以 1983: 533-541; Lü Fangshang 呂芳上 1989)

After the Xinhai Revolution, the Kuomintang (Guomindang, 國民黨) was unable to challenge the rule of the Beiyang warlords due to a lack of sufficient military strength. Following the failure of the “Second Revolution” in 1913, Sun Yat-sen’s (孫中山) revolutionary efforts fell into a slump. Although there were deep contradictions within the Beiyang warlords, they maintained actual control over China due to their military power and financial resources. Faced with the internal weakness and financial difficulties of the Kuomintang, Sun Yat-sen began seeking external support. However, Western countries like Britain and the United States, in order to protect their privileges and interests in China, preferred to see China remain divided rather than unified. Sun Yat-sen advocated for the overthrow of the Beiyang warlords and the establishment of a strong central government, contradicting the foreign powers’ strategy of controlling China through weak regimes. Thus, foreign powers tended to maintain the status quo and were unwilling to support the revolutionary forces represented by Sun Yat-sen. British and American leaders believed that Sun Yat-sen’s revolutionary movement lacked a realistic foundation, and preferred to continue cooperating with the Beiyang government rather than risk backing the Kuomintang.

As for Soviet Russia, following the October Revolution of 1917, the Bolshevik regime sought to promote a global communist revolution. In 1921, the Communist International actively intervened in Chinese politics, supporting the establishment of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) while also establishing ties with the Kuomintang to promote cooperation between the two parties. Sun Yat-sen believed that Soviet aid could help bolster the Kuomintang’s strength to challenge the Beiyang warlords, which facilitated the Kuomintang’s pro-Soviet policy.

In 1922, Sun Yat-sen met with Soviet representative Adolf Joffe (越飛) to discuss issues of cooperation with Russia and the inclusion of communists. In August, founding members of the CCP such as Chen Duxiu (陳獨秀) and Li Dazhao (李大釗) joined the Kuomintang. By September, the Kuomintang became a member of the Cooperative Commission, with Chen and Li serving as commissioners. In January 1923, Sun Yat-sen and Joffe issued a public statement: the Soviet Union was willing to sponsor China’s unification and grant complete freedom, asserting that the Soviet system should not be applied in China. Later, Joffe further indicated that the Soviet Union was willing to provide a loan of 2 million yuan, as well as dispatch military advisors to assist the Kuomintang in training its armies. In May, Sun requested the assistance of Communist International representative G. Maring (馬林) to help plan the restructuring of the party, introducing the Soviet model to reform the party’s organizational structure. For Sun Yat-sen, the Kuomintang needed to transform into a party organized in accordance with the Soviet-style, where both the military and the populace accepted the party’s leadership, understood revolutionary ideology, and were willing to sacrifice for the cause of revolution to succeed. In June, the CCP held its Third National Congress and acknowledged the Kuomintang as the central force of the national revolution. In October, Soviet representative Michael Borodin (鮑羅廷) arrived in Guangzhou, promising Sun Yat-sen military material assistance and drafting a plan for the restructuring of the Kuomintang.

In January 1924, the Kuomintang convened its First National Congress, officially allowing members of the CCP and the Socialist Youth League to join the Kuomintang, implementing the policies of pro-Soviet cooperation and supporting the workers and peasants. The congress declaration pointed out that the turmoil in China was caused by the collusion of imperialism and warlords, and that henceforth, the focus would be on resisting imperialism, marking the beginning of the “First Nationalist-Communist Alliance.”

Although some within the Kuomintang expressed dissent regarding the policy of pro-Soviet cooperation, objectively, the Kuomintang did indeed strengthen itself through restructuring, enhancing organizational methods, propaganda techniques, and mobilization capabilities to meet the demands of a revolutionary party amid rising nationalist sentiments. Notably, with the aid from the Soviet Union, the Whampoa Military Academy was established in 1924, with Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石) serving as the principal, becoming an important foundation for the National Revolutionary Army’s Northern Expedition.

As the revolutionary movement developed, internal divisions between left and right factions emerged within the Kuomintang: the left supported peasant and worker movements, while the right became increasingly dissatisfied with the influence of the CCP. Following the establishment of the Nationalist-Communist alliance, these internal contradictions quickly became evident. After CCP members joined the Kuomintang, they vigorously promoted the peasant and worker movements, raising concerns and discontent among the Kuomintang’s right faction.

After Sun Yat-sen’s passing in 1925, on April 12, 1927, Chiang Kai-shek launched the “April 12 Incident” in Shanghai, forcibly eliminating the Communist influence within the Kuomintang, marking the rupture of the First Nationalist-Communist Alliance. However, the left faction within the Kuomintang, represented by Wang Jingwei (汪精衛), still centered in Wuhan, opposed Chiang Kai-shek’s personal dictatorship, leading to the “Ninghan Split” scenario.

  1. Anti-Imperialism and Nationalism 1924 (Wang Rongzu 汪榮祖 1994: 188-192; Guo Tingyi 郭廷以 1983: 544-545; Wang Jianwei 王建偉 2011: 148-199)

Since the May Fourth Movement, the most prominent aspect of Chinese nationalism has been anti-imperialism. The theory of imperialism, originating from Lenin’s (列寧) assertions, was widely applied in China following the establishment of the Communist Party of China. After the Kuomintang adopted the policy of pro-Soviet cooperation, anti-imperialism also became a rallying cry for them.In the declaration of the First National Congress held in January 1924, it stated that since the end of the Qing Dynasty, China had fallen into a “semi-colonial status,” and after the overthrow of the imperial system, the “counter-revolutionary authoritarian class” had reconciled with imperialism, leading to the “first failure of the revolution.” “Since the Xinhai Revolution until now, the situation in China has not only seen no progress, but has also been on the decline. The tyranny of warlords and the erosion by foreign powers have both intensified, plunging China deeper into the hell of semi-colonialism. This is what the people across the country lament, and it is what those with insight seek day and night as they urgently wish to find a way out for the people.” The “nationalism” within the Three Principles of the People must aim to “eliminate the aggression of imperialism.” The principle of civil rights states that “any individual and group that truly opposes imperialism shall enjoy all freedoms and rights.” As for the principle of people’s livelihood, it emphasizes “equalizing land rights” and “restricting capital,” inevitably focusing on the liberation of peasants and workers. To promote the peasant and worker movements, one must engage in “resistance against imperialism and warlords, and against the special classes that harm farmers and workers in order to achieve the liberation of farmers and workers.”

In the “Northern Expedition Declaration of the Kuomintang” issued in September 1924, it pointed out that “the purpose of the National Revolution is to create an independent and free nation in order to uphold the interests of the country and the people.” The declaration noted that over ten years since the establishment of the Republic, China still harbored “the legacy of authoritarian thought,” including Yuan Shikai’s (袁世凱) claim to the throne, Zhang Xun’s (張勳) restoration, and the warlords’ division of territory. The reason “counter-revolutionary” forces still existed was due to “the protection of imperialism.” Therefore, the objective of the Northern Expedition was “not only to overthrow the warlords, but especially to dismantle the imperialism upon which the warlords depend for survival.” Only by completely eliminating the roots of counter-revolution could “China escape its semi-colonial status and establish a free and independent nation.” Regarding the situation, it advocated for convening a National Assembly to seek unity and construction, abolishing all unequal treaties with foreign nations, reclaiming concessions, and achieving self-governance and civil rights domestically.

After the May 30th Incident in 1925, the Kuomintang issued several declarations reaffirming their opposition to imperialism and the abolition of unequal treaties as well as restoring national independence and international equality. Following the establishment of the Guangzhou National Government in July 1925, Foreign Minister Hu Hanmin (胡漢民) issued a “Statement to the People of All Nations,” clearly requesting to establish equal status with all countries, asserting that only by abolishing the unequal treaties could the nation be “cleared.”

Thus, anti-imperialism became the highest revolutionary principle of the reorganized Kuomintang, revealing a distinct anti-imperialist stance. In short, “anti-imperialism” was not merely a slogan or demand unique to the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). However, some scholars have pointed out that although “anti-imperialism” was a common stance of both parties, there was no unified understanding of what constituted “imperialism” or how to oppose it, leading to three prominent contradictions: “slogans and actions” (the gap between party propaganda and the actual capabilities of the state), “national revolution and world revolution” (the Kuomintang more straightforwardly opposed foreign invasion, while the CCP also defended Soviet interests when opposing imperialism), and “individual and overall” (the Kuomintang advocated for differentiated treatment, primarily viewing Britain as the main enemy, while the CCP demanded that all foreign powers in China be seen as a collective imperialism to be toppled). Furthermore, the Kuomintang’s emphasis on “anti-imperialism” was targeted at overthrowing warlords and establishing a unified state, focusing on reclaiming concessions and abolishing unequal treaties as a secondary goal. Once implemented into political practice, the “anti-imperialism” slogan would undergo changes. Concerning the abolition of unequal treaties, prior to 1927, neither the Kuomintang nor the CCP was the central government, so their demands for treaty abolition could not be realized. After the establishment of the Nanjing National Government, Chiang Kai-shek’s approach toward handling unequal treaties in foreign policy was, in fact, similar to the “revision” of the earlier Beiyang government.

  1. The Training Period of the Three Principles of the People’s Construction 1927 (Shanghai Law Studies Translation Society 上海法學編譯社 1931: 139-149, 3160-162, 174-176)

In March 1929, the Kuomintang convened its third National Congress, marking the first party representative meeting held in its capacity as the ruling party in China since the establishment of the National Government in 1927. In the declaration issued at this congress, it noted that with the completion of the purge of the party and the Northern Expedition, China was entering the “training” phase, and the most important future work would be the construction of “Three Principles of the People.” “In regard to the national environment, unless there is an urgent effort to realize the construction of the Three Principles of the People, there is no way to eliminate the aspirations of imperialism and curtail the power of feudal forces, and thereby achieve the goal of China’s national independence and freedom.”

The report from the second Central Executive Committee of the Third National Congress indicated that the current party affairs focus on “striving for internal party unity, national unification, and the commencement of the training period.”Particularly, the public movement issues were reviewed from three perspectives: (1) The past public movements were “purely to awaken the masses,” and once the masses were mobilized, “the masses themselves were unable to… the organizers of the public movement were also unable to assist them,” resulting in “placing the masses in a state of reckless unrest and riots”; (2) The past public movements only “focused on the organization of the masses,” without taking into account “the organization of all the people based on their social survival needs,” which resulted in “a portion of the minority becoming a movement of the masses, instead of seeing a movement of a portion of the masses supporting the organization of the majority”; (3) The methods and organization of public movements implemented during the military period were not perfect and were unsuitable for the training period. Since “the past work focused on revolutionary destruction, the future work will be based on revolutionary construction.” Therefore, the literacy, afforestation, sanitation, and civil defense movements initiated by the second Central Executive Committee were partial remedies for the aforementioned three shortcomings.

At the same time, four guidelines were proposed for future public movements: (1) Public movements must start from the people’s “social survival needs,” thereby “creating organized people”; (2) Given the existing substantial organizations among national farmers and workers, “assistance from our party is necessary”; (3) Future public movements “must focus on supporting rural education, rural organizations, cooperative movements, and instilling new agricultural production methods” as primary tasks; (4) Regarding youth movements, efforts should be made to “develop autonomy within schools, implement universal physical training for both genders, promote gatherings and organizations in science and arts, and reward practical scientific research and inventions.”

Furthermore, in terms of government policy, the education section emphasized that “the center must be the culture fostering the Three Principles of the People,” specifically through “integrating the strengths of Eastern and Western cultures with the spirit of the Three Principles of the People, so that all citizens possess the practical functions of the Three Principles in ‘the people’s lives, social survival, national livelihood, and the lives of the masses,’ achieving the goals of national independence, universal civil rights, and development of the people’s livelihood.” To this end, it was necessary to “correct the laissez-faire errors from past education and replace them with state educational policies,” defining “the standards of knowledge, capability, and moral character required by the state from the training period to the constitutional government,” thereby improving the educational system to cultivate the citizens and talents needed by the country.

It is evident that since the national government established its capital in Nanjing in 1927 and began the training period, there was a transition from an opposition revolutionary party to a ruling revolutionary party. The primary threat it faced was not “anti-imperialism” but rather “suppressing communism,” while also reinforcing the Kuomintang’s (國民黨) position of power. Against this backdrop, the Kuomintang adjusted its public movement policies from “revolutionary destruction” to “revolutionary construction,” and sought to prevent the CCP (中國共產黨) from utilizing public movements to threaten its ruling foundation. This adjustment helped to alleviate large-scale non-structured movements. However, the Kuomintang’s emphasis on education (Three Principles of the People-based education) reflects its prioritization of ideology, thus giving rise to challenges regarding how Christianity would relate to the Three Principles of the People.

In summary, for the Kuomintang, the development of Chinese nationalism in the 1920s shifted from a preventive struggle against imperialism to an integrated national construction centered around the Three Principles of the People.
