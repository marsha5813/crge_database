China: Context (1920-1930)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. 〈教育部訓令第138號〉1921（朱有瓛、高時良1993：783）

  2. Ministry of Education Instruction No. 138 (1921) (Zhu Youhuan & Gao Shiliang 1993: 783)

In April 1921, the Ministry of Education issued six regulations concerning the registration of missionary secondary schools:

“The name of the school must be prefixed with the term private.”

“Secondary schools must be administered in accordance with the Secondary School Ordinance and its Implementation Regulations.”

“Subjects and curriculum standards for secondary education must be followed as prescribed. If any modifications are deemed necessary, a justification must be provided and submitted to the relevant provincial educational authority for approval by the Ministry of Education. However, Chinese language and national geography courses are not subject to alteration.”

“The content and methods of instruction must not contain proselytizing elements.”

“All students within the school, regardless of religious belief, must be treated equally.”

“Any school found to be in violation of the above regulations shall not be granted registration. If violations occur after registration, the registration may be revoked.”

  1. 〈教育部佈告第16號〉1925（朱有瓛、高時良1993：784）

  2. Ministry of Education Bulletin No. 16 (1925) (Zhu Youhuan & Gao Shiliang 1993: 784)

In November 1925, the Ministry of Education issued six regulations regarding the establishment of schools funded by foreign donors:

“All schools of any level established with foreign donations must apply for official recognition from the education authorities in accordance with the rules set forth by the Ministry of Education.”

“The name of the school must be prefixed with the term private.”

“The principal of the school must be a Chinese national. If the current principal is a foreigner, a Chinese person must be appointed as vice-principal, who shall act as the legal representative when applying for recognition.”

“If the school has a board of directors, Chinese nationals must hold the majority of seats.”

“The school must not have religious propagation as its purpose.”

“The curriculum must conform to standards set by the Ministry. Religious subjects may not be included as required courses.”

  1. 〈浙江省教育廳禁止學校宣傳宗教令〉1925（朱有瓛、高時良1993：784-785）

  2. Zhejiang Provincial Department of Education Order Prohibiting Religious Propaganda in Schools (1925) (Zhu Youhuan & Gao Shiliang 1993: 784–785)

On May 1, 1925, the Zhejiang Provincial Department of Education issued an order stating:

“The doctrine of ‘theosophy’ is bizarre and unfounded, and in fact inhibits intellectual development.” As “schools are institutions for cultivating talent,” “they must not propagate religion, as this impedes educational progress.” It was therefore specifically stipulated that: “Religious propaganda in schools is prohibited.”

  1. 〈私立大學及專門學校立案條例〉及〈私立中等學校及小學立案條例〉1927（大學院1928：26-31）

  2. Regulations for the Registration of Private Universities and Specialized Schools and Regulations for the Registration of Private Secondary and Primary Schools (1927) (University Council, 1928: 26–31)

On December 20, 1927, the Regulations for the Registration of Private Universities and Specialized Schools and the Regulations for the Registration of Private Secondary and Primary Schools were promulgated. These regulations stipulated that such schools must be registered through the University Council of the Republic of China, via the educational administrative authorities of the respective provinces or regions. Both sets of regulations specified that:

“The principal must be a Chinese national.”

  1. 〈私立學校條例〉1928（朱有瓛、高時良1993：786）

  2. Private School Regulations (1928) (Zhu Youhuan & Gao Shiliang 1993: 786)

In February 1928, the University Council issued the Private School Regulations, which included the following provisions:

1“All schools established by individuals or organizations are considered private schools; this includes schools established by foreigners or religious missions.”

2“Private schools are subject to the supervision and guidance of educational administrative authorities.”

3“The principal of a private school must be a Chinese national.”

4“Private schools may not designate religious courses as required subjects, and no religious propaganda may be conducted within class time.” “If religious rituals are conducted at private schools, students must not be compelled to participate.”

  1. 〈私立學校董會條例〉1928（朱有瓛、高時良1993：786）

  2. Regulations for the Boards of Directors of Private Schools (1928) (Zhu Youhuan & Gao Shiliang 1993: 786)

Also in February 1928, the University Council promulgated the Regulations for the Boards of Directors of Private Schools. Article 12 stated:

“In special circumstances, foreigners may be appointed as members of the board of directors; however, the number of foreign members must not exceed half of the total. The chairperson of the board or the head of the board of directors must be a Chinese national.”

  1. 國民政府對於信教自由之通令 1928（邢福增1997：350-352，361）

  2. Nationalist Government General Order on Religious Freedom (1928) (Xing Fuzeng 1997: 350–352, 361)

In January 1928, two Christian members of the Kuomintang (KMT), Zhang Zhijiang (a National Government Council member and Military Advisory Councilor) and Niu Yongjian (Governor of Jiangsu Province), submitted a proposal to the Central Political Council advocating for the implementation of religious freedom and the abolition of slogans opposing Christianity and other religions.

In their proposal, they argued that Christianity had absolutely no connection to imperialism. Instead, Christianity was rich in “revolutionary meaning,” as its doctrines taught believers about “equality, fraternity, and freedom,” values that had inspired revolutions in Britain, America, and Germany. They noted that many comrades of the Chinese revolution—including Sun Yat-sen and members of the Northwestern Army—had been moved by Christian teachings and had worked tirelessly to “save the nation and its people.” Without the influence of Christianity, they wrote, “how could the deeply ingrained traditions of monarch-subject ethics, cultivated over millennia, have been suddenly replaced by the advocacy of nationalism and livelihood?”

Furthermore, Christianity emphasized “moral transformation” and “the relief of the suffering lower classes,” making it inherently incompatible with imperialism and therefore unfit for indiscriminate attacks. They also pointed out that the anti-Christian slogans originated from the Communist Party’s hostility toward Christianity. Since the KMT was then conducting a purge of the Communists (the qingdang), it was only logical to eliminate Communist ideology as well. Thus, the anti-Christian slogans should be abolished, and religious freedom implemented—thereby honoring the “lifelong sincerity of the late Premier’s [Sun Yat-sen’s] faith.”

The proposal was discussed at the 128th meeting of the Central Political Council on February 15, 1928, and was referred to the Standing Committee of the Central Party Department for further deliberation. On February 26, the KMT Central Executive Committee responded, stating that “as a resolution had already been passed last year, there was no need to deliberate again.” They reaffirmed the decision of May 13, 1927, which stated that the public “must not misunderstand the anti-imperialism movement as having xenophobic or anti-religious characteristics, nor use any form of power to oppress or infringe upon the freedom of belief of either Chinese or foreign nationals.”

  1. 教會在中國內地租用土地房屋條例 1928（趙廣軍2014：101）

  2. Regulations on the Leasing of Land and Property by Churches in the Interior of China (1928) (Zhao Guangjun 2014: 101)

In July 1928, the Nationalist Government promulgated the Regulations on the Leasing of Land and Property by Churches in the Interior of China. According to the regulations, foreign churches were permitted to lease land or purchase buildings in China in order to establish hospitals or schools, “as permitted by treaties between the respective country and China,” and could do so in the name of the church.

The land and property in question were required to comply with all current or future Chinese laws and tax regulations. Churches had to submit a joint application with the property owner to the local authorities for approval. The quantity and size of the land or property leased or constructed must not exceed the actual needs of the church. The property could not be used for profit or commercial purposes; otherwise, the local authorities were authorized to reject the application or cancel the agreement.

(9)〈私立學校規程〉1929（朱寶琛1930：[伍]96-97）

  1. Private School Regulations (1929) (Zhu Baochen 1930: [Vol. 5] 96–97)

In September 1929, the Ministry of Education issued updated Private School Regulations, stating in Article 5:

“Private schools established by religious organizations must not designate religious subjects as required courses, nor may religious propaganda be conducted during class hours. If religious rituals are held within the school, students may not be forced or persuaded to participate. Furthermore, no religious ceremonies may be conducted during school hours.”

  1. 教育部宗教團體興辦教育事業辦法（朱寶琛1930：[伍]98）

  2. Ministry of Education Regulations for Religious Organizations Engaged in Educational Activities (1929) (Zhu Baochen 1930: [Vol. 5] 98)

In April 1929, due to the dual nature of religious organizations’ involvement in education—either “donating funds to establish schools for talent cultivation,” or “gathering followers to study and propagate doctrine,” which often led to confusion—the Ministry of Education issued a four-article directive to clarify distinctions:

Religious organizations that donate funds under their name to establish schools within the national education system must comply with private school regulations. If they establish supplementary or public schools for the masses, they must follow the Ministry’s corresponding regulations.

Religious organizations seeking to promote their doctrines by establishing institutions for student recruitment “may not use names belonging to any official level within the national school system.”

Religious organizations forming associations to study doctrine or other academic subjects “may do so according to procedures for academic associations.”

“Both items (1) and (3) must be submitted to the educational administrative authorities for registration or filing,” while item (2) “shall be managed by local government authorities and does not require submission.”

  1. 監督慈善團體法 1929（朱寶琛1930：[伍]98-99）

  2. Law on the Supervision of Charitable Organizations (1929) (Zhu Baochen 1930: [Vol. 5] 98–99)

In June 1929, the Nationalist Government promulgated the Law on the Supervision of Charitable Organizations, defining such organizations as “those engaged in food relief, disaster aid, elderly care, support for orphans, and other undertakings for aiding children.” The law stipulated: “Charitable organizations may not use their activities for religious propaganda or engage in profit-seeking business operations.”

  1. 教會醫院醫師服從中國法令（朱寶琛1930：[伍]96-97；趙廣軍2014：101）

  2. Church Hospital Physicians Must Abide by Chinese Law (Zhu Baochen 1930: [Vol. 5] 96–97; Zhao Guangjun 2014: 101)

Between 1929 and 1931, the Nationalist Government issued a series of laws to regulate the national medical system, including the Midwifery Regulations (1929), Provisional Physician Regulations (1929), Western Medicine Regulations (1930), and Regulations on the Certification of Foreign Doctors (1931). Many missionaries had used medical practice as a vehicle for evangelism in China. These new laws imposed restrictions on the qualifications of foreign physicians practicing in China and required registration.

In 1929, the central government determined that church- or foreign-run hospitals across provinces should be considered charitable institutions and should be returned to direct church management. However, it also mandated that physicians and nurses working in church hospitals must abide by Chinese laws.

  1. 國民黨中央民眾訓練部關於制定指導基督教青年會辦法的文件1929-1930（中國第二歷史檔案館1994：1023-1026）

  2. KMT Central Department of Popular Training’s Policy on Guiding YMCA Activities (1929–1930) (Second Historical Archives of China 1994: 1023–1026)

On October 28, 1929, the KMT Central Department of Popular Training proposed that local YMCAs, being “mass organizations,” in practice “used religion as a guise for cultural imperialism.” In recent years, they had “frequently misinterpreted or distorted the teachings of our Late Premier [Sun Yat-sen] with seemingly plausible religious arguments.” Allowing such unchecked propaganda would “betray the Party’s aim of training the entire population during the period of political tutelage.” Thus, it was deemed necessary that local KMT branches participate in guiding YMCA activities.

The proposed guidance measures included:

Issuing instructions to all provincial and municipal party branches to direct their local sub-branches to assign representatives to guide local YMCA chapters.

Requiring local YMCAs to notify local party branches in advance of any meetings; party branches would then send representatives to attend and offer ideological guidance or promote party principles.

Requiring all YMCA meetings to follow protocols for mass organization meetings, including reading the Premier’s testament, displaying the party and national flags, and singing the party anthem.

On February 10, 1930, KMT Central Secretariat Secretary-General Chen Lifu responded to the Popular Training Department. After review by Sun Ke, Wang Zhengting, and Kong Xiangxi during the 68th Standing Committee meeting, the following points were raised:

The YMCA should indeed “accept the Party’s guidance.”

However, the YMCA, being “a religious and international organization,” differed significantly from other mass groups directly organized under KMT leadership. “Direct intervention” would be “inappropriate and likely to cause numerous unexpected disputes,” and an alternative approach was needed.

Any such alternative should be negotiated jointly between the Popular Training Department and the National YMCA before submission to the Standing Committee for approval.

In June 1930, after a meeting between Popular Training Section Chief Yu Wenxian and National YMCA General Secretary Yu Rizhang, Yu agreed that YMCA branches would hold memorial events for Sun Yat-sen and notify local party branches about important meetings. Yu also suggested that while party representatives could attend committee meetings and speak at major public gatherings, the volume of internal YMCA meetings was too high for party oversight of all. The two sides failed to reach a consensus.

On August 19, the Department reported that talks had not yielded results, and the matter was deferred with the note: “To be addressed once military pacification is achieved.”

  1. 中共基督教政策（趙廣軍2014：114、121-122）

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. 陳獨秀〈基督教與中國人〉1920（張欽士1928：37-51）

  2. Chen Duxiu, “Christianity and the Chinese People,” 1920 (Zhang Qinshi 1928: 37–51)

In the February 1920 issue of New Youth, Chen Duxiu published an article titled “Christianity and the Chinese People,” in which he observed that Christianity already had a considerable number of adherents in China, “occupying a part of our spiritual life… and influencing our actual lives.” Therefore, “the issue of Christianity is a major issue that Chinese society ought to study.”

First, Chen did not deny Christianity’s “crimes” in medieval Europe, such as oppressing scientists and free thinkers. Nonetheless, this could not obscure the fact that Christianity was one of the “sources” of European culture. Although doctrines like “creationism” and the “Trinity” had already “collapsed” in the face of advances in natural science, Christianity had not “fundamentally disintegrated” as a “religion of love.” “The fundamental doctrine of Christianity is simply faith and love; everything else is secondary.” Regardless of whether one is a believer or a critic, one cannot ignore this core teaching.

Chen then outlined ten reasons why Christianity faced opposition in China:

There are more people who benefit from the Church than genuinely believe in it, leading to social contempt.

Foreign governments used missionary work as a tool of aggression, arousing Chinese resentment.

The Chinese idea of honoring sages and resisting foreigners leads to rejection of Jesus.

The Chinese are obsessed with official rank and revere nobility, whereas the Bible depicts Jesus interacting with fishermen and the sick.

Officials who pander to foreigners anger the public, while those who venerate saints provoke the believers.

Righteous missionaries who defend believers’ rights anger officials and incite public jealousy, while corrupt missionaries who shelter wrongdoers to expand their churches earn the people’s hatred.

Christian doctrine clashes with Chinese practices of ancestor worship and idolatry.

The vernacular Bible (Old and New Testaments) lacks the elegance of the Four Books and Five Classics.

The low level of education among the Chinese has led to many baseless rumors.

The mystical practices of Catholicism provoke suspicion and rumor.

Chen Duxiu argued that the root of these ten reasons lay mainly in the Chinese people’s own “errors”; the missionaries, for their part, had made only “one or two mistakes,” which had more or less already been corrected. Thus, he again called on the “gentlemen who revere the sages and defend the Way” to approach Christianity with calm and rational study, rather than oppose it with “unreasoning hostility.”

Regarding the Christian question, Chen called for a “profound awakening,” so that the “noble character of Jesus and his passionate, deep emotions may be cultivated in our blood, rescuing us from our fall into coldness, darkness, filth, and the pit.” He pointed out that the source of Chinese moral decay lay in the lack of spiritual impulse and emotional depth beyond the material realm. Christianity’s core values—“faith” and “love”—were precisely what could counter these deficiencies.

In Chen Duxiu’s view, the personality of Jesus exemplified a noble spirit of sacrifice, great forgiveness, and universal love—qualities that could remedy the flaws in the Chinese national character.

Chen reaffirmed that we must abandon “creationism,” the “Trinity,” and various “supernatural” elements of the “old faith,” and instead seek a “new faith” centered on “Jesus’s noble, great personality and his passionate, deep emotions.” “Those ancient, unreliable legends and allegories need not be believed; even the trivial and nihilistic theology and formal doctrines of today are not as important as Jesus’s character and emotion.”

  1. 基督教如何回應新思潮的討論 1921（邢福增：2020：356-362）

  2. How Christianity Responded to the New Thought Movement (1921) (Xing Fuzeng 2020: 356–362)

From May 5 to 10, 1921, the China Continuation Committee held its Ninth Annual Conference. Of the six-day conference, two full days were dedicated to discussions of the New Thought Movement, highlighting the event’s significance. On the second day, four individuals gave presentations on the relationship between Christianity and the new thought: Liu Tingfang (Dean of Theology at Yenching University and editor of Life), P. Frank Price (Professor of Systematic Theology at Nanjing Theological Seminary), John L. Childs (secretary of the Peking YMCA), and Luo Yunyan (chief editor of Xinghua News), each offering perspectives from different angles.

Liu Tingfang’s talk, titled “The Responsibility of Christian Missionaries in the New Culture Movement,” pointed out that the influence of the movement had “already spread across the nation.” “Whether one supports or opposes it, all are now under its influence.” Christianity, he argued, should see these challenges as an opportunity for “self-verification” and “self-examination.” It should accept the baptism of the New Culture Movement and respond appropriately. The Church must both help Chinese intellectuals understand Christianity and examine its own shortcomings—especially by correcting its superstitious elements. In this movement of “Christian Church reform in China,” all missionaries must show “prophetic awareness” and fully commit to the following tasks:

Carefully examine their own faith,

Review church customs,

Abandon dogmatism and preach the truth with a spirit of free discussion,

Promote the “social gospel,”

Proclaim a positive “personal gospel.”

Liu especially called on church elders and pastors to value “research habits” and a “critical attitude” in addressing church issues. Clearly, Liu Tingfang took a welcoming and appreciative view of the New Culture Movement, seeing it as a catalyst for Christian reform.

Luo Yunyan shared a similar view. He firmly believed that while conflicts between Christian faith and the new thought were “inevitable,” the two were nonetheless “interdependent and together form essential components of truth.” When facing a “faithless world,” Christians must offer a “reasonable explanation” of their faith to guide people “from doubt to inquiry, and from inquiry to belief.” Therefore, Christians should “study” the new thought and accept its “testing” while using rational methods to interpret Scripture, thereby illuminating its truth. This was Luo’s approach: to “use the New Culture Movement to help advance the Kingdom of God.”

In contrast, the two foreign missionaries were more reserved. P. Frank Price delivered a report titled “The Present Intellectual Awakening and Its Bearing upon the Christian Church.” He was especially concerned that, in responding to the movement, Christians might end up “abandoning Christian principles entirely in favor of non-Christian philosophies.” He believed that Christianity should instead take up the “guiding role” to “direct, restrain, warn, encourage, and advise the New Culture Movement.”

Price acknowledged the “opportunity” of the movement—namely, the “freedom of moral choice”—but he also warned of the “danger” of “unbridled indulgence” and “hopeless skepticism.” Nevertheless, he concluded, “even with all these dangers, we must still welcome the movement’s quest for freedom.” The key question, he said, was how Christianity should respond. Price believed the best response was to “turn enemies of Christianity into friends and disciples of Jesus Christ.” “To inspire a single leader of the New Culture Movement,” he claimed, “is worth more than a thousand arguments.”

John L. Childs, on the other hand, openly expressed dissatisfaction with the New Thought Movement, calling it “arrogant.” He criticized it for lacking “formal organization” and claimed it did not produce any “new social theories,” “new political views,” or “new religious insights”—being merely a “foreign import from the West.” However, due to his close contact with students at the YMCA, Childs had to admit that both Christian and non-Christian students—whether in church-run or public schools—were profoundly influenced by the movement. In particular, many Christian student groups had already come under its sway. Childs believed that church organizations, church teachings, and leaders would increasingly feel the “pressure” brought by the movement.

While critical, Childs also proposed that Christianity should “understand this movement.” He urged people not to ignore the similarities between Christianity and the new thought, including their shared calls to reform Chinese society, break with old civilization, critically examine current institutions, uphold scientific spirit, promote democracy and freedom, emphasize service based on universal love, and advocate for a rational life. Based on this, Childs argued that the New Thought Movement was “not opposed to Christianity” and that its core elements were actually “compatible with Christianity.” He emphasized that the opposition toward Christianity was actually a rejection of traditional authority and power. The new thought leaders, having shaken off the shackles of old power, were unwilling to easily submit to new authority. Therefore, the movement was “opposed to certain coercive interpretations of Christianity,” not “the central doctrines of Christianity.” Christians should “welcome their rigorous scrutiny of Christianity” because the New Culture Movement brings “vital energy” and a “reviving effect” to the Church.

From the presentations of the four speakers, it is evident that the two Chinese Christians—Liu Tingfang and Luo Yunyan—adopted a more open stance toward the New Culture Movement and advocated that Christianity should adapt and respond effectively. In contrast, the two missionaries—P. Frank Price and John L. Childs—adopted a more critical yet understanding position, advocating a Christian response that maintained the faith’s doctrinal integrity.

On the third day of the conference, discussions on the New Thought Movement continued. The assembly proposed five key discussion points:

What impact has the new culture had on women?

What methods can we use to help Christian missionaries and believers understand the nature and progress of the New Culture Movement, eliminate misunderstandings, and enhance mutual benefit?

What methods can we use to help the leaders influenced by the New Culture Movement understand the essence of Christianity?

What attitude should we take, inspired by the New Culture Movement, toward the issue of Chinese Christian leadership?

What written contributions should we make to the New Culture Movement—and how much?

In summary, the China Continuation Committee’s discussions of the New Thought Movement revealed Christianity’s recognition that the challenges posed by the New Culture Movement could no longer be ignored. However, a closer analysis shows a diversity of views among Christians: some advocated that Christianity should accept the scrutiny of new thought and reform itself to meet the times, while others insisted that Christianity should instead offer guidance to the New Thought Movement.

  2. Dialogue with the Young China Association (Xing Fuzeng 2020: 355–356)

Beginning in December 1920, the Young China Association organized a series of lectures on religious issues in Beijing and Nanjing. Speakers included Zhou Zuoren, Wang Xinggong, Liang Shuming, Li Yuying, Tu Xiaoshi, and Lu Zhiwei. The contents of these lectures, along with other related articles, were compiled into a special issue titled “Religious Questions”, which was published across three issues of the association’s journal, Young China, in 1921. This publication reflected the basic stance of the intellectual world toward religion at the time. Topics addressed included the definition and origins of religion, its functions and pros and cons, and especially the question of whether the new China needed religion.

In February 1922, the Christian magazine Life invited over ten intellectuals associated with the New Culture Movement to write about their impressions of Christianity. Responses were ultimately received from Zhou Zuoren, Zhang Dongsun, Gao Yihan, Hu Shi, and Qian Xuantong. Life compiled their responses into a special feature titled “The Chinese Intellectual World’s Attitudes Toward Christianity”, which was published in its March issue, alongside a reprint of an older article by Chen Duxiu, “Christianity and the Chinese People.” These responses clearly revealed the intellectuals’ attitudes toward Christianity.

For example, Zhou Zuoren remarked, “When casually reading the Bible, I find the spirit of Christianity to be quite good.” The “spirit” he referred to was “love.” Zhou also hoped that Christianity could “embrace” the scientific spirit, and not turn itself into a “Western-suited Jade Emperor” or become an obstacle to “free thought.”

Zhang Dongsun, although believing that religion was not necessary, still felt that compared with Confucianism and Buddhism, Christianity had its merits and could “correct certain Chinese habits.”

Gao Yihan affirmed Christianity’s past historical success but believed that science would ultimately replace religion.

Hu Shi, a central figure in the New Culture Movement, argued that religion consisted of three components: moral teachings, theological theory, and superstition. Christianity, he insisted, must “abandon” the “superstitions” of the past two thousand years, as well as the “theological doctrines” inherited from the Middle Ages. As for its moral teachings, “some parts can be preserved.” If Christianity could “eliminate” superstition and theology, then its moral message would “shine all the more brightly.” Otherwise, even its morality would be “dragged down with it.”

At the same time, Hu Shi urged Chinese intellectuals to adopt a stance of “tolerance” toward Christianity, in the spirit of religious freedom, and to recognize and allow its freedom to preach. He also called for a more nuanced understanding of its “blessings” and “evils,” its “brilliance” and “dross.”

Indeed, during his early years studying in the U.S., Hu Shi had participated in summer conferences of the North American Chinese Christian Student Association, giving him direct exposure to Christianity. While he retained a positive impression of its ethical teachings, he was often critical of Christianity as an institutional religion, viewing it as superstitious and ignorant. Furthermore, he believed that religion must undergo the baptism of scientific civilization to keep pace with the times. This, it seems, was a common view of Christianity among China’s intellectuals.

  2. The Debate over Sun Yat-sen’s Religious Beliefs (1928) (Xing Fuzeng 1997: 350–361)

In 1928, a public debate erupted between Christians and members of the Nationalist Party (KMT) over how to interpret Sun Yat-sen’s religious beliefs. On February 19, the supplement “Awakening” of the Republican Daily (Min’guo Ribao) published an article by the non-Christian Yuan Yeyu titled “A Study of the Attitude of Three Principles of the People (Sanmin Zhuyi) Supporters Toward Christianity.” While not denying that Sun Yat-sen had been a Christian, Yuan argued that once Sun had “formulated the Three Principles of the People, he abandoned religious belief and placed his faith in the ideology he had created.” Yuan cited a statement by Sun in which he advocated replacing religious belief among party members with belief in the Three Principles:

“The reason why religion can move people is because it has a kind of ideology that inspires belief. Ordinary people, once they believe in an ideology, … can even die for it. For this reason, missionaries often struggle for their faith, even to the point of sacrificing their lives. Therefore, the power of religion surpasses that of politics. The reason our Kuomintang wants revolution is to reform China’s politics and implement the Three Principles of the People and the Five-Power Constitution. Our ideology is more concrete than that of religion. Religious ideologies concern the future and the world beyond. Our political ideology addresses present-day issues that touch the real pain of humanity. Religion seeks the happiness of the soul; politics seeks the happiness of the physical body in the here and now. Speaking of the future of the soul is, by nature, somewhat empty; speaking of the needs of the physical body in the present is naturally grounded and verifiable.”

Yuan argued that Sun’s claim that the Three Principles were “more concrete than religious ideologies” proved that they were, in fact, superior.

In response, the Christian Zhang Shizhang—who had served as a translator for the Christian Literature Society, editor at the Wenshe Monthly, and editor for the National YMCA Literature Department—wrote an article titled “Mr. Sun Yat-sen’s Religious Beliefs,” published in “Awakening” on March 8 and 9. In it, he carefully compiled evidence from Sun’s letters, notes, private conversations, speeches, political platforms, and even his final words, all of which contained references to Christianity. Zhang sought to prove that Sun Yat-sen “had deep faith in Christianity”—thus rendering baseless the claim that “party ideology and religion cannot coexist.”

Responding directly to Zhang Shizhang’s argument, the non-Christian Zhang Zhenzhen published a counter-article titled “The Integrity of Sun Yat-senism: A Rebuttal to ‘Mr. Sun Yat-sen’s Religious Beliefs’.” He questioned the methodology used to understand “Sun Yat-senism” and defined its scope as follows:

“An ideology does not arise out of nowhere; it is shaped by its historical context. Dr. Sun’s historical context was precisely one that was ripe for the development of ‘Sun Yat-senism’. Therefore, he was not only the leader who developed this ideology, but many others also contributed to its creation—such as Mr. Hu Hanmin and Mr. Zhu Zhixin. Strictly speaking, ‘Sun Yat-senism’ was a fusion of historical circumstances, global trends, and China’s national condition. … It must not be distorted by interpreting it through isolated past actions of an individual.”

Zhang Zhenzhen thus asserted that accepting the integrity of Sun Yat-senism was a necessary precondition for any discussion of Sun’s relationship with Christianity. Without this, “Communists could claim that Sun believed in Marxism, nationalists could say he believed in statism, and anarchists could claim he believed in anarchism.” This would fracture the ideology into pieces: “To divide Mr. Sun’s beliefs into fragments is to reduce them into dozens or even hundreds of versions!” He further ridiculed the practice of “splitting apart Christianity and seizing on this so-called ‘religious belief of Mr. Sun Yat-sen’” as a mistaken and misleading approach.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. 聯俄容共與第一次國共合作 1924（郭廷以1983：533-541；呂芳上1989）

After the 1911 Revolution, the Kuomintang (KMT) lacked sufficient military strength to challenge the rule of the Beiyang warlords. Following the failure of the “Second Revolution” in 1913, Sun Yat-sen’s revolutionary movement fell into a period of stagnation. Although the Beiyang warlords were plagued by internal conflicts, they maintained effective control over China through their military power and financial resources. Faced with internal weakness and financial difficulties, Sun began to seek external support. However, Western powers such as Britain and the United States, aiming to preserve their privileges and interests in China, preferred to maintain a divided China rather than support unification. Sun’s call to overthrow the Beiyang warlords and establish a strong central government conflicted with the great powers’ strategy of dividing and controlling China through weak regimes. As a result, these powers favored the status quo and were unwilling to support revolutionary forces led by Sun Yat-sen. Britain and the United States viewed Sun’s revolutionary cause as lacking a realistic foundation and believed it was better to continue working with the Beiyang government than to risk supporting the KMT.

In contrast, Soviet Russia, following the October Revolution of 1917, sought to promote communist revolution globally. By 1921, the Comintern had begun to actively intervene in Chinese politics, supporting the founding of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and establishing contact with the KMT to promote cooperation between the two parties. Sun Yat-sen believed that Soviet assistance could help the KMT strengthen its forces and challenge the Beiyang warlords, which led to the adoption of a pro-Soviet policy.

In 1922, Sun met with Soviet representative Adolf Joffe to discuss the issues of alliance with the Soviet Union and toleration of communists. In August, CCP founders such as Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao joined the KMT; by September, they became members of the KMT’s Executive Committee. In January 1923, Sun and Joffe issued a joint communiqué in which the Soviet Union pledged to support China’s unification and freedom and recognized that the Soviet system was not applicable to China. Joffe later expressed the USSR’s willingness to provide a two-million yuan loan and to send military advisors to assist in training KMT troops. In May, Sun invited Comintern representative Maring (G. Maring) to assist in reorganizing the party based on the Soviet model. For Sun, the KMT needed to transform into a Leninist-style party in which both the army and the people were subject to party leadership, committed to revolutionary ideology, and willing to sacrifice for the cause—only then could the revolution succeed. In June, the CCP held its Third National Congress and recognized the KMT as the central force of the national revolution. In October, Soviet representative Michael Borodin arrived in Guangzhou and promised Sun military aid and a proposal for reorganizing the KMT.

In January 1924, the KMT convened its First National Congress, officially adopting policies of alliance with the Soviet Union, toleration of communists, and support for workers and peasants. The Congress declaration asserted that China’s turmoil was caused by the collusion of imperialism and warlordism, and henceforth the party would focus on resisting imperialism—a position later known as the First United Front between the KMT and CCP.

Although some within the KMT opposed the policies of alliance with the Soviet Union and toleration of the communists, the party’s reorganization objectively strengthened its capacity. In terms of organizational methods, propaganda techniques, and mobilization ability, the KMT now met the needs of a revolutionary party amid rising nationalist sentiment, significantly increasing its power. One notable outcome was the establishment of the Whampoa Military Academy in 1924 with Soviet assistance, with Chiang Kai-shek serving as its first commandant. The academy would later become a crucial foundation for the Northern Expedition launched by the National Revolutionary Army.

As the revolutionary movement progressed, internal divisions emerged within the KMT between the left and right wings. The left supported workers’ and peasants’ movements, while the right grew increasingly uneasy about the CCP’s expanding influence. After the CCP joined the KMT, it energetically promoted labor and peasant movements, provoking alarm and dissatisfaction among the KMT’s right wing.

After Sun Yat-sen’s death in 1925, Chiang Kai-shek launched the “April 12 Purge” in Shanghai in 1927, using military force to eliminate communist influence within the KMT, effectively ending the First United Front. However, the KMT’s left wing, led by Wang Jingwei, opposed Chiang’s personal dictatorship and established a rival center in Wuhan, leading to what became known as the “Ninghan Split.”

  1. 反帝國主義與民族主義 1924（汪榮祖1994：188-192；郭廷以1983：544-545；王建偉2011：148-199）

Since the May Fourth Movement, the most prominent feature of Chinese nationalism has been its anti-imperialist stance. The theory of imperialism, derived from Lenin’s ideas, was widely applied in China following the founding of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). After the Kuomintang (KMT) adopted its policy of alliance with the Soviet Union and toleration of the communists, anti-imperialism also became one of its most prominent slogans. In the declaration of its First National Congress in January 1924, the KMT stated that since the late Qing period, China had fallen into a “semi-colonial status,” and that after the fall of the monarchy, the “counter-revolutionary autocratic class” colluded with imperialist forces, leading to the “first failure of the revolution.” The declaration lamented that:

“Since the 1911 Revolution, China’s situation has not improved but has instead worsened steadily. The tyranny of warlords and the encroachment of foreign powers have intensified, plunging China ever deeper into the hell of semi-colonialism. This is what causes the people of the nation to groan in anguish and the enlightened to despair day and night, urgently seeking a path to survival for the Chinese people.”

Accordingly, the “nationalism” in Sun Yat-sen’s Three Principles of the People had to be directed toward eliminating imperialist aggression. The “principle of democracy” meant that all individuals and groups who truly opposed imperialism should enjoy full freedoms and rights. The “principle of livelihood” emphasized “equalization of land rights” and “regulation of capital,” inevitably prioritizing the liberation of peasants and workers. To advance peasant and labor movements, it was necessary to “resist imperialism and warlords, and oppose those privileged classes that harmed the interests of peasants and workers.”

In September 1924, the KMT’s Northern Expedition Manifesto stated that “the goal of the national revolution is to establish an independent and free state to protect national and popular interests.” The manifesto criticized the enduring legacy of autocracy even after the founding of the Republic, including Yuan Shikai’s self-declaration as emperor, Zhang Xun’s restoration attempt, and the continued power of regional warlords. It declared that the persistence of counter-revolutionary forces was largely due to “the protection and encouragement of imperialism.” Hence, the objective of the Northern Expedition was “not only to overthrow the warlords, but also to eliminate the imperialism on which the warlords depended for survival.” Only by thoroughly eradicating the roots of counter-revolution could China shed its semi-colonial status and become a truly free and independent nation. In terms of policy, the KMT advocated convening a National Assembly to pursue national unification and reconstruction, abolishing all unequal treaties, reclaiming foreign concessions, and implementing democracy and civil rights.

Following the May 30th Incident of 1925, the KMT repeatedly issued declarations reaffirming its opposition to imperialism, its call to abolish unequal treaties, and its demand for the restoration of national independence and international equality. After the establishment of the Guangzhou Nationalist Government in July 1925, Foreign Minister Hu Hanmin issued a “Message to the Peoples of All Nations,” explicitly demanding equal diplomatic status and stating that only through the abolition of unequal treaties could the country be “cleansed.”

Thus, anti-imperialism emerged as the highest revolutionary principle of the reorganized KMT, signaling a clear and assertive anti-imperialist stance. In short, “anti-imperialism” was not an exclusive slogan or cause of the CCP. However, scholars have pointed out that although both the KMT and CCP embraced anti-imperialism in rhetoric, they differed significantly in their understanding of what imperialism was and how it should be resisted. These differences can be summed up in three main contradictions:

Rhetoric vs. Action: A gap existed between party propaganda and the actual capacity of state power to implement anti-imperialist measures.

National vs. Global Revolution: The KMT’s opposition to imperialism was largely focused on resisting foreign invasion, whereas the CCP also aimed to defend Soviet interests as part of its broader international revolutionary goals.

Particularism vs. Universalism: The KMT tended to adopt a differentiated approach, viewing Britain as the primary enemy; the CCP, in contrast, regarded all imperialist powers in China as a collective threat to be opposed without exception.

Furthermore, for the KMT, the overarching goal of anti-imperialism was to overthrow the warlords and establish a unified state. The focus on reclaiming foreign concessions and abolishing unequal treaties was of secondary importance. When translated into actual political practice, the KMT’s anti-imperialist slogans often underwent significant dilution. Additionally, before 1927, neither the KMT nor the CCP held central governmental power, so their calls to abolish unequal treaties could not be implemented. After the establishment of the Nanjing Nationalist Government, Chiang Kai-shek’s foreign policy in handling the unequal treaties bore a close resemblance to the earlier “treaty revision” approach of the Beiyang Government.

  1. 三民主義建設的訓政時期 1927（上海法學編譯社 1931：139-149、3160-162、174-176）

“The Tutelage Period of Three Principles of the People–Based State Construction, 1927”:

In March 1929, the Kuomintang (KMT) convened its Third National Congress—its first as the ruling party of China since the establishment of the Nationalist Government in 1927. In the congress declaration, it stated that following the completion of the anti-Communist purge (qingdang) and the Northern Expedition, China had entered the “tutelage” phase (xunzheng). The most important task going forward was the “construction based on the Three Principles of the People.” The declaration emphasized: “Given the national conditions, unless we rapidly pursue the realization of Three Principles–based development, we will not be able to eliminate imperialist covetousness, curb the resurgence of feudal forces, or achieve the goals of national independence and freedom.”

In the party affairs report of the Second Central Executive Committee, which was passed at the congress, it was stated that current party work was focused on “promoting internal party unity, unifying the nation, and beginning the tutelage phase.” The report particularly reflected on three shortcomings in previous mass movements:

Earlier mass movements merely aimed to “awaken the people.” Once the masses were mobilized, “they themselves lacked the means to act, and the movement leaders also had no way to assist them,” resulting in “irrational and violent outbursts.”

These past movements only focused on organizing “a segment of the people,” rather than organizing society in a way that met the livelihood needs of the whole population. This meant that “a small portion of the population became the people’s movement,” rather than a broader effort in which “one segment of the masses helped support the organization of society for the majority.”

The methods and structures used during the military phase of the revolution were ill-suited to the new tutelage phase, since “the former efforts were aimed at revolutionary destruction, but the tasks ahead involve revolutionary construction.”

To address these failures, the Second Central Executive Committee initiated campaigns focused on literacy, reforestation, hygiene, and local security systems (baojia) as partial remedies.

Looking ahead, the report laid out four main principles for future mass movements:

Mass movements should be grounded in the “needs of the people’s social existence” and aim to “organize them into an effective citizenry.”

For the existing organizations of workers and peasants, “the Party must offer support.”

Future mass work should prioritize “rural education, rural organization, the cooperative movement, and the dissemination of new agricultural production methods.”

In terms of youth movements, the focus should be on “cultivating self-governance within schools, implementing universal physical education for both sexes, promoting scientific and cultural societies and publications, and encouraging the research and invention of practical sciences.”

In the area of educational policy, the party emphasized that education should center on the creation of a “culture rooted in the Three Principles of the People.” This involved integrating the best elements of Eastern and Western civilizations through the spirit of the Three Principles, so that all citizens could apply these ideals in “personal life, social existence, national livelihood, and collective vitality.” The ultimate goals were national independence, universal civil rights, and the development of people’s livelihood. To this end, it was necessary to “correct the past laissez-faire tendencies in education and replace them with a national education policy.” This meant establishing clear standards for the knowledge, skills, character, and morality required of citizens in both the tutelage and future constitutional phases. By doing so, the government could cultivate the kinds of citizens and talent needed by the state.

From this, we can see that after the Nationalist Government was established in Nanjing in 1927 and entered the tutelage period, the KMT transitioned from a revolutionary opposition party to a ruling revolutionary party. Its primary concern was no longer anti-imperialism, but rather the suppression of the Communist Party. At the same time, it sought to consolidate its rule. Within this context, the KMT adjusted its approach to mass mobilization, shifting from “revolutionary destruction” to “revolutionary construction” and aimed to prevent the Chinese Communist Party from using mass movements to destabilize its rule. This policy shift helped curb radical grassroots mobilizations.

Nonetheless, the KMT’s strong emphasis on education—especially its effort to nationalize education under the ideology of the Three Principles—also revealed its concern with ideological control. This, in turn, posed new challenges for Christianity in China, which now had to navigate its relationship with this dominant political doctrine.

In sum, for the Kuomintang, Chinese nationalism in the 1920s evolved from a defensive struggle against imperialism into an integrated project of state-building centered on the Three Principles of the People.
