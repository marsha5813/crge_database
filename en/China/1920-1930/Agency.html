China: Agency (1920-1930)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Hsu Pao Chien (徐寶謙) (Wu Liming 吳利明 1981: 171-222; Hsu Pao Chien 徐寶謙 1934: 44-50)

Hsu Pao Chien (徐寶謙, 1892-1944), also known by his courtesy name Liu Ji (字六吉), was from Shangyu, Zhejiang. He grew up in a traditional Confucian family and went to Shanghai for studies in 1905. Before he left, his uncle urged him: (1) do not join foreign religions; (2) do not join revolutionary parties; (3) do not cut your braid. While in Shanghai, he was heavily influenced by revolutionary thoughts, reading extensively from revolutionary publications. The following year, he transferred to Shaoxing Municipal Middle School, maintaining his enthusiasm for revolution. However, during his time at school, he became aware of the Confucian philosophy, particularly the Song-Ming Neo-Confucian interpretation. After graduating in 1910, he enrolled in the Beijing Taxation School, cutting off his braid as a sign of his alignment with the revolution. After the Wuchang Uprising in 1911, he witnessed the political landscape post-revolution, feeling a mix of hope and disappointment, leading to inner turmoil. At that time, he believed that China’s hope lay in the revival of Confucian spirit and even participated in activities organized by the Confucian Association. However, after personal involvement, he became disillusioned with the “politicization” of the association.

In 1912, after participating in activities at the Beijing Youth Association and hearing speakers discuss the prosperity of the West due to Christianity, he and his classmate Zhang Qinshi decided to privately study the Bible seriously starting in 1913, initiating his exploration of Christianity. In the spring of 1913, inspired by speeches from John R. Mott and Sherwood Eddy in Beijing, he decided to join a Bible study group. He then participated in the North China Government School Student Summer Conference, where he was moved and resolved to convert, listing reasons such as (1) the greatness of Jesus’ character; (2) the sincerity of believers’ words and actions; (3) the absence of true conflict between science and religion. He emphasized that converting to Christianity did not require abandoning Confucianism, as the two could “coexist without contradiction and complement each other.” He then attended services at the London Mission on Mi Shi Hutong in Beijing and sought guidance from Minister Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡). After Christmas in 1913, he was baptized by Cheng Jingyi.

In 1914, Hsu Pao Chien became the president of the Youth Association at the Taxation School, actively serving in the school while also assuming the presidency of the Social Progress Association. After graduating from the Taxation School in 1915, he turned down a high-paying customs job to work as a student officer at the Beijing Youth Association. In response to new ideologies and the May Fourth Movement, Hsu and Hu Xuecheng founded the magazine “Life Monthly” (《生命月刊》) in November 1919 in Beijing, and in early 1920, they established the “Beijing Preaching Group.” At that time, Hsu wrote in the Chinese Recorder describing their work as “Christian Renaissance” (or “基督教新思潮運動”).

In January 1921, he traveled to the United States to study at Union Theological Seminary and Columbia University, majoring in religious philosophy. After returning to China in 1924, he served as the executive officer of the Beijing School Business Association while also teaching at Yenching University. Starting in 1926, he taught full-time at Yenching. In 1930, he was granted a year off by Yenching University to serve at the World Student Christian Federation in Geneva and the World YMCA. In 1931, he returned to the U.S., taking classes at Union Theological Seminary and Columbia University while teaching. In 1932, he went back to Yenching to teach, obtaining his doctorate in 1933 with a thesis titled “Ethical Realism in Neo-Confucian Thought.”

Furthermore, in the face of Japanese aggression against China, he reflected on the narrow nationalism issues of the past, especially during his studies in America when he interacted with Japanese students, questioning the appropriateness of rejecting nationalism. Coupled with his involvement with the Love Only Society, he turned towards promoting pacifism and internationalism. Thus, he advocated for enlightened individuals from China and Japan to strengthen cooperation, promoting the “Chinese-Japanese Christian Conference” in Beijing in 1934.

Although Hsu Pao Chien found fulfillment in student work and teaching at Yenching, he increasingly felt detached from the suffering masses. He also realized the contradiction between private property and the spirit of Jesus, leading him to contemplate abandoning everything to share the hardships with the people and realize the ideal Christian society. However, due to family responsibilities, he felt pained to challenge the existing social system. In 1935, he decided to accept the position of general secretary in a rural experimental zone in Lichuan, Jiangxi Province, resolutely resigning from his teaching post at Yenching and committing himself to rural development work. In 1936, he published “Experiences in Rural Work” (《農村工作經驗談》). In 1937, disheartened, Hsu resigned and took up teaching positions at Hujiang University and Fudan University in Shanghai.

After the fall of Shanghai during the War of Resistance, Hsu moved to the rear in Chengdu to participate in editing the “Compilation of Classic Christian Works” translation project.The project was initiated in Chengdu in 1941 by Sun En San, Earl Cressy (葛德基), and Francis P. Jones (章文新), receiving support from the Board of Founders of Nanking Theological Seminary (金陵神學院託事部). In 1942, Hsu took charge of the editing. On January 2, 1944, while traveling from Chengdu to Chongqing, Hsu passed away due to a car accident at the age of 53.

  1. Leland Wang (王載) (Zha Shijie 查時傑 1983: 267-277; Li Jiafu 李佳福 2001: 65-83)

Leland Wang (王載, 1898-1975), courtesy name Liren (字歷人), was born into a non-Christian family in Fuzhou, Fujian. After graduating from Nanyang Public School in Shanghai in 1915, he initially attended the Wusong Merchant Marine School, followed by the Chefoo Naval College (煙台海軍學校). After graduation, he served on warships in Wusong, Nanjing, and other locations.

In 1916, he married Pan Shaorong in Fuzhou. At that time, Pan was studying at the South China Women’s School of the American Baptist Mission, where she attended Ruth Paxon’s revival meetings and converted to Christianity, writing to hope that Leland would also come to know the Gospel, but he remained hard-hearted. When preparing for their wedding, Pan insisted on a Christian ceremony, to which Leland agreed in order to marry her. Thus, the wedding was held at the chapel of the South China Women’s School. However, after their marriage, Pan refused to kneel before the Wang family ancestral tablets, greatly displeasing Wang’s family. Nevertheless, Leland complied with his new wife’s wishes and accompanied her to church for Sunday worship. Unexpectedly, he was moved by a hymn sung during the worship service titled “Closer to You, Lord.” Later, guided by his wife, Leland began reading the Bible and was deeply attracted to the “Sermon on the Mount” in the Gospel of Matthew. When he read about the redemption of the cross, he decided to accept the Gospel and receive baptism in November 1918. However, he later felt that the sprinkling baptism he had received did not align with biblical teachings, so he was re-baptized by immersion in January 1920 on the beach in Gulangyu, Xiamen.

On one occasion, while aboard a warship in Fuzhou at Luoxing Tower, one day he disembarked and followed the sound of singing to a church, where he met gatherings led by British missionary Margaret E. Barber (和受恩). Margaret E. Barber had been sent to Fuzhou by the Church of England in 1899 but had separated from the Anglican Church due to theological disputes over baptism and continued her mission as a free missionary in Fuzhou. From then on, she became a mentor in Leland’s faith journey.

In the spring of 1920, Leland began attending weekly Bible study gatherings hosted at the home of Ni Tuosheng. Their shared views on faith fostered a close relationship. As Leland pursued his faith more deeply, he began to feel a call to become a preacher. He struggled between his work in the navy and his commitment to ministry, one day reading Isaiah 52:11-12, which deeply moved him: “Depart! Depart! Go out from there! Touch no unclean thing; come out from it and be pure, you who carry the vessels of the Lord.” He firmly believed it was God’s calling. In the spring of 1921, Leland resolutely left the navy, returned to Fuzhou, and committed himself to work for the Lord. Without entering a theological seminary for training, he instead adopted a free missionary approach, going out in the mornings with gospel pamphlets, ringing a bell while singing hymns, and sharing gospel stories.

In the summer of 1922, Leland and his wife, together with Ni Tuosheng, held their first breaking of bread meeting at Leland’s home, and from then on, they began a weekly breaking of bread gathering every Sunday. Soon after, Leland moved to “Ziyuan” on Cangqian Mountain in Fuzhou, continuing to open his home for gatherings, with Leland serving as the Sunday preacher (if Leland was away for meetings, Ni Tuosheng would replace him). By the end of that year, attendance grew to over 20 people, mostly fellow students from Trinity College, Fuzhou. A group of enthusiastic young people preached in the city and countryside of Fuzhou.

As the number of attendees increased, the space at “Ziyuan” became insufficient. Leland also wished to expand rural preaching, so he moved to Yangqi Township, about ten miles from Fuzhou, to establish a new mission. However, they hoped for a larger space for formal gatherings, so they rented a building at “Shierlingpai.” With great zeal for evangelism and wearing gospel vests, they distributed pamphlets and sparked a revival of gospel work in Fuzhou. Between 1923 and 1924, six young people fully committed to full-time ministry, known as the “Six Gentlemen of Fuzhou”: Leland Wang, Ni Tuosheng, Wang Lianjun, Lu Zhongxin, Miao Shaoxun, and Wang Zhi (Leland’s younger brother).

However, in April 1924, a split occurred between Leland and Ni Tuosheng regarding the mission’s direction, ultimately leading to a division.The conflict between the two was as follows: (1) Leland Wang advocated for revival evangelism as a free preacher, conducting evangelistic work in various places, while Ni Tuosheng supported the establishment of “local churches”; (2) regarding the ordination of pastors, Leland began to agree with the necessity of ordaining pastors for the sake of the gospel, while Ni opposed it. Ultimately, Ni Tuosheng left Fuzhou.

In 1927, Leland was invited to speak at a spiritual conference in Guangzhou. In 1928, the two regions of Guangdong and Hong Kong began jointly hosting spiritual conferences, and Leland was invited to lead three times (in 1928, 1931, and 1932). Through the spiritual conferences, he became acquainted with the missionary R. A. Jaffray (瞿輔民), who invited him to participate in missionary work in Southeast Asia. In the winter of 1928, he traveled from Annam to Singapore and Malaysia, then entered the Indonesian archipelago in 1929, with his final stop being East Borneo. In 1929, Jaffray established the “South Seas Evangelical Band,” with Leland serving as its chairman. In October 1929, Leland led revival meetings in Shanghai, Hangzhou, Qingdao, Jinan, and Beijing, appealing to believers for support of the South Seas mission, which later changed its name to the “Chinese Overseas Evangelization Society.”

During the War of Resistance, Leland lived in Singapore and Indonesia, and thereafter devoted himself to evangelistic work in Southeast Asia, leading revival and evangelistic gatherings in various locations.

On December 13, 1975, Leland Wang passed away in California, USA, at the age of 77.

  1. Watchman Nee (倪柝聲) (Li Jiafu 李佳福 2001; Wu Xiuliang 吳秀良 2004; Liang Jialin 梁家麟 2003; Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2005)

Watchman Nee (倪柝聲, 1903-1972), originally named Shuzu (述祖), was born into a Christian family in Fuzhou, Fujian. His grandfather, Ni Yucheng, was the first Chinese pastor of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions in Fujian. His parents, Ni Wenxiu and Lin Heping, were ardent Christians, with his mother having a background in the American Baptist church. In 1916, Watchman Nee entered Trinity College (福州三一書院), which was operated by the Anglican Church. Although he grew up in this Christian environment and was baptized as a child by the American Baptists, he did not truly believe at that time.

In February 1920, evangelist Yu Cidu held a revival meeting at the Fuzhou Congregational Church, which not only revived his mother Lin Heping but also led Watchman Nee to reaffirm his faith and dedicate himself to becoming a preacher. Later, he was nurtured by missionary Margaret E. Barber (和受恩) and read the works of Jessie Penn-Lewis (賓路易師母) through her. In March 1921, he and his mother were re-baptized.

In 1922, Watchman Nee and Leland Wang began breaking of bread meetings in Fuzhou, which led to a revival there. By 1924, the two had disagreements over church policy and ordination issues, resulting in Nee leaving Fuzhou. He first served at the Ling Guang Newspaper in Nanjing for eight to nine months before going on to evangelize in Southeast Asia and Japan. After returning to China in May 1925, he lived at Luoxing Tower in Fuzhou and established the “Gospel Bookstore,” publishing the “Christian Reporter” (《基督徒報》). By 1926, he was evangelizing in Xiamen but had to go to Nanjing for treatment for tuberculosis, during which he completed “The Spiritual Man” (《屬靈人》).

In 1927, Watchman Nee moved to Shanghai, renaming the “Gospel Bookstore” to “Gospel Publishing House,” where he published “Revival,” “The Christian,” and “Sermon Records.” In January 1928, he rented a space on Haatong Road to begin meetings. In that year, “The Spiritual Man” was officially published by Gospel Publishing House. By this time, gathering places were being established across the country. Meanwhile, he also established connections with the Brethren movement in the UK, and in 1931, they sent representatives to visit the meetings in Shanghai, and in 1933, Nee was invited to visit the Brethren in the UK.

In October 1934, Watchman Nee married Zhang Pinhui, but faced public opposition from Zhang’s aunt, Zhang Meizhen, who announced her disapproval in a newspaper. This incident led Nee to retreat from public life for a year. In the autumn of 1935, while in Yantai, Shandong, during a charismatic gathering, he experienced a “pouring out of the Holy Spirit.” After conducting a special meeting in Yantai, he returned to Shanghai and Quanzhou to lead a special conference, resulting in another revival.

In 1938, he published “A Reconsideration of Work,” further establishing his view of the “local church.” In July, he set out on a visit to the UK, followed by a trip to Denmark in October. His speeches in Denmark were later compiled into “The Normal Christian Life.” He returned to Shanghai in July 1939 to lead a special conference.

At the end of 1939, he took over the “China Biochemical Pharmaceutical Company” from his brother, Ni Huaizhu. This decision led to dissatisfaction among his coworkers, who believed he had abandoned the church to run a business. Even his colleague Li Yuanru called on the elders of the Shanghai church to halt Nee’s ministry. Faced with internal pressure, Nee ultimately accepted expulsion in May 1943, marking his second retirement from ministry.Later, Nee explained that one of the reasons for operating the biochemical factory was to help solve the economic problems of preachers and their families, and to assist believers across various regions in overcoming financial crises. The profits from the factory could also be used for church ministry. (It was suggested that the real reason for his retirement was the exposure of his adultery with a female coworker.)

In April 1948, Watchman Nee returned to Shanghai to explain the situation regarding the biochemical factory, while still acknowledging his mistakes. At the same time, he led meetings for coworkers, restoring his leadership position. This was during the Chinese Civil War, and Nee proposed the “Give Up” and “Gospel Migration” movements as directions for the development of the meeting places. He also established a national training center for coworkers at Guling in Fuzhou, conducting two training sessions from May 1948 to August 1950.

After the founding of the People’s Republic of China, Nee’s stance shifted from opposition to active support for the Three-Self Movement. Within the meeting places, there emerged internal divisions regarding participation in the Three-Self Movement. In April 1952, Nee was arrested by the government in Northeast China on charges of economic crimes. However, by January 1956, the Chinese Communist Party dealt with the anti-Three-Self forces within the meeting places, declaring they had dismantled the “Watchman Nee Revolutionary Group.” In June, the Shanghai High People’s Court found him guilty of counter-revolutionary crimes, sentencing him to 15 years in prison and revoking his citizenship for 5 years. In January 1957, the Supreme People’s Court rejected his appeal, and he was transferred to Tilanqiao Prison in Shanghai to serve his sentence. In April 1967, after his prison term expired, Nee was moved to Qingdong Farm in the suburbs of Shanghai for labor reform. By the end of 1969, he was transferred to the Baimaoling Farm in northern Anhui, and in April 1972, upon his release, he was forbidden from returning to Shanghai or Beijing. In May, he moved to “Shanxia Pu” in Baiyun Mountain, where he passed away on May 31. He left a testament: “Christ is the Son of God, who died for our sins and rose on the third day. This is the greatest fact in the universe. I believe in Christ and die.”

  1. Y. C. James Yen (晏陽初) (Y. C. James Yen 晏陽初 1992: 492-555; Song Enrong 宋恩榮 1989: 3-6; Jing Meizhu 井梅珠 2000; Boorman 1968c: 52-54; Keehn 1993)

Y. C. James Yen (晏陽初, 1890-1990), also known as Xingfu and Yuchun, was a modern educator for the common people in China, born in 1890 in Bazhong County, Sichuan. His father was a Chinese teacher and a missionary with the China Inland Mission. In 1903, he left home to study at the Inland Mission school in Baoning, where he was baptized in 1904. He studied for two years at the Methodist Church’s Huamei High School in Chengdu in 1910 but left due to dissatisfaction with the school’s neglect of moral education and lack of Christian spirit. Later, he was introduced to James L. Stewart, an Anglican missionary in Chengdu, who was preparing to establish the “Furen Society.” Yen assisted in organizing activities for the society and served as an interpreter for Sichuan dialect for the missionaries. Stewart’s brother, Arthur D. Stewart, was the principal of St. Paul’s College in Hong Kong, and with his support, Yen left Sichuan in 1912 and began attending St. Stephen’s College in Hong Kong in January 1913. The following year, he was accepted into the University of Hong Kong, receiving an Edward VII Scholarship for his outstanding academic performance. Therefore, the urgent task was to promote popular education to enlighten the people’s minds and cultivate their democratic awareness and national consciousness.

Since over 85% of China’s population were farmers, the focus of popular education needed to shift towards rural areas. In 1926, the association began rural education experiments in Baoding (Ding County), Hebei. The experimental area utilized education in literature, livelihood, health, and citizenship, combining schools, society, and families with the aim of enhancing the literacy, productivity, health, and unity of farmers, thus nurturing a generation of “new citizens” while fundamentally eradicating the four great deficiencies: “ignorance, poverty, weakness, and selfishness.” A weekly publication called “Farmers’ Report” (《農民報》) was also published.

When the war broke out in 1937, Ding County was occupied, forcing the cessation of experimentation. Yen then moved the rural education movement to Hunan, continuing experiments in Western Hunan. In 1940, he established the “China Rural Construction Talent Institute” in Chongqing, serving as its director. After the victory in the war in 1945, the institute expanded into the “Rural Construction Academy,” becoming the center of the association’s work.

Yen’s efforts in the popular education movement led to an interview by Pearl S. Buck, which was compiled into the book “Tell the People: Talks with James Yen about the Mass Education Movement,” published in 1945. Buck called for the dissemination of Yen’s experiences in popular education in China to Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

In 1949, Yen left China, first going to Taiwan and later to the U.S. In 1960, he established the “International Institute of Rural Reconstruction” in the Philippines, continuing to promote his philosophy of popular education and assisting developing countries in Asia, Latin America, and Africa in advancing their popular education movements.

In May 1943, Yen was selected as one of the “Ten Modern Revolutionary Heroes” in the United States. In 1956, he was awarded the Ramon Magsaysay Award in the Philippines; he subsequently received the Dwight D. Eisenhower Award and the Presidential End Hunger Awards, among other lifetime achievement awards. He dedicated his life to transforming rural communities globally and was honored as the “Father of Popular Education in China.”

His positions as a member of the Wuhan Municipal Political Consultative Conference and Vice President of the Hubei Provincial Foreign Cultural Association were also revoked. It would take four years for him to shed the rightist label and regain a level of 3. During the Cultural Revolution, he faced significant setbacks, turning to philosophical contemplation and engaging in translation work. Wei Zhuomin passed away on March 26, 1976, at the age of 88.

Wei Zhuomin’s works include Confucian Ethics (1929), The Spirit of Chinese Culture (1947), The Concept of God in Ancient Chinese Texts, The Political Thought of Mencius, A Study of Sacrifice, and Foundational Beliefs of Christianity (1950). He also translated works such as The Critique of Pure Reason by Kant, Explanations of Kant’s Philosophy, and selections from Principles of Kant’s Philosophy.

The report published in 1922 titled Zhōnghuá guīzhǔ – Zhōngguó jīdūjiào xuānjiào shìyè tǒngjì (The Christian Occupation of China: A General Survey of the Numerical Strength and Geographical Distribution of the Christian Forces in China) stated that in 1920, the number of communicants reached 366,524.   1. Zhōnghuá xìnyù huì (Lutheran Church of China), 1920 (Xiāo Kèxié 1997: 55-65)

Beginning in the 19th century, various Lutheran missionary societies from Europe and America entered China for evangelization. In 1906, eight Lutheran missionaries, led by J. A. O. Gotteberg from the Norwegian Missionary Society, sent a letter to all contemporary Lutheran missionary institutions in China, suggesting to hold a conference of Lutheran missionaries during the 1907 centenary missionary conference to discuss matters of unification. In May 1907, ten Lutheran societies participated in this conference, including seven American and European societies working in Central China and three German societies operating in Southern China. Since the three German societies had entered China earlier than the other seven and primarily worked in Guangdong dialect, the meeting decided that collaborative work would begin in Central China. In August 1908, the Union Lutheran Conference held its first meeting at Jigong Mountain in Henan, devising concrete cooperation plans and deciding to establish a jointly operated seminary by Lutheran missionary societies. In March 1913, the Lutheran seminary, supported by the American Missouri Synod (American Lutheran Mission), Hauge Synod Mission, Finnish Missionary Society, and Norwegian Missionary Society, was established in Huabei Luanjiao. In September of the same year, the seminary published the Xìnyù bào (Lutheran News), becoming the first printed publication of the Lutheran Church in China.

In 1915, the Union Conference organized a temporary Chinese Lutheran Association (The Temporary Council of the Lutheran Church of China) as a preparatory institution for a nationwide Lutheran church. In 1917, during the 400th anniversary celebration of the Reformation, the Union Conference held its second general assembly and completed the draft of its constitution.

In August 1920, the Zhōnghuá xìnyù huì (Lutheran Church of China) was established at Jigong Mountain in Henan and held its first general assembly, adopting a constitution. The church governance structure of the Lutheran Church of China was established as a four-tier system: church, synod, district, and parish. The five initial missionary societies that participated in founding the Lutheran Church of China became its five synods: Yuzhong Synod (Augustana Synod Mission), Yuer Synod (United Lutheran Church, which merged with the Missouri Synod in 1917), Xiangzhong Synod (Norwegian Mission), Xiangxi Synod (Finnish Mission), and Xiangbei Synod (Church of Sweden Mission). The first bishop was O. R. Wu Lid (伍礼德).Wold（豫鄂总会），with deputy bishops Liang Jiasi (湘中总会) and Zhu Haoran (豫鄂总会).

At the second general assembly held in October 1923, the American Lutheran Free Church and the German Breklum Mission were accepted, leading to the establishment of the Yudong Synod and the Yuannan Synod. At the third general assembly in 1926, the Guangdong-Ganzhou Synod (Berlin Mission), Ludong Synod (United Lutheran Church in America), and Guandong Synod (later renamed Dongbei Synod, Danish Lutheran Mission) were added. During the seventh general assembly convened in 1946, the Yuer-Shanxi Synod (Norwegian Missionary Society) and the Shan’nan Synod (Norwegian Evangelical Lutheran Free Church) were newly established. The eighth general assembly scheduled for October 1949 was unable to take place due to the political situation; however, the presiding bishop Peng Funai convened the 27th regular meeting at Daofeng Mountain in Hong Kong, accepting four new member churches: Zundao Church (The Lutheran Brethren Mission), Lixian Church (Barmen Mission), Chongzhen Church (Basel Mission), and the Christian Mission to the Buddhists at Daofeng Mountain (Daoyou Church).

  1. Zhōnghuá shèngjīng liánhùi (Bible Union of China), 1920 (Yao 2003: 55-100)

The Bible Union of China was established in August 1920 by a group of missionaries in China who held a fundamentalist position. Following a speech opposing liberal theology by prominent North American fundamentalist William H. G. Thomas at a missionary retreat in Guling, approximately twenty missionaries convened a meeting to form a national alliance — the Bible Union of China — and passed a draft declaration and statements. The declaration emphasized that missionaries must “earnestly contend” for the truth revealed clearly in the Old and New Testaments and adhere to the basic doctrines stated in the Apostles’ Creed. It reaffirmed that, in response to the differences within Western theological debates, particularly the emergence of doubts about truth and the acceptance of a one-sided Social Gospel, action was necessary to protect the church and theological education. The movement aimed to awaken the church, rekindle the awareness of the Gospel’s power, promote the dissemination and study of the Bible, and propagate educational materials and articles on biblical fundamental truths, encouraging local missions and churches to accept only those who rightly handle God’s word as missionaries, while vigilantly guarding against attempts to influence students and church leaders with erroneous beliefs and activities. However, the declaration underwent several revisions, replacing radical language with more moderate discourse in order to garner broader support. The main revisers were J. W. Lowrie, president of the American Presbyterian Mission, and D. E. Hoste, head of the China Inland Mission. Lowrie later became the general secretary of the Union.

The Bible Union established its headquarters in Shanghai and published Zhōnghuá shèngjīng liánhùi tōngxùn (The Bulletin of the Bible Union of China) in 1921, which was renamed Zhōnghuá shèngjīng (The Bible for China) in 1924. The first national conference was held in Shanghai in May 1922, electing a hundred-member committee for the leadership structure and an executive committee, with the highest proportion of members being from the Presbyterian and Inland Mission. The number of members grew from over 400 in 1920 to over 2,000 by the end of 1922. During the 1920s, members constituted about 30% of the total number of missionaries in China.At the same time, other countries emulated the Bible Union of China by establishing anti-modernist organizations, including the Bible League for India, Burma and Ceylon, the Bible Union of Victoria in Australia, the Japan Bible Union, and the Madras Bible League.

Liberal missionaries in China also countered the Bible Union of China, accusing it of stirring up theological disputes, initiating heresy trials, and undermining unity and cooperation. Some conservative missionaries also disagreed with the Union’s aggressive stance. Even among the members of the Union, there were factions of both radical and moderate views.

Since the Union was merely a loose alliance, its national organizational structure was not effectively established, and the staff at the headquarters were mostly volunteers or part-timers. As a result, the Union’s influence began to decline gradually, especially beyond the publication of its newsletters, by the late 1920s. In 1935, there were even calls from members to disband. However, with the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, the Bible Union of China ultimately could not sustain its activities.

  1. Běijīng zhèngdàotuán (Peking Apologetic Group), 1920 (Wu Guoan 2000: 33-46; Xing Fuzeng 2020: 350-353)

In 1920, a group of Chinese and foreign Christians in Beijing formed the “Zhèngdàotuán” (Peking Truth-Witness Group) in response to the May Fourth New Culture Movement. Their founding declaration clearly stated, “The stimulation of the environment has awakened the conscience,” firmly believing that the dissemination of Christianity “must align with contemporary scientific and philosophical concepts to clarify various misunderstandings and doubts in modern people, and substantiate the truth of Christianity.” Xu Baoqian, one of the founders, articulated the background of the group, pointing out that Beijing was “the birthplace of the patriotic movement and the New Culture Movement,” whose influences spread nationally from Beijing. He admitted that Christianity faced “attacks” from these movements, thus prompting a group of Christians in Beijing to feel the urgent responsibility to “bear witness,” hoping to clarify misunderstandings from opponents and re-examine the content, organization, system, and traditions of the Chinese church that “fail to adapt to new ideas.” Their goal was to “demonstrate how Christianity adapts to the spirit of the times and how it can serve as a pioneer in this evolutionary era.”

One of the key tasks of the Truth-Witness Group was to edit the monthly journal Shēngmìng (The Life: A Journal of Christian Thought and Practice), which reflects their emphasis on Christian thought and practice. The inaugural issue was published in June 1920, with the launch declaration stating, “Now is the time for social reform in Chinese thought. Students and leaders in the educational community have awakened. Critically and independently addressing all issues based on ‘knowledge’ and ‘principles’ and shouldering leadership responsibilities in society is indeed commendable. We Christians, who have always aspired to reform the world, should seize this opportunity to ardently promote the ‘truth’ and ‘strength’ of Christianity as guidance for transforming China. Feeling the weight of this awareness and responsibility, we have united to publish this ‘monthly journal.’”

The editorial team for Shēngmìng comprised 23 members, with Liu Tingfang as the chief editor and Hu Xuecheng as the publishing manager. The members of the Truth-Witness Group and Shēngmìng had significant overlap with the Young Men’s Christian Association, the China Continuation Committee, and Yenching University, attesting to the rise of an educated Christian intellectual community in China under the foundation of Sino-Western collaboration.

In September 1924, the Truth-Witness Group reorganized and was renamed the Life Society. In April 1926, Shēngmìng merged with the Zhēnlǐ zhōukān (Truth Weekly) published by the Beijing Truth Society, subsequently renaming itself Zhēnlǐ yǔ shēngmìng (Truth and Life), until its suspension during the Pacific War in 1941, after which it resumed publication post-war. Despite several reorganizations, the content of the publication continued to revolve around themes of Christianity and Chinese culture, social reconstruction, and reform, making it a representative continuous publication within the liberal Christian camp in early 20th-century China, inspired and shaped by the impetus of the May Fourth New Culture Movement.(4) Zhōnghuá quánguó jīdūjiào xiéjìn huì (National Christian Council of China), 1922 (Quán Shàowǔ 1922: 249-252; Wáng Chéngmiǎn 2006: 116-120)

At the national congress of Chinese Christianity held in Shanghai in May 1922, a resolution was adopted by representatives to establish the “Zhōnghuá quánguó jīdūjiào xiéjìn huì” (National Christian Council of China) to promote cooperation and unity among various churches. As an unifying body, the Council clarified at its inception that “all theological and ecclesiastical issues are outside the scope of this organization” and explicitly expressed the Council’s declaration of the Christian faith: “We believe and reaffirm our loyalty to God, the Almighty Father; to Jesus Christ, His Son, our Lord and Savior, who loved us and gave Himself for our sins; and to the Holy Spirit, our Lord, the giver of life; we further acknowledge our loyalty to the Holy Spirit as the supreme guide for faith and actions; and we acknowledge the fundamental Christian beliefs held by the churches to which we belong.”

According to the Council’s charter, its powers included: (1) fostering and promoting the spirit of communication and unity among Chinese Christian churches, and the spirit of communion with world Christianity; (2) studying progress toward self-supporting, self-governing, and self-propagating churches, to facilitate the establishment of indigenized churches in China; (3) promoting evangelization in China; (4) assisting church leaders, regardless of denominational affiliations, in exchanging opinions; (5) cultivating leadership talent to meet various needs; (6) organizing national Christian activities; (7) nurturing spiritual life and evangelistic zeal in churches; (8) organizing church communications; (9) coordinating various organizations to work in unity; (10) representing Chinese churches in communications with national Christian organizations in other countries; (11) making statements on matters of significant relation to the Chinese church; (12) handling tasks delegated by national congresses; (13) preparing for the subsequent national Christian congress.

The Council selected representatives as members (100 in total) based on the number of individuals from different denominations and groups, appointing a steering committee (no more than 21 members, with a majority being Chinese). In May 1923, the Council held its first annual meeting, electing Yu Rizhang, the general secretary of the National Association of the Young Men’s Christian Association of China, as president. The Council established various specialized committees, including those for evangelism and retreats, rural churches and farmers’ lives, industry, family, drug prevention, indigenous churches, and international goodwill.

  1. Zhōngguó jīdūjiào dàxué liánhé tuōshì bù (United Board for Christian Higher Education in China, UBCCC), 1922 (Liú Jiāfēng, Liú Tiānlù 2003: 188-211; Xíng Fúzēng 2012: 181-182)

Since the late Qing dynasty, the development of higher education by various Christian missions in China progressed rapidly. In 1919, the presidents of Christian universities formed the “Zhōngguó jīdūjiào dàxué xiéhuì” (Association of Christian Colleges and Universities) to discuss common challenges. From 1921 to 1922, the Committee of Reference and Council of the Foreign Missions Conference of North America organized the China Educational Commission to conduct a comprehensive survey and review of the educational situation in China, recommending strengthened cooperation in Christian higher education and the establishment of a united board to serve the common interests of the universities.

In October 1922, Jinling University, Yenching University, Qilu University, and Fujian Union University established the “General Office of Chinese Union Universities” in New York, with West China Union University and Yenching College for Women also joining in the late 1920s. This center became the first tangible outcome of the cooperation movement among Chinese Christian universities.In October 1925, the “Zhōngguó jīdūjiào gāoděng jiàoyù xiézuò yǔ chóukuǎn chángshè wěiyuánhuì” (Permanent Committee for the Coordination and Promotion of Christian Higher Education in China) was established, primarily responsible for joint fundraising and overall planning. Its members included Lingnan University, Fujian Union University, Jinling Women’s University, Zhejiang University, South China Women’s College of Arts and Sciences, Jinling University, Yenching University, Qilu University, Yali University, and Yenching College for Women (later, West China Union University also joined). In January 1928, the committee was renamed the “Zhōngguó jīdūjiào dàxué wěiyuánhuì” (Committee for Christian Colleges in China).

In 1928 and 1930, the higher education committee of the China Christian Education Association proposed the “Comprehensive Plan for Christian Higher Education in China,” providing recommendations for curriculum and systems to be discussed by various universities, their boards of trustees, and missionary societies. In 1931, 13 university boards of trustees and related missionary societies approved the plan. However, due to the impact of the Great Depression beginning in 1929, which affected fundraising efforts, only regional initiatives in Central China were able to progress, leading to the abandonment of the “Comprehensive Plan.” Nevertheless, the Committee for Christian Colleges in China began efforts to establish a united board of trustees.

In October 1932, the boards of trustees from ten universities announced the establishment of the “Zhōngguó jīdūjiào dàxué liánhé tuōshì bù” (United Board for Christian Higher Education in China) in New York, with the 1922 General Office of Chinese Union Universities reorganized as the executive office of the new board. The ten universities included: Zhejiang University, Fujian Union University, Yenching University, Qilu University, Jinling University, Lingnan University, Huazhong University, Dongwu University, West China Union University, and Jinling Women’s College of Arts and Sciences. Later, South China Women’s College of Arts and Sciences, Hujiang University, and St. John’s University also joined in 1935, 1937, and 1944, respectively.

The mission of the United Board for Christian Higher Education in China was to “promote the interests of Christian higher education in China through cooperation.” Its specific work involved management, planning, and joint fundraising. In 1936, the board began contemplating reorganization into a united trusteeship (rather than a joint association). This vision made substantive progress after the war when, in 1945, the boards from Fujian Union University, South China Women’s College of Arts and Sciences, Jinling Women’s University, Jinling University, West China Union University, and Yenching University reached a merger agreement. In September, they established the United Board for Christian Colleges in China. In 1946, Qilu University joined, followed by St. John’s University, Huazhong University, Dongwu University, and Zhejiang University in succession. The boards of trustees from the various universities transferred their assets to the new board, with only Hujiang University and Lingnan University maintaining their independence.

In January 1949, the United Board decided to respond to the changing landscape in China with “a positive and courageous policy,” stating that as long as Christian universities in China operated according to their Christian principles, the board would continue to provide comprehensive support. In May 1950, the board held its annual meeting in New York, clearly stating that despite the challenges posed by communist ideology in the curriculum, and the threats to the ideals of Christian education, it would continue to support Christian universities in China, considering the lack of widespread official intervention and the commitment of the Christian faculty to maintain these principles. In November, as Sino-American relations deteriorated over the Korean issue, the board noted they had received telegrams from presidents of three universities in October indicating they could still accept overseas subsidies, prompting further remittances. Even when the U.S. government announced an economic embargo against China in December, the board sought special permission from the U.S. government to remit funds to China. As the anti-American cultural invasion movement gained momentum in China, conditions for Christian universities worsened. In February 1951, the United Board expressed concerns about the prospects for Christian higher education in China. In the fall of 1952, the Ministry of Education in China began extensive restructuring of higher education institutions, with all church universities being nationalized and merged with other institutions. The church universities (and the entire system of Christian education) were “uprooted” from China, becoming part of history.

In May 1951, the United Board for Christian Colleges in China convened its annual meeting and decided to establish Christian universities for the Chinese in Asia, planning to found Chung Chi College in Hong Kong to continue the tradition of Christian higher education in China. In 1955, the board also established Tunghai University in Taiwan.In May 1955, the board was renamed the “Yàzhōu jīdūjiào gāoděng jiàoyù liánhé dǒngshìhuì” (United Board for Christian Higher Education in Asia).

  1. Zhēnlǐ shè (Truth Society), 1922 (Wu Guoan 2000: 58-66)

The founding of the Truth Society arose from discussions in the winter of 1922 between Liu Tingfang and Hu Xuecheng about the desire to unite like-minded individuals to form a small group for devotional activities and discussions on church issues. Later, Hu Xuecheng put this idea into action by inviting six others—Wu Leichuan, Bao Guanglin, Zhang Qinshi, Peng Jinchang, Wu Yaozong, and Chen Guoliang—to form a small group in December 1922. They met weekly to study church and national issues. After discussions, they decided to begin with written works, publishing the Zhēnlǐ zhōukān (Truth Weekly) in April 1923 in Beijing and officially naming the group the “Truth Society.” In the preface of the inaugural issue, they clearly stated that since Christians “have been given the mission to build the Kingdom of Heaven and cherish the lofty ambition to reform society,” they must warn against the corrupt current situation and awaken their compatriots to “jointly build the nation of our dreams.” From September 1923 onwards, every issue of Zhēnlǐ zhōukān featured the motto “Join with fellow believers, in the fearless spirit of Jesus, to bear witness to the truth, seek church reform, and promote the transformation of China.”

Active participants in the Truth Society and contributors to Zhēnlǐ zhōukān included Wu Leichuan, Bao Guanglin, Zhang Qinshi, Peng Jinchang, Wu Yaozong, Chen Guoliang, Hu Guocheng, Li Rongfang, Liu Tingfang, Xu Baoqian, Jian Youwen, among others. However, the church community generally regarded the statements from the Truth Society as extreme, sometimes labeling them as “heretical.”

In 1926, Wu Leichuan noted that of the 11 members of the Truth Society, 10 were also members of the Life Society, with 5 of them serving as editors of Shēngmìng (The Life Monthly). Zhēnlǐ zhōukān merged with Shēngmìng in April 1926 to become Zhēnlǐ yǔ shēngmìng (Truth and Life), continuing until its suspension during the Pacific War in 1941, after which it resumed publication post-war.

  1. Zhōngguó wéilove shè (Chinese Fellowship of Reconciliation), 1922 (Yao Xiyi 2008: 20-23)

In 1922, the Chinese Fellowship of Reconciliation was established in Beijing as a branch of the international Christian anti-war organization, Fellowship of Reconciliation. Its founder, H.T. Hodgkin, was sent by the British Quakers to Sichuan to engage in educational and medical missionary work from 1905 to 1910. Upon returning to England and facing World War I, he advocated for peace and initiated the establishment of the Fellowship of Reconciliation in 1914. In 1919, the first international conference of the Fellowship of Reconciliation was held in the Netherlands, and by 1930, similar organizations had been established in over 20 countries across Europe and Asia.

At the end of 1920, Hodgkin visited China and introduced the fellowship movement. In 1922, the Chinese Fellowship of Reconciliation was formally established in Beijing. That same year, a national conference was held in Shanghai, where they adopted a declaration stating: “We believe that the love of Jesus Christ overcomes all the evils of the world,” and therefore, “we will not participate in any war, whether aggressive or defensive.” They contended that war could not solve problems and contradicted the love of Jesus Christ, affirming that “only goodness can overcome evil.”

The Chinese Fellowship of Reconciliation later established its headquarters in Nanjing and formed a national committee responsible for overseeing research, advocacy, communication, and other activities, with Bao Guanglin serving as the national secretary. Within a few years, they conducted activities in cities including Beijing, Shanghai, Nanjing, Tianjin, Anqing, Chengdu, Xiamen, and Fuzhou and began publishing Wéilove (Fellowship) bimonthly in 1931. Xu Baoqian and Wu Yaozong were among the most notable members of the Chinese Fellowship, with Wu later becoming the president of the organization.

  1. Jīdūtú zhùhuìchù (Assembly Hall), 1922 (Rèn Zhōngxiáng 1996: 1-7; Li Jiafu 2001; Bays 1996: 311-312)

In early 1922, Ni Tuosheng and the Wang couple hosted a breaking of bread gathering at Wang Zai’s home in Fuzhou. The number of participants grew, and they began to refer to themselves as “the believers gathering in Fuzhou in the name of the Lord.” In January 1923, Li Yuanru was invited by Ni and Wang to preach in Fuzhou, resulting in many conversions, which led to the renting of a twelve-room contact center managed by Ni, Wang, and Wang Lianjun. This gathering became the origin of the “Assembly Hall.” During this time, Ni also began publishing Fùxīng bào (Revival News) in Fuzhou.In 1924, Ni Tuosheng and Wang Zai experienced a divergence in church direction; Wang advocated for the establishment of pastors and named the church “Christ Hall,” whereas Ni opposed this, believing that the Bible did not support the institution of pastors, and that churches should only be referred to by their geographic location. In 1924, Ni left due to the split in the church. In May 1925, Ni established an evangelical bookstore in Luoxingtan and began publishing the Jīdū tú zhào (Christian Report) to articulate his ideas. In 1927, Ni Tuosheng moved to Shanghai, where the evangelical bookstore also relocated, and gatherings commenced there. The meeting place transitioned from Hatoon Road to Wenderli, attracting many believers, leading some recipients of his teachings to establish their own meeting places.

According to Ni’s understanding, the church was local and had no formal name. However, because Ni referred to Jesus calling His disciples “little flock,” he termed the gatherings in various locations as “little flock meetings.” Since the hymnal he published was titled Xiǎoqún (Little Flock), other churches began to call them “little flock.” Eventually, Ni changed the title of the hymnal to simply Shīgē (Hymns) to avoid using “little flock” in the naming of the meetings. Internally, the primary names for churches were based on geographic locations, such as Shanghai Church and Nanjing Church, and were also referred to as “local churches.” Externally, for convenience, they began using the term “Christian Assembly Hall.”

In February 1928, a national “Special Investigation Group Meeting” was held in Shanghai. Starting in 1932, Ni Tuosheng ordained elders in the Shanghai Church and established deacons, along with coworkers, forming the governance system for various meeting places. By the time of the War of Resistance Against Japan, there were over 200 meeting places nationwide. According to Ni’s teachings, these places developed independently without a central or national organization; however, due to the influential coworkers, including Ni in Shanghai and the evangelical bookstore also located there, the Shanghai Church held a unique status and influence.

In the period surrounding the establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, it was estimated that there were a total of 80,000 believers across all meeting places, making it the fastest-growing indigenous independent denomination of the 20th century.

  1. Zhōnghuá jīdūjiàohuì quánguó zǒnghuì (General Assembly of the Church of Christ in China), 1927 (Chén Zhìhéng 2013: 83-108)

The Church of Christ in China was the first national interdenominational church in China, a product of the “unification” movement. Its founding can be traced back to April 1918 when the National Presbyterian Assembly held a meeting in Nanjing to discuss the establishment of a national temporary general assembly. Representatives from the London Mission and the Congregational Church also attended to explore the possibility of creating a unified church among the three bodies. In January 1919, representatives from the three bodies established the “Executive Committee of the Church Federation of China” in Nanjing, with the English Baptist Mission and the American Friends Mission present, to discuss forming a united organization for the Chinese church and to divide into two groups for deliberation on articles and creeds, initially proposing “Church of Christ in China” as the name with a governance structure of four levels (congregational assembly, district assembly, general assembly, and national assembly).

In addition to Guangdong, large assemblies began to be established in Hebei, Guandong (Manchuria), Liuhe, Huabei, Minbei, Minzhong, Minnan, Lingdong, and other areas.

In 1922, the temporary general assembly of the Church of Christ in China was held in Shanghai. In 1927, the first General Assembly of the Church of Christ in China was held at St. Mary’s School in Shanghai, attended by 88 representatives (including 66 Chinese representatives and 22 missionaries), founding the National General Assembly of the Church of Christ in China. Cheng Jingyi was elected as the national president, while Asher R. Kepler served as the general secretary. At that time, there were already 12 conferences and 47 district assemblies nationwide. By 1928, this number increased to 51 district assemblies involving 16 different denominations. By 1946, there were 20 conferences and 105 district assemblies, with 17 different missionary organizations participating (including the United Church of Canada, the Woman’s Missionary Society of the United Church of Canada, the Presbyterian Church in Canada, the Woman’s Missionary Society of the Presbyterian Church in Canada, the American Congregational Church, the Southern Presbyterian Church, the Advent Christian Church, the Northern Presbyterian Church, the Reformed Church, the Brotherhood, the London Mission, the English Baptist Church, the Irish Presbyterian Church, the Scottish Presbyterian Church, the Presbyterian Church of New Zealand, and the Presbyterian Church of Australia). The Church of Christ in China maintained an estimated 130,000 believers. By 1949, the number of believers had increased to 176,000, making it the largest “denomination” in China.

The Church of Christ in China represented an “organic union,” which, according to Asher R. Kepler, meant “to unite without seeking uniformity”; “unity does not require sameness.”

  1. Zhōnghuá jīdù zhǔyì liánméng (Christian Fundamental League for China), 1927 (Yao 2003: 88-92; Xíng Fúzēng 2001: 26-27)

The Bible Union of China, established in 1920, began to experience ideological differences by the late 1920s. More radical and militant fundamentalist missionaries grew dissatisfied with the Union’s more moderate stance towards modernism, resulting in the Union losing its representative status and influence. In June 1927, a group of predominantly Presbyterian missionaries formed the “Christian Fundamental League for China.” Core members included Bai Xiusheng (Hugh W. White), Ge Jia (Arie Kok), Shi Jiada (Edgar E. Strother), and He Gengshi (M. A. Hopkins). In 1928, the League published Zhōngguó jīchǔ bào (The China Fundamentalist), serving as a platform for strong critiques against modernist tendencies in organizations and individuals.

In its opposition to liberal theology, the Christian Fundamental League for China took a more confrontational stance compared to the Bible Union, even questioning whether North American missionary societies could guarantee the dispatch of faithful missionaries to various fields of service. Therefore, they adopted a direct approach to communicating with their mother countries. The League maintained an extreme position on biblical authority, believing that the Bible is divinely inspired, with its original version recognized as authoritative and infallible.They even considered modernism or liberal theology to be a “conspiracy” of Bolshevism, viewing liberal missionaries as “socialists and sympathizers of the Soviet Union.”

  1. Yēsū jiātíng (Jesus Family), 1927 (Tao Feiya 2004; Lian 2010: 64–84; Bays 1996: 312-313)

The “Jesus Family” was one of the indigenous Christian sects that arose in China in the early 20th century, originating from Mazhuang in Shandong. Founded by Jing Dianying in the 1920s, it began as the “Saints’ Credit Cooperative” (1921) and the Sericulture School (1926), eventually establishing the “Jesus Family” in 1927. The Jesus Family aimed to create a communal Christian community focused on the concept of “family” (both big and small) and was committed to practicing communal living and shared property. Influenced by Jing’s background, the Jesus Family blended traditional Confucian and Daoist ethics with Christian beliefs, becoming a representative of the indigenous Christian movement in China.

Jing Dianying was introduced to Christianity early on and received teachings from Presbyterian, Assemblies of God, and Methodist missionaries. In the 1920s, he began exploring localized forms of Christianity and established the first Jesus Family in Mazhuang, Shandong. The Jesus Family emphasized daily spiritual exercises, Bible reading, and prayer, placing great importance on hymns. Jing wrote many hymns expressing the pursuit of an ideal society (e.g., “As the elders care for the young,” “Living by the Lord is not difficult,” “No competition or grievances,” “No borders for nations”). However, as the “big family” leader, Jing also established a high authoritative position.

The Jesus Family reached its peak in the 1930s and 1940s, with believers spread across Shandong, Hebei, and other areas, numbering in the thousands. During the war, there was an average annual increase of 8 new households, with Shandong being the center, accounting for 62% of the total. By 1949, there were a total of 104 “families” nationwide, comprising 12,000 believers. Due to its “communist” structure, it was even praised by certain mainstream Christian circles. The Jesus Family actively supported the Three-Self Reform Movement, with a significant representation in the first batch of signatures for the Three-Self declaration (1,527 signatories), where 381 out of 407 signers from Shandong were members of the Jesus Family. By April 1951, the number of signers from the Jesus Family reached 1,747, accounting for one-fifth of the signers in Shandong.

However, the Jesus Family was ultimately labeled as a “feudal regime,” and in 1952, the national Christian Three-Self organization sent a work team to Mazhuang to investigate, with Jing becoming a primary target during a campaign of accusations. Besides issues regarding lifestyle and morality, the organization was also labeled an internal dark and reactionary group. The “reform” proposed by the Jesus Family signaled it as the first Christian sect to be “dismantled” after the establishment of the People’s Republic of China.

  1. Zhōnghuá guówài bùdào tuán (Chinese Foreign Missionary Union), 1929 (Liang Jialin 1999: 22, 86-89, 125-126; Peng Peishi 2004: 95-98)

Founded in 1929, the Chinese Foreign Missionary Union was the first missionary organization from Chinese churches to engage in overseas missions, established by Robert A. Jaffray, a missionary from the Christian and Missionary Alliance. Jaffray arrived in China in 1897, where he not only preached in Guangxi but also took on significant responsibilities for evangelistic work in the nearby French colonial territory of Annam. He conducted an exploration trip to Annam in 1898 and felt firsthand the obstacles presented by the Catholic Church and the French colonial government against the introduction of Protestant missions there. After some efforts, he established an evangelistic base in Da Nang in 1911. In 1916, he was appointed as the president of the Vietnam Mission by the American Conference (with headquarters in Wuzhou, Guangxi) while also serving as the Southern China area missionary director and principal of the Wuzhou JianDao Bible School. In January 1928, he began pioneering work in Indonesia. By that time, graduates of the JianDao Bible School were already preaching in Annam and Indonesia. In 1927, he issued a call to the students for pioneering mission work in Southeast Asia. The following year, he made exploratory trips to Borneo and Indonesia, and upon returning to Wuzhou, he formed a temporary committee for developing Southeast Asian ministries, composed of Chinese coworkers from the Alliance, with Jaffray serving as the chair and Pastor Zhao Liutang as the vice-chair. At the end of 1928, he sent the Lin Zhengye couple and Lian Guanglin to Southeast Asia, with funding provided by Chinese believers in Guangxi. However, the American Alliance did not support Jaffray’s suggestions for expanding work in Southeast Asia due to financial constraints, believing that efforts should focus on Guangxi and Annam.In March 1929, Jaffray established the “Nányáng bùdào tuán” (Southeast Asia Missionary Team), with Wang Zai serving as the team leader, and Jaffray as the deputy leader and treasurer. The team included members Zhao Liutang, Huang Yuansu, Liang Xigao, and Wang Zhi, with headquarters located in Wuzhou. In October, to emphasize its status as the first missionary society established by Chinese people, it was renamed “Zhōnghuá guówài chuándào tuán” (Chinese Foreign Missionary Union). The funding was primarily raised by Jaffray, supplemented by Chinese missionaries raising funds through faith. In 1930, Jaffray resigned as the president of the Southern China Mission of the Alliance to focus on missionary work in Indonesia.

Since its inception, many graduates of the JianDao Bible School had been sent to Southeast Asia. Over the 13 years, the union dispatched 20 Chinese coworkers and trained 30 Western coworkers, along with 140 local workers, establishing 5 churches in 139 locations.

After the outbreak of the Pacific War in December 1941, Jaffray was imprisoned in a concentration camp in Makassar, where he passed away in July 1945. The loss of leadership, coupled with financial difficulties, severely impacted the work of the missionary union.

  1. World’s Student Christian Federation, 1922 (Zhang Lixia (張麗霞) 2018: 18-22)

In April 1922, the World’s Student Christian Federation (World’s Student Christian Federation, WSCF) held its eleventh annual meeting at Tsinghua University (清華大學) in Beijing. WSCF was founded in 1895 by John R. Mott (穆德) and others, with its headquarters in Geneva. It consists of the student branches of various national YMCA (基督教青年會) and YWCA (女青年會) organizations, with member groups mainly from North America, the UK, Germany, and Nordic countries.

In 1896, the “Christian Children’s Society of China” (中國學塾基督徒幼童會) was established and joined the federation. Later, the school association branch of the National Christian Association of China and the student department of the YWCA also became members. According to WSCF conventions, a meeting is held every two years. In 1920, at its General Committee meeting, the Chinese representatives proposed that the next meeting be held in Beijing. In 1921, when the WSCF Executive Committee met in the Netherlands, Gu Ziren (顧子仁), the secretary of the student department of the National Christian Association, reiterated the same request. After discussions, it was decided that the eleventh annual meeting would be held in Beijing.

The National Christian Association of China actively prepared for the meeting and designated Tsinghua University as the venue. The annual meeting took place from April 4 to 9, attended by representatives from 32 countries, including 146 foreign delegates and over 400 Chinese representatives. The theme was “Christianity and the Transformation of the World,” discussing six different topics: (1) International and racial issues; (2) Christianity and the transformation of society and industry; (3) How to promote Christianity among modern students; (4) The Christianization of school life; (5) Responsibilities within the student church; (6) How to strengthen the World’s Student Christian Federation globally.

Once the news of the annual meeting in Beijing spread, it directly stimulated non-Christian movements in cities like Beijing and Shanghai. Meanwhile, the meeting passed a proposal to establish Christian student movements in China, marking a new chapter in the development of the Chinese Christian student movement.

  1. National Christian Conference of China, 1922 (Quan Shaowu (全紹武) 1922; Duan Qi (段琦) 2004: 209-215)

From May 2 to 11, 1922, the National Christian Conference of China convened at the Shanghai Municipal Hall, with the overall theme being “The Chinese Church” (中國教會). A total of 1,112 representatives from churches across the nation attended, including 564 Chinese representatives. Unlike the three previous national Christian conferences held in 1877, 1890, and 1907—which were organized by missionaries in China with no formal participation from Chinese representatives (although there were several who attended as visitors in 1907)—in 1922, the Chinese representatives comprised more than half of the attendees. The conference was presided over by Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡) and he delivered the opening address. F. S. Brockman (巴樂滿) gave the closing speech titled “Spiritual Unity” (團結的精神).

Other Chinese speakers included: Zhao Zichen (趙紫宸) on “Strengths and Weaknesses of the Chinese Church,” Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) on “Christianity in China,” Luo Yunyan (羅運炎) on “Issues of Church Leadership,” Fan Yurong (范玉榮) on “Women’s Leadership,” Wu Junming (吳濬明) on “Christian Educational Endeavors in China,” and Wei Zhuomin (韋卓民) on “The Position of Christian Education in China,” and Jia Yuming (賈玉銘) on “The Personal Life of Christians.”

The conference was organized around five key themes, divided into five groups: (1) The current status of Christianity in China; (2) The future work of Christianity in China; (3) The mission of the church; (4) Leadership in church work; (5) Unified actions and cooperation in church activities. The third group, concerning the mission of the church, was exclusively managed by Chinese members, with no missionaries participating, and was led by Cheng Jingyi. Each group produced reports to be presented at the conference. Among the reports, the declaration from the third group, titled “The Declaration of the Church,” included two parts: “To Fellow Workers” and “To the People of the Nation,” serving as a confessional statement of faith for the Chinese church of that time. In addition to the reports from the groups, the conference also held special discussions on education, healthcare, literacy initiatives, industrial and economic issues, the role of women and the church, and evangelistic efforts.The records of the National Conference were published in both Chinese and English in Shanghai: “Report of the National Christian Conference” (1922) and The Chinese Church as Revealed in the National Christian Conference (1922).

  1. The Christian Occupation of China: A General Survey of the Numerical Strength and Geographical Distribution of the Christian Forces in China (《中華歸主──中國基督教宣教事業統計》) published in 1922 (Stauffer (司德敷) 1922; 查時傑 (Zha Shijie) 1994: 541-542)

After the establishment of the China Continuation Committee (中華續行委辦會), a large-scale investigation into the situation of Christianity in China was conducted, the results of which were published in The Christian Occupation of China: A General Survey of the Numerical Strength and Geographical Distribution of the Christian Forces in China, edited by Milton T. Stauffer (司德敷) and published by the China Continuation Committee in 1922. In addition to the English report, Lu Shiyin (陸士寅) translated part of the content into Chinese, re-titling it as 《中華歸主──中國基督教事業統計》, published by the Commercial Press (上海商務印書館) in 1922. In the 1980s, the Institute of World Religions at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences published a complete translation of the Chinese report, compiled by the Special Committee on Survey and Occupation of the China Continuation Committee, translated by the Institute of World Religions: 《中華歸主──中國基督教事業統計（1901-1920）》 (Beijing: 中國社會科學出版社, 1985). It was reprinted again in 2007.

The impetus for the investigation came from the China Continuation Committee’s decision in 1916 to establish a Special Committee on Survey and Occupation to conduct a comprehensive statistical survey of the status of the Christian enterprise in China. The special committee engaged in detailed discussions on how to conduct the survey and statistical analysis, drafted questionnaires, and collected data for statistical analysis based on the situation in 1918. The investigation was led by Stauffer and was completed by 1922.

The report is divided into fifteen sections, which not only provide context regarding China but also systematically review the development of Christianity in China, especially analyzing the reforms and advancements of Chinese Christianity from 1900 to 1920. The focus of the report is to present the current situation of churches in each province through statistical data and analysis. This includes the number of missionaries, Chinese believers and clergy, the number of churches, and the distribution of different denominations and societies across various regions. Additionally, it examined the Christian enterprises in each province (such as church schools, hospitals, and other initiatives) and conducted comparative studies in different areas. Furthermore, analyses were made on specific topics: evangelism among special groups (various ethnicities, blind individuals, fishermen, youth, different workers, etc.), the Chinese church, education efforts, healthcare initiatives, literacy projects, and the conditions of the Roman Catholic Church and the Russian Orthodox Church in China.

This report remains the most detailed and comprehensive statistical survey of Protestant missionary efforts in China to this day. However, since the English title includes the term “Christian Occupation,” it later became a point of contention in the non-Christian movement, with “occupation” being viewed as evidence of Christian imperialist aggression.

  1. Christianity and Rural Construction 1922 (Chen Guangpei (陳廣培) 2001: 117-120)

In the early 20th century, 80% of China’s population still lived in rural areas and depended on agriculture for their livelihoods. To move towards modernization, addressing rural issues is undoubtedly a crucial component.Facing the serious issues in Chinese rural areas (economically, educationally, and socially), by the mid-1920s, intellectuals began to pay attention to the importance of improving rural life for the reconstruction of China and initiated reconstruction movements in various localities.

However, Chinese Christianity had already started to focus on rural reconstruction in the early 1920s, with discussions regarding western missions and agriculture, as well as Christianity and rural reconstruction (Rural Reconstruction) appearing in the Chinese Recorder (《教務雜誌》). In 1920, Jinan University (金陵大學) even hired missionaries trained in agriculture to teach agricultural courses to help seminary students understand and recognize relevant issues. The 1922 report published in 《中華歸主》 suggested establishing an “Agricultural Mission” within the educational sector, indicating that “the role of agriculture in evangelistic work lies in establishing contact points and promoting self-support for the church by improving the economic conditions of believers. To enhance the economic state of the community, farmers must first be educated to fully understand the benefits of using better agricultural methods.” The report also emphasized the significance of moral and social issues.

Therefore, when the National Christian Association of China (中華全國基督教協進會) was established in 1922, it formed the “Committee on Rural Issues and Village Life.” The committee’s secretary, Pastor Zhong Ketu (鍾可託), conducted investigations in provinces such as Guangdong (粵), Sichuan (川), Hubei (鄂), Jiangxi (贛), Shandong (魯), and Hebei (冀), and later appointed “visiting secretaries” to continue the related work. The objectives were: (1) to have rural churches and schools serve the rural community; (2) to recruit voluntary staff from villages to advance relevant movements; (3) to incorporate training for rural work talents in theological education; (4) to organize rural health movements. However, this phase of Christian “rural construction” mainly focused on investigation and discussion.

In 1928, the International Missionary Conference held a meeting in Jerusalem where rural work was also a focal point. Later, the association hired Zhang Fuliang (張福良) as the rural work secretary. From the 1930s onward, Christianity became more practically involved in rural construction efforts.

  1. Sun Yat-sen’s Death and Christian Prayer, 1925 (Chenbao (晨報) 1925a, 1925b; Bao Shijie (包世傑) 1988: 216-219)

On March 12, 1925, Sun Yat-sen (孫中山) died in Beijing due to liver disease. According to Bao Shijie’s (包世傑) diary entry titled “Private Memorandum on the Death of Mr. Sun Yat-sen,” it was mentioned that following Sun’s death, there was a “violent conflict of religious opinions.” One faction argued that “since the Generalissimo was a Christian believer, he should be buried according to Christian ceremonies,” while another faction contended that “since the Generalissimo was a leader of the revolution, he should be honored based on revolutionary significance, not be utilized by any particular religion.” Even some young party members expressed strong hints, suggesting that if Christian burial ceremonies were necessary, they would not hesitate to use force to stop it. Through mediation and explanation by figures like Wang Jingwei (汪精衛), along with efforts from General Lu Zhonglin (鹿鐘麟) of the Northwest Army, a consensus was reached. Ultimately, it was decided that before the coffin was moved to the Union Medical College (協和醫院), a ceremonial prayer would be held in the hospital auditorium. However, a portion of the Kuomintang (國民黨) members held dissenting views, stating that “the Kuomintang opposes Christianity and should not adopt such a ritual.” Finally, it was accepted that “the family sought these rituals to comfort the deceased, which seemed permissible,” noting that Sun had expressed in his lifetime that “the Kuomintang did not oppose Christianity, with only a few party members holding such personal opinions,” thus calming the controversy.

On March 19, 1925, the Chenbao reported that regarding the religious prayer held in the hospital, “the Communist Party had significant objections, asserting that the Kuomintang opposes Christianity and should not adopt such rituals. However, from the family’s perspective, Sun was indeed a Christian. What harm is there in the family seeking this comfort for the deceased?” The Chenbao also cited a notice from the funeral organizing committee: “Mr. Sun’s family, including Madam Song and her son Zhesong, and Kong Yung’s son, plan to hold a family prayer in the hospital auditorium before the public ceremony. Party members may have objections, but the Central Executive Committee stated that this was a familial action and had nothing to do with the party. The party would thus not bear any responsibility and decided to allow it.”

Following mediation from the Executive Committee, it was concluded that the family’s actions were unrelated to the party, leading to a decision to proceed. It was heard that the arrangements prior to this were organized by Sun’s protocol officers, who issued ceremonial tickets to friends and party members supportive of this ritual for attendance. At the front of the auditorium, a floral tribute made of narcissus, oleander, and magnolia was arranged, with Sun’s portrait suspended in the center. At ten o’clock, the prayer ceremony began, and male and female guests filed in. Madam Song wore a mourning veil, seated close to Sun’s effigy, reflecting on the deceased with tears in her eyes. The coffin was draped with the Kuomintang (國民黨) party flag featuring the blue sky and white sun, as well as the newly created national flag. There were fourteen guides, divided into two rows of seven, all dressed in pastor robes, wearing blue long tunics with white overcoats, the buttons facing backward, and holding white wax candles approximately two feet long. Liu Tingfang (劉廷芳) delivered a eulogy, conveying that Sun was the first man in China who, today, ascended to heaven to meet with God. The family prayer held today was to ask for Mr. Sun to rest with God. Subsequently, the hymn “Abide with Me” was played, with music and singing harmonizing in poignant melodies that filled the room with a somber atmosphere. Everyone in the hall stood for a prayer, during which Zhu Youyu (朱友漁) read from the Bible, primarily to silently bless the soul in peace with God. These parallel the biblical notions of “faith, hope, and love,” firmly establishing Sun as a Christian. He has now returned to meet God and rest in the bosom of the Father. His spirit still exists in the universe, which is why we gather today to pray, believing that Sun continues his hope through us, in accordance with the biblical principle of “eternal life.”

  1. The May Thirtieth Incident and Christianity, 1925 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1997: 124-125, 222-232)

In May 1925, the infamous “May Thirtieth” incident occurred, which escalated the swelling anti-imperialist tide across the nation to its peak. The incident originated in early May when a Japanese textile factory in Shanghai fired workers, leading to strikes and subsequent clashes between labor and management, resulting in damage to the factory. When factory staff and security, along with police, forcibly removed the workers, a Japanese individual shot and injured worker Gu Zhenghong (顧正洪), who later died two days afterward. The injured workers were subsequently accused by the Japanese factory. On May 23, two university students were arrested for supporting the workers. On the 24th, the engineering community held a memorial service for Gu Zhenghong, and four university students were detained by the police in the foreign concession. Six arrested students were scheduled to stand trial on May 30. On the morning of that day, students from Nanyang University (南洋大學) and Fudan University (復旦大學) formed a “Student Speakers’ Group” and marched out of school. As the students entered the British Concession, workers joined the rally, stirring up passionate sentiments. The police forcibly dispersed the crowd and arrested the speakers, taking them to the Old Wharf Police Station (老閘捕房) on Nanjing Road. Students and members of the public began gathering outside the police station. At this moment, the police also arrested students on Xizang Road, filling the police station with the detained and their supporters. Around two thousand people gathered outside the police station, and the police chief, E. W. Everson, first raised his weapon in warning and then opened fire, prompting other police to shoot into the crowd. As a result, four people died on the spot, seven were seriously injured (and later died), and numerous others sustained minor injuries, marking the beginning of the “May Thirtieth Incident.” During this time, nearly ten thousand civil organizations were formed, launching large-scale anti-British imperialist propaganda campaigns, and cities such as Shanghai, Beijing, and Guangzhou became the epicenters of the anti-imperialist movement spurred by the “May Thirtieth” incident.Between June 2 and July 19, the Shanghai Shen Bao (《申報》) published a total of 38 reports from Chinese and foreign Christians and organizations responding to the incident. This shows that the Shanghai church and its believers were at the forefront and played a significant role, with the Shanghai Chinese Christian Union becoming a point of contact for Christians nationwide. The Salvation Army openly opposed the massacre of Chinese people, advocating for support based on “justice.” Various organizations, including the National Christian Association (中華基督教青年會), the YWCA (女青年會), and the Shanghai Western Missionaries Association, mostly agreed to establish an investigative committee made up of both Chinese and Western individuals to follow up on the case. Some individual Western missionaries even acknowledged the responsibility of the British. However, on June 9, a missionary from the Inland Mission, Harry Westindge (惠司尼), during the public trial stated, “If I speak as a clergyman, I oppose killing, but if I speak as a lawyer and as a police officer, I have no choice but to open fire. I believe the provocateurs in this matter are the students, not the police.” Such remarks sparked a vehement reaction among Chinese believers, who felt there was an implicit bias favoring the British and demanded an apology from the missionary. There were also calls for the Inland Mission to reprimand the missionary for his testimony. Some Chinese believers within the Inland Mission believed that the clergyman had harmed the dignity of believers and violated doctrine, subsequently petitioning the mission’s leadership to order him back to the country. However, the leadership disregarded their concerns, stating that the Chinese believers were overly sensitive. Consequently, over 40 members of the Inland Mission publicly called upon all members to withdraw from the mission.

The May Thirtieth incident ignited anti-imperialist sentiments across the nation, leading to a xenophobic mass movement that further impacted Christianity and propelled the non-Christian movement to new heights.

  1. The Chinese Christian Promotion Association for the Abolition of Unequal Treaties, 1925 (Wang Zhixin (王治心) 1925; 1-2)

In 1925, the Chinese Christian Promotion Association for the Abolition of Unequal Treaties was established, initiated by Wang Zhixin (王治心) from Jinan Theological Seminary. The rationale for its formation pointed out that “the great powers oppress our nation through unequal treaties, causing us immense suffering.” Thus, this Christian spirit of struggle aims to resist such malevolent forces and promote the movement to abolish unequal treaties.

Wang emphasized that the association would maintain a non-partisan stance, allowing any Christian from the Republic of China to participate. Even if a Christian belonged to a specific political party, they could still join the movement as Christians, “but they must not use this association for activities related to any particular party.” This organization is entirely voluntary among Chinese Christians, while foreign friends can be supporting members but cannot be formal members.

Four reasons were presented for the establishment of the promotion association: (1) Christianity advocates for equality, opposing unequal treaties; (2) Many unequal treaties are related to church incidents, yet have little to do with Christianity itself. However, Christianity caused great harm upon entering China through these incidents, thus it bears significant responsibility and should launch efforts to abolish these treaties; (3) Many patriots criticize Christianity as imperialistic; therefore, to demonstrate the true stance of a Christian, one should oppose unequal treaties, showcasing the peaceful spirit and egalitarianism of Christ; (4) In 1925, the East China Christian Education Federation passed a resolution to abolish unequal treaties, making the formation of an organization necessary to realize this resolution.

As for the necessity of establishing the promotion association, four points were raised: (1) Christians seek progress for China; thus, they must eliminate obstacles to progress; (2) Christianity promotes peace, making it essential to oppose unequal treaties that lead to international conflict; (3) Christians are also citizens, with patriotic fervor, and for the sake of patriotism, this movement is necessary; (4) To display the complete character of Christians, unity is required to save the nation.

The specific work of the association focused on raising nationwide Christian awareness and support for the movement, while also reflecting to various Christian organizations worldwide, hoping for their advocacy towards their respective governments for treaty abolition. Key Theological Issues

  1. The Conflict Between Old and New Theologies Among Missionaries in China (Bates 1974: 135-158; Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2001: 21-23)

Entering the 20th century, with the rise of the Social Gospel and Modernism or Liberal Theology, whether in North America or in overseas mission fields, an increasing number of missionaries began to significantly revise the content of the gospel. To counter the challenge posed by Liberal Theology to traditional doctrines, Fundamentalists started to unite, becoming the “antithesis” of Liberal Theology on the stage of 20th century church history.

Fundamentalism refers to a religious or theological movement that originated in the United States, encompassing those conservative church members who arose to defend “fundamental” doctrines in dissatisfaction with Liberal Theology. They could not agree with the approach taken by Liberal Theology, which used reason as the sole standard for determining whether traditional doctrines should be abandoned in response to the Enlightenment’s challenges. They deeply felt the threat that the rise of Liberal Theology posed to Evangelicalism, particularly in its negation of the authority of Scripture, and thus united against theological modernism, often adopting a militant stance in opposition to Liberal Theology. Fundamentalists mainly defended five core doctrines: (1) the inspiration and infallibility of Scripture; (2) the deity of Christ, including His virgin birth; (3) the substitutionary atonement of Christ’s death; (4) the literal resurrection of Christ from the dead; (5) the literal return of Christ in the Second Advent.

The theological debate between Fundamentalism and Liberalism became an important event in 20th century missionary history, and the situation in China was no exception. In 1920, the renowned North American Fundamentalist W. H. Griffith Thomas visited China. After returning home, he expressed deep concern over the modernist tendencies among missionaries in China and published an article titled “Modernism in China,” which sparked wider controversy. For Fundamentalist missionaries, nearly all large-scale national Christian organizations in China were already dominated by Liberals at that time. Institutions such as the National Christian Council of China (中華全國基督教協進會) and the YMCA (中國基督教青年會) had seemingly become strongholds of Modernism. In 1926, due to theological differences, the China Inland Mission (中國內地會) and the Christian and Missionary Alliance (宣道會) successively withdrew from the National Christian Council of China, intensifying the opposition between the old and new theologies.

Yao Xiyi 姚西伊 pointed out that the focus of this theological debate in China mainly revolved around three major issues: the authority of Scripture, evangelism and social engagement, and the perspective on Chinese religions. In contrast, the intense debate between evolution and creationism that stirred up North America did not become a major “hot spot” in China (Yao Xiyi 1999: 194-204). Lian Xi 连曦 emphasized that it was “religious syncretism” rather than higher criticism that became one of the important characteristics of Chinese Liberal Theology. The debate between old and new theologies in China was not merely an imported “exotic product” from North America; the theological disputes of the 1920s and 1930s in China had a reflex influence (reflex influence) on North America (Lian 1997: 171-206).(2) Christianity and National Salvation (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1997a: 67-71; 1997b: 400-403)

During the Republic of China period, Christian intellectual elites bravely faced various challenges and committed themselves to literary creation. Through the publication of magazines like “Life” (生命), “Truth” (真理), “Truth and Life” (真理與生命), “Youth Progress” (青年進步), “Purple Crystal” (紫晶), “Literary Society” (文社), “Field Family” (田家), and “Heavenly Wind” (天風), they engaged in extensive discussions and practices about indigenizing theology and liturgy, reflecting on the relationship between Christian faith and the reconstruction of Chinese society, thus leaving a rich and valuable legacy for Chinese theology. National salvation or national reconstruction became the ultimate concern for the people of the 20th century. The crisis of reality gave rise to a cultural crisis, compelling intellectuals who cared about national affairs to review and explore the future of Chinese culture from a cultural and ideological perspective. For Chinese Christians, the question of what role Christianity would play in the reconstruction of Chinese society became a topic of their reflection. The theology of national salvation became an important focus of contextualization in the 20th century.

In examining 20th century Chinese history, many intellectuals, including Christians, tended to approach the issue of “national salvation” and cultural reconstruction with a monistic ideological framework. They were eager to find a way out for the dilemmas facing China and to reduce complex national, social, and cultural issues to fundamental “core” problems (such as moral corruption and imperialism). At the same time, they hoped to quickly find a fundamental solution to these problems and thereby promote the reconstruction of Chinese society. Thus, figures like “Mr. Democracy” (德先生), “Mr. Science” (賽先生), “Miss Beauty” (美小姐), “Confucius” (孔夫子, Kongfuzi), “Miss Moral Ethics” (穆姑娘), “Mr. Sun” (孫先生, Sun Yat-sen), and “Mr. Marx” (馬先生) all took to the historical stage, attempting to demonstrate their salvific functions. Just as various schools of thought were competing for recognition, “Mr. Christ” (基先生) also represented the Chinese church in this contest, hoping that Christianity could provide a remedy for the deep-seated problems in China, thus achieving the grand vision of “China returning to the Lord” (中華歸主).

Although the Chinese church viewed Christianity as the only solution to the problems facing China and as a “proprietary remedy” (according to Wang Zixin 王治心), there were varied understandings among Christians from different backgrounds regarding how Christianity could achieve national salvation. In other words, “Mr. Christ” presented different images to the public: (1) Jesus as the Restorer of Moral Personality. This perspective holds that the fundamental problem of Chinese culture lies in the renewal of personality and hearts, with Jesus being the model of ideal and perfect character. His love, sacrifice, and selflessness are seen as the remedy for China’s moral and spiritual issues and serve as the cultural foundation for social reconstruction. (2) Jesus as the Social Reformer. This group believes that China’s problems stem from various social deficiencies. Therefore, Christians should be dedicated to participating in social reforms on different levels (such as education and rural construction). The greatest mission of Jesus on earth was to promote social reform consciousness, serving as the basis for the coming of the Kingdom of Heaven. (3) Jesus as the Social Revolutionary. This faction emphasizes that to fundamentally solve China’s problems, mere individual improvements are insufficient; only through revolutionary means to establish new systems can the mission of national reconstruction be fulfilled. Jesus serves as a typical example of a revolutionary. How the revolution aligns with Christian ideals depends on various revolutionary positions—Jesus can support the Nationalist Revolution of the Three Principles of the People as well as endorse socialist revolutions.

In summary, as China faced a critical moment for survival and its culture underwent immense transformation, Chinese Christians in the 1920s sought to assert Christianity as the sole solution for national salvation by interpreting it as a faith with strong communal redemptive significance. Thus, Jesus not only became a “secular Messiah,” losing His divine essence, but also became an accessory to various secular national salvation proposals or ideologies. The different images of Jesus illustrate that Chinese Christians first identified the root causes of China’s problems before seeking remedies for those issues, ultimately packaging these solutions with Christianity. In essence, Christianity only played the role of rationalizing or sanctifying these secular proposals. The various representations of Jesus Christ in 20th century China reflect the dilemmas faced by the Chinese church when merging Christianity with national reconstruction.

  1. The National Christian Council and Indigenous Church (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1997: 64-70)

In May 1922, the National Christian Conference was held in Shanghai, with the theme “The Church in China.” The conference president Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡) emphasized in his opening speech that the greatest problem for the Chinese Church lay in its reliance on the thoughts, systems, and methods of others, stating, “The church has never questioned many things, nor does it have independent thought and judgment.”Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡) pointed out that the Chinese church, on one hand, should learn from the merits of Western churches, while on the other hand, it must express “the spirit of the nation, in a free and appropriate manner.” It cannot “blindly follow what others say and do, nor can it fully accept foreign customs, traditions, forms, systems, and methods as the best; accepting their interpretation of religious truth without probing its roots and applying their thoughts indiscriminately cannot be deemed ideal.” Therefore, to eliminate the foreign image of Christianity, the indigenization of Christianity becomes an urgent and inescapable issue.

At the conference, a “Declaration of the Church” was also issued. The content of the declaration was finalized by a committee composed entirely of Chinese individuals (the only committee at the conference without Western missionaries), after extensive and prolonged discussions. In the “Message to Fellow Workers” section, the declaration stated: “The history of China, the characteristics of the Chinese people, the nature of church affairs, experiential indications, and the rapid changes occurring within the country all call for a Chinese indigenous Christian church, in order to distinctly promote indigenous Christianity.” The term “indigenous” refers to the church “maintaining a continuous historical relationship with churches from various Christian traditions worldwide while also genuinely adapting to the inherent culture and spiritual experiences of the Chinese nation.” Therefore, the declaration emphasized the importance of Chinese believers reflecting on what constitutes an indigenous church: “We declare that the time has come for Chinese believers to cautiously study and boldly experiment, to determine their own church rituals and ceremonies, church organization and systems, as well as methods of evangelism and promotion, ensuring that everything assists the current church in becoming an indigenous Chinese church.” The declaration also called upon Western missionaries to assist Chinese believers in “this great task” and to “grant the Chinese believers unrestricted freedom in their experiments.”

So, how did the National Christian Council implement the reflections on constructing an indigenous church? After all, discussing indigenization without any concrete actions remains impractical. To this end, during the second annual meeting of the National Christian Council held in May 1924, the establishment of a standing organization called the “Indigenous Church Committee” was approved. Its members included Liu Yanfang (劉延芳, chair), Zhao Zichan (趙紫宸), C. G. Sparham (施伯珩), Cheng Jingyi (誠靜怡), M. C. Mullen (明思德), and Li Yaobang (李耀邦), who discussed detailed issues in five areas: (1) the relationship between the church and missions; (2) the issue of self-governance; (3) the organization and management of the church; (4) church unity; (5) expressions of indigenization. Shortly after, Liu Tingfang proposed to organize regional committees in Beijing, Jinan, Shanghai, Hankou, Fujian, and Guangzhou, to conduct regional investigations on various issues, including ancestor worship and marriage and funeral rites.

In May 1925, the council convened its third national meeting, during which the Indigenous Church Committee submitted a report detailing its work over the past year. The report particularly noted that due to restructuring related to the council’s affairs, the Higher Education Council, and the National Evangelical Society, the work of the Indigenous Church Committee had to be temporarily shelved, and even the meetings of all committee members could not be convened, causing the committee’s work to nearly stall. However, the committee still emphasized that the related work must continue and outlined directions for the following year’s work, including studying the enduring cultural elements of Chinese culture, such as preserving the virtue of respecting parents, as well as researching the Chinese church’s funeral and marriage rites. Ultimately, the annual meeting resolved that the Indigenous Church Committee must specifically “understand the customs of our country, so that Chinese Christians know how to commemorate and respect their ancestors,” and approved the lists of regional committee members, hoping to deepen discussions in various places in order to gather collective wisdom.

Unexpectedly, in the following year, the work of the Indigenous Church Committee remained stagnant. By the time the council convened its fourth annual meeting in October 1926, the committee was unable to submit any reports, having to resort to a “Report on the Study of Indigenous Church Issues” as a substitute. The annual meeting also did not pass any proposals regarding the committee or its members’ appointments. In other words, the Indigenous Church Committee became essentially defunct, and it would not reappear in the fifth annual meeting.

Why did the committee’s work stagnate so soon after it began? According to the explanations in the “Report on the Study of Indigenous Church Issues,” this was primarily due to the impact of changing political circumstances. In May 1925, the shocking “May Thirtieth Incident” occurred, triggering nationwide anti-imperialist demonstrations and pushing the already rising tide of anti-imperialism to another peak.In the context of an unstable situation, “the regional committees have yet to be established, the circumstances have become stagnant, contributions are nonexistent, and there is no talk of progress, which is indeed deeply regrettable.”

Why did the committee’s work stagnate so soon after it began? According to the explanations in the “Report on the Study of Indigenous Church Issues,” this was primarily due to the impact of changing political circumstances. In May 1925, the shocking “May Thirtieth Incident” occurred, triggering nationwide anti-imperialist demonstrations and pushing the already rising tide of anti-imperialism to another peak.In the context of an unstable situation, “the regional committees have yet to be established, the circumstances have become stagnant, contributions are nonexistent, and there is no talk of progress, which is indeed deeply regrettable.”

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Cai Yuanpei (Cài Yuánpéi, 1868-1940), a native of Shaoxing, Zhejiang, was the second-place winner in the 1892 imperial examination and was appointed a shujishi at the Hanlin Academy. He paid attention to current affairs and Western learning and later sympathized with the revolution. With the founding of the Republic of China, he served as Minister of Education. After Yuan Shikai (Yuán Shìkǎi) assumed the presidency, he continued to serve as the Minister of Education for the Beijing government. However, he resigned a few months later due to dissatisfaction with Yuan’s autocracy. In 1916, he was appointed President of Peking University, fostering an atmosphere of intellectual freedom and becoming an important figure in the New Culture Movement while supporting students during the May Fourth Movement. In October 1927, he became the President of the Academia Sinica (Zhonghua Minguo Daxueyuan, 中華民國大學院) and the following year was appointed the president of the Central Research Institute. After the September 18 Incident, he supported resistance against Japan and participated in the Cultural Salvage Association in Shanghai. At the end of 1937, he moved from Shanghai to Hong Kong, where he passed away in 1940.

In 1917, Cai published “Using Aesthetic Education to Replace Religion” in the journal New Youth (Xīn Qīngnián, 新青年), discussing the role of religion from the perspectives of intellect, will, and emotion. He pointed out that due to advancements in science, knowledge and will have become “independent,” and that the remaining emotional influence of religion could be replaced by “aesthetic education.” This article sparked discussions on religious issues among intellectuals during the New Culture Movement.

In April 1922, the Beijing Non-Religious Alliance held a conference, where Cai delivered a speech stating that “various religions today are constrained by outdated views, using absurd rituals and exaggerated propaganda to gain blind faith from the uneducated, thus supporting the livelihood of missionary leaders.” Therefore, religion is undoubtedly “an external force invading the personal spiritual realm,” infringing on human rights. He particularly opposed church schools and the YMCA, which, “through various suggestions, seduce underage students to believe in their Christianity.” Thus, he strongly advocated for “educational independence,” which must “remain above all sects.” Specifically, he proposed (1) that universities could offer courses on the history of religion and comparative religion, but not on theology; (2) that no school should have doctrinal courses or hold prayer sessions; (3) that those engaged in missionary work should not be involved in education. Addressing the World Student Christian Federation’s annual meeting, Cai, adhering to his consistent principle that religion should not infiltrate education, questioned why these students “would want to carry a Christian title,” and asked Tsinghua University why it would “allow a religious federation to use its venue.” He supported the establishment of a “Non-Religious Alliance,” reaffirming that this does not hinder “freedom of belief,” as “believing in religion is freedom, and not believing in religion is also freedom.” “Since we have this ‘non-religious’ belief and encounter the necessity of this ‘non-religious’ movement, we shall freely pursue our movement without any reservations!”

Cai Yuanpei’s advocacy for the separation of religion and education was implemented when he became the President of the University (Daxueyuan, 大學院) in 1927. In December, the University announced regulations for establishing private universities and specialized schools, as well as regulations for private secondary schools and elementary schools, embodying the spirit of separating religion from education. Various church schools had no choice but to respond to the demand to relinquish educational authority, marking a new phase for Christianity in China.

  1. Wu Zhihui (Wú Zhìhuī, 1866-1953), a native of Wujin, Jiangsu, joined the Tongmenghui (Chinese Revolutionary Alliance) in France in 1905. In 1912, he established the French Study Association and the following year served as the chairman of the Unified Phonetic Meeting at the Ministry of Education, promoting the phonetic system for the national language. He strongly advocated for the Kuomintang to purge its ranks, and in 1927, he became a member of the Central Supervisory Committee of the Kuomintang. In 1946, the National Government convened the Constitutional National Assembly, where Wu served as the chairman of the constitutional representatives. He was elected as a representative of the National Assembly in 1947, and the following year, he was elected president of the Academia Sinica, serving as an advisor to the presidential office. In 1949, he moved to Taiwan, where he died in 1953.

As the initiator and drafter of the “Non-Christian Alliance,” in August 1924, Wu published “Christianity at the End of Coercion” in the “Awakening” supplement of the Republic Daily (Minguo Ribao, 民國日報). This article was also Wu’s speech at the founding conference of the “Non-Christian Alliance.” Using his personal observations in Britain and France as examples, he noted that clergy are often “false believers” who gain “some power” through this pretense, and “many people do not attend church,” while there is a significant anti-Christian sentiment in France. “Today, Westerners are exceptionally diligent in social causes, advocating for freedom of thought, free from any shackles.”

With the rise of socialism, “everyone is making efforts to alleviate the suffering of the poor and oppressed,” leading him to conclude that “rather than relying on God, we should each work hard to address our own lives.”Regarding the situation in China, it is said that the missionaries coming to China are “those who have exhausted their options in their own countries,” and what they do in China is merely “capturing hearts” to make “ordinary fools at home” praise “these imported ruffians.” Thus, “when will China have a day of strength?” Ultimately, the article concludes with the author’s position: “I do not oppose Christianity simply for the sake of opposition; rather, I believe it is unnecessary. I am fully aware that there is no God above me, and only in the last twenty years have I clarified my thoughts about God. I believe that regardless of whether there is a God or not, if people want to commit evil, they will commit evil; how can we be afraid of the devil binding us when we base our actions on reason? If China has become a socialist country, there is no need for us to organize a Non-Christian Alliance. As for the age of great harmony, there is even less need to mention it, at that time, we may no longer exist because we will have perished. Why should we oppose Christianity today? Because those foreign bigwigs of religion all want our Chinese compatriots to become ‘the bones on the banks of the Unfixed River,’ forever buried in the deep mountains and the depths of the sea.”

  1. Tang Gongxian (Táng Gōngxǐan, 1898-1938) was from Suichang, Zhejiang. He entered the Zhejiang Provincial First Normal School in 1917, during which he was influenced by new ideas. In 1921, he adopted communist ideology and joined the Hangzhou Socialist Youth League in 1922. He became a member of the Communist Party in 1923 and was one of the first five members in Zhejiang Province. During the first United Front, he joined the Kuomintang in his personal capacity, but was expelled from the party in 1925. He continued to work as a leftist within the Kuomintang. After the split between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, he was arrested in 1927 during the Kuomintang’s purge and was sentenced to 12 years in prison. After being granted amnesty and released in 1932, he continued to be reformed. He passed away in 1938.

When the “Non-Christian Alliance” was restructured in August 1924, Tang served as the chairman of the executive committee. In October 1924, he published “Observing Christianity from the New Testament: No Absolute Room for Existence” in the “Awakening” supplement of the Republic Daily (Minguo Ribao, 民國日報), condemning Christianity as a tool for the imperialist powers to invade China. In this article, he stated that the Jesus recorded in the New Testament “is merely a illegitimate child,” and the matter of virgin birth “cannot be explained scientifically.” “If we believe in ‘virgin birth,’ then it can be said that all illegitimate children in the world can also be called ‘born of the Holy Spirit.’ Therefore, those who adhere to the faith should also worship these illegitimate children and hold Jesus Christ in the same reverence.” He then pointed out that the content of the New Testament is “fabricated lies,” used to “deceive ordinary fools,” “promote absolute superstition,” “suppress people’s freedom,” and “create chaos in the world,” while also “disdaining the dignity of women.” Thus, his conclusion was: “Christianity is a falsehood and deception, worshipping idols, disturbing the world, despising women, devoid of freedom, equality, and any meaning of brotherhood; it is the lackey of imperialism and colonial plunder.”

  1. Li Weihan (Lǐ Wéihán, 1896-1984), a native of Changsha, Hunan, co-founded the New People’s Study Association with Mao Zedong (Māo Zédōng) and others in April 1918. During his time in France for labor-study in 1919, he was influenced by Marxism. In 1922, he, along with Zhou Enlai (Zhōu Ēnlái) and others, established the European League of Young Chinese Communists, and by the end of the year, he returned to China and joined the Chinese Communist Party. He became an early member of the Communist Party and played an important role in the Chinese communist movement.

In 1924, Li, then the secretary of the Hunan Provincial Committee of the Communist Party, wrote that the imperialist policy of invading China can be divided into economic, political, and cultural forms.

His explanation of cultural aggression is as follows: “Cultural aggression is manifested in two ways: church schools and Christian churches. In a church school, everything is subject to interference from outsiders, and there may even be armed police monitoring class time, prohibiting teachers from discussing patriotism or politics. The teachings of the Christian church claim that physical pleasures do not need to be considered, stating that after death, the soul enjoys equal happiness in heaven. This tells the Chinese people to remain as slaves to outsiders and not to rise up in resistance. It also teaches us to love our enemies as we love our brothers. Our public enemy is the outsider; should we love him as a brother?! Moreover, foreigners are often given opportunities to indoctrinate the Chinese people through missionary work, where aid organizations are led by outsiders, thus giving foreigners influence over the beliefs of the people, which penetrates into civil society. Chinese educators are mostly students who have studied in the United States, and now that the U.S. has imposed restrictions, it allows Chinese students only to attend church schools; isn’t this a very dangerous piece of news?!”

After the establishment of the People’s Republic of China, he became the first head of the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee.(5) Yun Daiying (Yùn Dàiyīng, 1895-1931), a native of Wuchang, Hubei. During his early years studying in Wuchang, he came into contact with the YMCA. Although he appreciated some aspects, he ultimately refused to become a Christian. In 1919, he joined the Young China Association and a year later organized the Chinese Socialist Youth League. He then joined the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and became an early member. During the first cooperation between the Nationalist Party and the Communist Party, he joined the Kuomintang (Nationalist Party). In 1926, he was elected as a member of the Central Executive Committee of the Kuomintang and served as a political instructor at the Whampoa Military Academy. After the split between the Nationalists and the Communists, he participated in the uprisings in Nanchang and Guangzhou. In 1930, he was arrested in Shanghai and executed in Nanjing in April 1931.

In November and December 1923, Yun Daiying published articles in the journal Chinese Youth (Zhōngguó Qīngnián) opposing Christianity. In the first article, he countered the YMCA’s argument of “saving the nation through personality” by asking, “Who has destroyed China through lack of personality?” One of the culprits was precisely the “benefactors” from Britain and the United States associated with the YMCA. These “believers” rely on the protection of foreign capital, saying: “You say, ‘Personality saves the nation,’ that sounds really good!” In another article, he listed various reasons to oppose Christianity: (1) religion hinders human evolution; (2) the education, social, and medical work of Christianity is aimed at “fooling people into becoming their disciples”; (3) the YMCA merely promotes “Western happiness” and has no relation to society; (4) the content of Christianity undermines old beliefs in China; (5) believers are “foreign slaves,” with “every additional Christian being one more foreign slave”; (6) Christianity colludes with foreign forces; (7) Christianity has an “exclusivist” nature; (8) Jesus himself is part of a “conquered nation,” so how can it be said that “the teachings of Jesus can save China”? (9) Christianity is “a tool used by foreigners to soften China”; (10) Christians are protected by extraterritorial rights.

In May 1926, he delivered a speech titled “Jesus, Confucius, and Revolutionary Youth” at Lingnan University during the Non-Christian Movement period. In his speech, he praised many teachers and students at Lingnan for being comrades in the anti-imperialist movement, expressing great happiness to deliver a speech at “such an anti-imperialist Lingnan University.”

In his speech, Yun Daiying declared that he did not belittle Jesus and regarded him as a “sage” superior to Confucius, because Jesus, unlike Confucius, “only preached persuasion,” possessed a “revolutionary spirit,” and “like our current revolutionary party, was close to propagating to the masses,” even rebuking oppressors. However, he noted that both Confucius’s exhortations and Jesus’s rebukes could not save the world, and only revolution’s means of “fighting” would do. Furthermore, today, Jesus’s disciples witnessing his rebuking often incur death, and as a result, “they learn the teachings of Jesus,” yet do not possess the same “courage” to oppose imperialism as Jesus did.

Yun acknowledged that there were many good people in the church, but they “could neither admonish against the brutality of imperialism and warlords… nor dared to support or advocate for the Chinese common people to resist imperialism and warlord actions,” instead “clinging to the teachings of peaceful tolerance,” and propagating the doctrine of peace and tolerance. Thus, his opposition to Christianity was precisely because many good Christian individuals did not only refrain from revolution but also “discouraged others from revolution,” even “enticing many to stray from the revolutionary path to overthrow imperialism and warlords.” In his writings, Yun did not shy away from advocating the closure of externally-established schools, because “foreign teachers love their own countries.” While they may wish to bring happiness to our Chinese people, they absolutely do not want the Chinese students they educate to oppose their own country or to be unfriendly to their imperialist government. In their schools, they only discuss how the imperialist government “is good to us,” while concealing their own evils. Whenever students rise up in resistance, they “assist the imperialist government in oppressing our students.” Therefore, he stated: “Every Chinese youth who enters a school run by the British means one less person opposing British imperialism… The more developed these foreign schools become, the fewer individuals there will be who resist imperialism.” In his speech, he called on all students at Lingnan University to become “comrades against imperialism.” “Although educated by American teachers, we must sternly oppose American imperialists.” At the same time, he urged Lingnan University to abolish Bible courses and worship services, as forcing students to study the Bible and participate in prayers violates the freedom of belief.”Do not let these myths and superstitions disturb the minds of our youth, wasting our youthful time and spirit.” On the contrary, Chinese youth “must rebel.” He further mocked whether Christianity could change the world, stating, “Christians have prayed for over two thousand years, yet they have not seen the world improved through prayer; instead, numerous imperialists and warlords have emerged.”

In June 1926, Yun Daiying (Yùn Dàiyīng) published an article titled “Against the Cultural Aggression of Imperialism” in Guangdong Youth (Guǎngdōng Qīngnián), providing a detailed analysis of cultural aggression. He categorized the cultural policies utilized by imperialists to “soften and subdue weaker nations” under the umbrella of “cultural invasion,” which included missionary work, church schools, receiving international students, publishing, speeches, and more. Through these methods, imperialists conveyed four kinds of ideas: (1) promoting the superstitions of God, Jesus, heaven, and hell, making weaker nations oblivious to their immediate suffering while hoping for future happiness in a nebulous afterlife; (2) glorifying the civilization of imperialist countries while concealing their social evils, causing weaker nations to be dazzled and misled, willingly submitting and acknowledging imperialist nations as cultural superiors; (3) advocating a one-sided doctrine of peace and brotherhood, unable and unwilling to criticize or oppose the evils of imperialism that oppress weaker nations. Therefore, their so-called peace and brotherhood merely serve to assist imperialists in ensuring weaker nations do not resist their oppression; (4) promoting the virtues of imperialists towards weaker nations while remaining silent about the atrocities committed by imperialists against these nations, cherry-picking and greatly publicizing a few seemingly beneficial actions, instilling gratitude in weaker nations, thereby rendering them unwilling subjects.

Yun pointed out that 8,000 foreign missionaries were spread across China, establishing churches and recruiting followers, with over 300,000 people participating in communion nationwide. Additionally, there were more than 40 YMCA chapters in various cities, with membership reaching 8,000. The churches had established over 2,000 schools, with a total enrollment of 100,000 students. All of this was “nurtured and misled directly and indirectly by imperialism, unaware of or too afraid to resist it.” British and American imperialists were actively expanding church endeavors, using compensation from the Boxer Rebellion as funding for cultural invasion, fundamentally eradicating the spirit and movements of the Chinese nation. Thus, it was essential to oppose this “cultural invasion.” In conclusion, he called for “the Christian church and church schools to immediately sever ties with imperialism,” “to oppose imperialists operating any cultural enterprises in China,” and urged Christians and church students to rise together and participate in the anti-cultural invasion movement.

  1. Zhang Zhenzhi / Zhang Zhenzhen (Zhāng Zhènzhèn, 1929: 1-110)

Zhang Zhenzhi (筆名振振, Zhèng Zhènzhèn, dates of birth and death unknown), a member of the Kuomintang, worked at the Central Party Headquarters and published several works, including “The Pathology of Chinese Society at Present” (1929), “The Triad Theory of Party, Government, and People” (1929), “Interpretation of the Difficulties in Knowing and Acting,” “Revolution and Religion” (1929), and “Critique of Hu Shi’s Anti-Party Essays” (1930). Among these, “Revolution and Religion” compiled his critical articles on religion, particularly Christianity. As he mentioned in the preface, “The purpose of this book is to delineate the divisions between religious thought and revolutionary thought. The advancement of revolutionary thought raises the question of whether it can tolerate the existence of religious thought; that is, whether the revolution aims to abolish religion and destroy it.” In addressing this question, he clearly reached the conclusion: “I affirmatively recognize that under the thought of revolution, there can be no strong presence of religious thought; any reaction or widespread propagation of religious thought must be condemned.”

The book comprises two parts: the first part discusses “the issues of Christianity,” critiquing Christianity and detailing the debate between him and the Christian Zhang Shizhang (Zhāng Shìzhāng) in the “Awakening” supplement of the Republic Daily (Minguo Ribao). The second part, “Folklore Discussions,” primarily critiques folk religions and beliefs.

In Zhang Zhenzhi’s view, the evils of Christianity in China include: (1) serving as a protective cover for the military-economic aggression of imperialists; (2) accumulating followers such that congregants gradually turn all their property over to the church; (3) interference in politics by pastors and priests in the interior, leaving both Chinese and Western officials unable to act against them; (4) using congregants to oppress non-believing citizens. Therefore, he believes that “overthrowing Christianity” is morally justified. He even advocated for the confiscation of all church institutions nationwide for use as labor schools. He did not agree with the principle of “freedom of belief” because belief itself is “superstitious.” Under “superstitious belief,” there is fundamentally no freedom. Thus, “a person only has freedom if they do not believe; once they believe, their freedom is seized by the religion.” As a member of the Kuomintang, he was further opposed to “party-religion collaboration.”Because the Kuomintang could not collaborate with Christianity associated with imperialism. As a revolutionary party, the Kuomintang only believes in one ideology and reveres the leader, stating that “there is only freedom for the Party, not for the individual.” Thus, both the Party and religion are “uniquely singular”; religion is in opposition to the Party, and it must be overthrown.

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. Shanghai “Anti-Christian Student Federation,” 1922 (Zhāng Qīnshì 1928: 187-190; Lutz 1988: 59-60)

In response to the World Christian Student Federation’s 11th annual meeting held in Beijing in April 1922, an organization named “Anti-Christian Student Federation” was established in Shanghai in March. On March 9, the federation sent a telegram to “students of Qinghua School in Beijing and students from schools across the nation,” stating that the concepts of separation of church and state, as well as the separation of education and religion, have been prevalent in Europe since the Renaissance. However, in modern times, Christianity has become the “forerunner of colonization,” a “spiritual enslavement,” and in the face of the threats of “Christianization and capitalization,” the World Christian Student Federation gathered in “the capital of my weak nation.” Thus, they called upon “everyone to speak out together and denounce oppression, in the hope of forever purifying our youth education.”

On the same day, the Shanghai “Anti-Christian Student Federation” also issued a declaration condemning Christianity and Christian churches as the root of many historical evils. It claimed that modern Christianity and Christian churches have become demons that assist the “bourgeoisie” in plundering and oppressing the “proletariat,” thus becoming a “cruel, oppressive, tragic capitalist society” and “demons that aid the wicked.” The declaration also pointed out that countries establish churches in China to “tempt the Chinese people to welcome capitalism” and to “cultivate kind-hearted lackeys for capitalists.” Therefore, while opposing capitalism, the Chinese people must also oppose “the modern Christianity and Christian churches that deceive the common people in support of capitalism.”

The declaration concluded that the “World Christian Student Federation” is also a product of modern Christianity and the churches, and its annual meetings are “robber gatherings that insult our nation’s youth, deceive our people, and plunder our economy.” Thus, they decided to “declare war” against it.

In the April 15, 1922 issue of The Pioneer, the fourth volume, there was an edition titled “Anti-Christian Student Federation.” This is the official newspaper of the Chinese Socialist Youth Corps, containing the “Anti-Christian Student Federation Declaration, Telegram, Articles of Association,” “Christianity and World Reconstruction” (Chì Guāng), “Christianity and Capitalism” (Lú Shú), “Christianity and Communism” (Qǐ Yuán), and “Christianity and Women” (Zhè Huá) among other articles.

In April 1922, the Anti-Christian Student Federation became a permanent organization and simultaneously sought more members under the names “Anti-Christian Student Federation” and “Anti-Christian Federation.” On April 20, the Shanghai Daily published an announcement in its “Awareness” supplement titled “Recruitment Notice for Comrades of the Anti-Christian Federation,” stating that invading powers exploit Christianity to “enslave the spirit of colonies and nations under invasion.”

  1. Beijing “Anti-Religion Federation,” 1922 (Zhāng Qīnshì 1928: 193-199; Lutz 1988: 59-60)

Following the Shanghai Anti-Christian Student Federation, the “Anti-Religion Federation” was established in Beijing. On March 21, 1922, Li Shizeng, Xiao Zisheng, Li Dazhao, Miao Boying, and 79 others jointly issued a telegram to newspapers, schools, organizations, and the people nationwide, stating that “the poison of religion is rampant… heretical doctrines are widespread,” and that “the Christian students of the world” are about to “promote superstition” in the capital. Therefore, they organized the “Anti-Religion Federation,” proclaiming “the spirit of scientific inquiry, illuminating the brilliance of evolution.” On April 1, they issued a second telegram reaffirming that the federation is not “exclusive” but simply “non-religious” in its pursuit of “science over religion.”

In their declaration, they clearly stated that religion is incompatible with scientific truth and entirely against humanitarianism; therefore, “religion and humanity cannot coexist.” Although defined as “non-religious,” they specifically targeted Christianity, claiming that it uses youth associations to “incite students,” with various activities being “poisonous anesthetics, hypnotism.” They welcomed “anyone who does not believe in religion or wishes to eliminate the harm of religion” to join as comrades of the Anti-Religion Federation.

  1. Shanghai “Anti-Christian Federation,” 1924 (Zhāng Qīnshì 1928: 376-380; 293-315)

In August 1924, the “Anti-Christian Federation” was reorganized, with Tang Gongxian (Táng Gōngxiàn) serving as the chairman and Wu Zhihui (Wú Zhìhuī) drafting the articles. Out of the five executive committee members, three were from the Communist Party of China (Tang, Kè Bónián, and Zhāng Qiūrén).

On August 19, the federation’s declaration and articles were published in the Shanghai Daily’s “Awareness” supplement. The declaration began by stating the reasons for opposing Christianity, including both “general” and “specific” meanings.In terms of “general significance,” opposing Christianity is similar to opposing other religions, primarily targeting the “superstitions” inherent in religion, which “calls forth a false notion of happiness.” This is because the hoped-for happiness is posited in the future, and thus there is a tendency to “undervalue the material struggle of the physical world, believing that such struggles are futile.” “All religions share these same evils, but Christianity is particularly organized and powerful, and its harm is especially deep and extensive; therefore, we should specifically oppose Christianity.” Additionally, opposing Christianity has its “specific significance,” as Christianity has served as a tool for the “privileged classes” both during the “feudal era” and the “capitalist era.” Especially in the capitalist era, it has been used to “narcotize the working class of our nation,” making them believe that the social disparities of wealth and poverty “are ordained by divine will,” hence “a struggle between classes” is seen as violating divine will; or it has been used to “narcotize the conquered colonized and semi-colonized peoples,” convincing them that the armed forces of the colonies come “to bestow the blessings of God, bringing education and all culture,” thereby making them “hesitant to resist.” This is precisely “the greatest reason why our Chinese people must particularly oppose Christianity.” Moreover, missionaries arriving in China, whether engaged in “preaching or education,” both intentionally and unintentionally propagate “international capitalist concepts to undermine China’s national consciousness and patriotism.”

After the 1924 reorganization, the Anti-Christian Federation systematically planned activities against Christianity, including publishing literature and holding speeches while conducting research on Christianity and its undertakings. The Kuomintang’s Shanghai Daily “Awareness” supplement regularly published “Anti-Christian Special Issues” as the official publication of the Anti-Christian Federation. For instance, the January 1925 “editorial” titled “An Unprecedented Anti-Christian Movement” asserts that “if imperialism is not fundamentally toppled, the influence of Christianity cannot be eradicated at its roots. If we truly wish to oppose Christianity, we must certainly strive against the imperialist movement.” “Opposing Christianity is also a method of opposing imperialism—a method of indirect resistance; at the same time, it can also diminish the forces of imperialism and expose the evils of imperialism.” “We hope that compatriots unwilling to yield to the influence of imperialism and Christianity will exert extra effort in the anti-Christian movement, making this Christmas a day for the anti-Christian movement.” Similarly, publications from the Communist Party of China (such as The Guide and Chinese Youth) also published a significant number of articles criticizing Christianity.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Anti-Christian Movement (非基督教運動), 1922-1927 (Lutz 1988; Yang Tianhong 楊天宏 1994: 122-220, 327-422; Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1994: 135-136)

In the 1920s, the significant challenge faced by Christianity in China was undoubtedly the “Anti-Christian Movement” (非基督教運動) (1922-1927). “Non-Christian” (非基督教) equates to “anti-Christian,” but in this context, “opposition” or “non” does not merely refer to a general questioning or rejection of Christian beliefs. Rather, it pertains to a mass mobilization movement that attacked Christianity through propaganda and even violent means. The organizers of the movement were political parties and their affiliated groups, whose accusations against Christianity were rooted in a strong ideological struggle with “anti-imperialism.”

The anti-Christian movement can be divided into two main phases. The first phase erupted in April 1922 in response to the World Student Christian Federation’s annual meeting held in Beijing. The Anti-Christian Student Federation (非基督教學生同盟) and the Non-Religious Federation (非宗教同盟) were established successively in Shanghai and Beijing, later spreading to other cities (such as Beijing, Wuhu, Taiyuan, Baoding, Tianjin, Hangzhou, Tangshan, Nanchang, Xiamen, Wuchang, Guangzhou, Changsha, etc.), where different anti-Christian student alliances or non-religious federations were formed between March and April. Some scholars, based on Soviet archives, pointed out that the anti-Christian movement of 1922 was not a spontaneous movement; rather, it was initiated and led by the Chinese Communist Party under the direct guidance of the Soviet Communist Party and the Far Eastern Bureau of the Communist International, involving members of organizations like the Kuomintang (KMT) in this political struggle (Tao Feiya 陶飛亞 2003a: 122).

The initial phase of the anti-Christian movement was primarily directed against the World Student Christian Federation’s annual meeting. Although the struggle’s target also included Christianity, the Shanghai Anti-Christian Student Federation rebranded itself as the Anti-Christian Federation (非基督教同盟) in April. However, as the annual meeting concluded, the objectives of the struggle or opposition diminished, affecting the movement’s mobilization capacity.

In 1924, the reorganization of the Shanghai “Anti-Christian Federation” marked the beginning of the second phase of the anti-Christian movement. This phase was closely linked to the First United Front between the KMT and the CCP. In January 1924, the KMT held its First National Congress in Guangzhou, where Sun Yat-sen (孫中山) proposed three major policies: “unite with the Soviet Union, unite with the Communist Party, and support farmers and workers,” allowing Communist Party members to join the KMT as individuals. In the “Proclamation of the First National Congress of the Kuomintang” (中國國民黨第一次全國代表大會宣言), it explicitly stated that imperialism and warlords were the root causes of chaos in China and that only by opposing imperialism could the freedom and independence of the Chinese nation be attained. Consequently, both parties actively engaged in leading various anti-imperialist movements across the country, under the anti-imperialist appeal, “anti-Christian” became the target of the “anti-imperialist” struggle. The “Anti-Christian Federation” became a crucial platform for the KMT-CCP collaboration against imperialism, with both parties leveraging their respective propaganda publications (such as the KMT’s “Republic Daily” (民國日報) and the CCP’s “China Youth” (中國青年), “Guide” (嚮導)) to publish numerous articles accusing Christianity of being a tool of imperialist aggression in China. At this time, “cultural invasion” became a significant narrative for the CCP’s criticism of Christianity (Tao Feiya 陶飛亞 2003b).

In December 1924, the Anti-Christian Federation called for the establishment of anti-Christian federations nationwide and designated the week before and after Christmas as “Anti-Christian Movement Week,” mobilizing crowds to organize protests, distribute anti-Christian flyers, and hold large meetings. As a result, cities such as Changsha, Guangzhou, Suzhou, Jiujiang, Shanghai, Shandong, Shaoxing, Taiyuan, Ningbo, and Qingdao responded. An editorial titled “Awakening” in the January 1925 issue of “Republic Daily” reiterated, “Opposing Christianity is also a way to oppose imperialism… It can eradicate the forces of imperialism and expose the evils of imperialism” (Chen Tong 1925).

In 1925, following the “May Thirtieth Incident” (五卅慘案), the nationwide anti-imperialist sentiment surged, leading to boycotts of businesses, strikes, and school boycotts; the anti-Christian mass movement also escalated towards violence. In 1926, during the Northern Expedition, wherever the National Revolutionary Army deployed, mass movements were orchestrated in unison, impacting Christian churches, schools, and hospitals, which were also occupied by the Northern Expedition troops. Amidst the severe political situation, missionaries began to flee in various regions.

By 1927, a new situation developed in China, with the establishment of the Nationalist Government (國民政府) in Nanjing. On the other hand, the Kuomintang began to purge the party, causing the First United Front to collapse. At this point, the Kuomintang had become the ruling party of a unified regime, needing to publicly affirm the freedom of belief while also opposing mass movement-style anti-religious incidents. For instance, the KMT’s Central Executive Committee passed a directive in 1927 stating that “the public must not misunderstand the opposition to imperialism as a nature of exclusion or anti-religion, nor utilize any force to suppress or harm the freedom of belief of both Chinese and foreign people.” Against this backdrop, the once CCP-led anti-Christian movement was poised to come to an end.However, there remained a strong anti-Christian sentiment within the Kuomintang (KMT), though this “opposition” no longer took the form of mass mobilization. Instead, it was operated at the ideological level. After 1927, many KMT members continued to oppose Christianity in their personal or party capacities, harboring animosity towards believers under the guise of their religious stance. In December 1928, the KMT still published a special issue of “Republic Daily” (民國日報) titled “Anti-Christian Special.” In January 1929, it even included a substantial publication, “Study on Unequal Treaties” (不平等條約研究集), exceeding a hundred pages, detailing how Christianity conducted cultural invasion with extensive text and numerous charts.

  1. Educational Rights Movement (收回教育權運動), 1922-1927 (Yang Tianhong 楊天宏 1994: 241-276; Yang Cuihua 楊翠華 1984: 235-289; Lutz 1988: 91-159)

The Educational Rights Movement was an essential component of the Anti-Christian Movement and was ultimately operationalized through government regulations. Its main focus targeted the educational enterprises established by Christianity in China.

According to the report “Christian Education in China: The Report of the China Education Commission of 1921-1922” published in 1922, there were 6,890 church-operated primary and secondary schools in the country, with a student population of 199,604. When accounting for various categories of Christian education institutions (such as Bible schools, theological seminaries, special education institutions, orphanages, and nursing training), there were 7,382 Christian educational institutions nationwide, serving 214,254 students. At that time, the total number of students at all levels in the nation was 5,700,000. The students enrolled in Christian educational institutions only accounted for 3.76% of the total student population. However, in higher education, students attending Christian universities (2,017) comprised 7.9% of the total student population at national universities (25,373) (China Education Commission 1922: 371-374).

After the establishment of the Republic of China, Christian education began to attract attention from the Chinese intellectual community. Many educators (such as Cai Yuanpei 蔡元培) advocated for the separation of religion and education, specifically criticizing the religious curricula and activities within church schools. The proselytization aimed at minors received particular condemnation from educational scholars. During the wave of nationalism in the 1920s, students participated in educational movements out of patriotic sentiment, and church schools were no exception. However, missionaries leading these church schools adopted a high-pressure approach to prohibit such activities, leading to conflicts and tensions between students and school administration. During this period, some students protested against “enslaving” education. Consequently, church schools found themselves positioned against Chinese nationalism.

In the 1920s, the intellectual community further addressed the relationship between religion and education in China. In December 1921, the China Educational Reform Society (中華教育改進社) was established, with board members including Cai Yuanpei, Huang Yanpei, and Zhang Boling. At its annual meeting in July the following year, Hu Shi (胡適) proposed that “no religious education (including theories and rituals) shall be included in elementary schools (including kindergartens),” a suggestion that received support from Tao Menghe (陶孟和) and Ding Wenjiang (丁文江). After the meeting, the China Educational Reform Society sent its stance to various church and religious schools. In July 1924, during its third annual meeting, the society further proposed a motion to reclaim educational rights: “Requesting efforts to reclaim educational rights,” “No one without Chinese nationality shall implement national education on Chinese people within the territory of the Republic of China,” and “Requesting the prohibition of foreigners establishing schools in China.” The amendments to the last three proposals were passed, primarily requesting the government to enact stringent school registration regulations, stating that “any foreigners found to be using schools to conduct aggression should be ordered by the government to cease operations.”

In October 1924, the Tenth Annual Meeting of the National Education Federation convened, passing proposals regarding the reclamation of educational rights. In 1925, the “Chinese Educational Community” published a special issue on the “Reclamation of Educational Rights Movement,” reaffirming that education is a matter of national sovereignty. However, due to the warlord fragmentation in China at that time and the absence of a unified government, the educational community’s recommendations to the government lacked the foundation for objective realization.

In February 1926, the Educational Administrative Committee was established by the National Government in Guangzhou, headed by Xu Chongqing (許崇清), a supporter of the reclamation of educational rights. In June, a draft policy for the separation of religion and education was published.In October, regulations such as the “Regulations for Private Schools,” “Regulations for Establishing Boards of Directors in Private Schools,” and “School Registration Regulations” were promulgated, requiring church schools to register and prohibiting the offering of religious courses as well as forcing students to participate in religious activities. In July, under Chiang Kai-shek’s (蔣介石) leadership, the Kuomintang began the Northern Expedition. As the National Revolutionary Army advanced, various warlord regimes gradually changed. By April 1927, the National Government was established with Nanjing as its capital. With the end of the Northern Expedition the following year, China returned to a unified regime. The Nanjing National Government established the “Academy of Universities,” with Cai Yuanpei as president. Based on the foundation of this unified regime, the call for the reclamation of educational rights was finally realized, and Christian education was incorporated into the Chinese educational system. All levels of church schools were required to register with the government, the principals had to be Chinese, over half of the board members had to be Chinese nationals, and foreigners could not serve as chairpersons of the board. Additionally, religious studies could not be a required subject, nor could students be compelled to participate in religious rituals.

By 1931, except for St. John’s University, West China Union University, and South China Women’s College of Arts and Sciences, the other ten Christian universities had registered with the Chinese government. In terms of primary and secondary schools, by around 1929, approximately 70% of church schools had completed or were in the process of registering.

  1. The Evacuation (傳教士大撤退), 1927 (Boynton 1928: 155-159; Zhang Junjun 張君俊 1928: 1-6; Jiang Diaozi 蔣調之 1928: 6-10)

In 1924, the number of missionaries in China reached a historical peak of 7,663. However, during 1927, nearly 3,000 missionaries left China (including those who took leave in 1928 and those who returned early in 1929), causing the number of missionaries in China to plummet to 3,113. The primary reason for the evacuation was the political instability in China. In July 1926, Chiang Kai-shek was appointed as the commander-in-chief of the National Revolutionary Army and issued a mobilization order for the Northern Expedition, launching an offensive from Guangdong. Within six months, the KMT defeated two major warlords, Wu Peifu (吳佩孚) and Sun Chuanfang (孫傳芳), expanding the National Government’s control from Guangxi and Guangdong to the provinces of Hunan, Hubei, Jiangxi, and Fujian. In January 1927, the Northern Expedition forces forcibly took over the British concessions in Hankou and Jiujiang, prompting foreigners across various places along the Yangtze River to retreat to Shanghai. In March, Shanghai and Nanjing fell into the hands of the revolutionary army, and after entering Nanjing, on March 24, radicals stormed the British, American, and Japanese consulates, as well as foreign churches, schools, hospitals, and shops, resulting in the murders of some missionaries, an incident known as the “Nanjing Incident.”

As the Northern Expedition army moved into each province, missionaries evacuated to neighboring provinces to monitor the situation. The reclaiming of the British concession in Hankou prompted even more missionaries to leave. Simultaneously, anti-Christian mass movements erupted across various regions. For example, in December 1926 in Hunan, there was an “Anti-Christian Week,” with crowds marching and giving speeches, even attacking churches, and some missionaries were publicly paraded while tied up. Many churches, church schools, hospitals, and youth organizations were occupied. Under these circumstances, missionaries evacuated from Hunan Province, leaving only about 30 to 40 behind. Most church and school operations in Changsha, Xiangtan, Hengzhou, and Yuezhou came to a halt. Zhang Junjun (張君俊), a preacher from the Zondao Society, described the impact faced by the churches in Hunan as a “stormy tempest.” Furthermore, due to the Nanjing Incident, local churches faced even more severe impacts, with most churches occupied by the revolutionary army, and many churches and schools were ransacked. The Nanjing Christian Association reported that “when the revolutionary army entered the city, the persecution of the churches was like a violent storm, a rushing tide sweeping everything away, reminiscent of the Boxer Rebellion’s trials in the north, and perhaps not an exaggeration.”

C. L. Boynton pointed out several reasons for the missionaries’ departure: (1) being advised by local Chinese colleagues to evacuate; (2) urgent withdrawal orders from consuls; (3) concerns that remaining could lead to more international disputes; (4) evacuation orders from churches or missionary societies.

As the situation stabilized, missionaries gradually returned to their missions. Of the more than 3,000 who left China in 1927, by 1928, about 500 to 1,000 returned. By the eve of the War of Resistance Against Japan, the number rose again to 6,020.
