China: Agency (1920-1930)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Hsu Pao Chien (徐寶謙) (吳利明 1981: 171-222; 徐寶謙 1934: 44-50)

Hsu Pao Chien (徐寶謙, 1892-1944), also known by his courtesy name Liuji, was from Dazhu County, Zhejiang. He grew up in a traditional Confucian family and moved to Shanghai in 1905 for his studies. His uncle advised him before his departure that he was not to: (1) join foreign religions; (2) join revolutionary parties; or (3) cut his hair and braid. While in Shanghai, he was deeply influenced by revolutionary ideas and read extensively about revolutionary literature. The following year, he transferred to Shaoxing Provincial School, remaining committed to the revolutionary cause. However, during his studies, he also engaged with Confucian thought and became aware of the scholarly interpretations from the Song and Ming dynasties. After graduating in 1910, he enrolled at the Beijing Taxation School, cutting off his braid as a sign of his support for the revolution. Following the Wuchang Uprising in 1911, he witnessed the political landscape shift from hope to disappointment, feeling frustrated. He began to believe that the hope for China lay in the revival of Confucian spirit and even participated in activities of the Confucian Association. However, after his direct involvement, he grew disillusioned with the “politicization” of the Confucian Association.

In 1912, after participating in the Beijing Youth Association’s activities, he heard speakers discuss how Western strength was attributed to Christianity; this led him and his classmate Zhang Qinshi to privately study the Bible seriously starting in 1913, marking the beginning of his exploration of Christianity. In the spring of 1913, after being deeply moved by speeches from John R. Mott and Sherwood Eddy in Beijing, he decided to join a Bible study class. He later attended the summer session for students at Government Schools in North China, where he felt compelled to convert due to (1) the greatness of Jesus’ character; (2) the sincere behavior of the believers he observed; and (3) the absence of true conflict between science and religion. He emphasized that one could embrace Christianity without discarding Confucianism, as the two could “coexist and complement each other.” Subsequently, he began attending worship at the London Mission in Beijing, where he asked Reverend Cheng Jingyi about baptism. He was baptized by Reverend Cheng after Christmas in 1913.

In 1914, Hsu Pao Chien became the president of the Youth Association at the Taxation School, actively engaging in service while also holding the position of president for the Social Progress Association. After graduating in 1915, he forsook a lucrative job at the customs to work as a student officer for the Beijing Youth Association. In response to the new ideas and the May Fourth Movement, he co-founded “Life Monthly” in Beijing in November 1919 with Hu Xuecheng and later established the “Beijing Preaching Society” in early 1920. At that time, Hsu wrote in The Chinese Recorder, describing their work as a “Christian Renaissance.”

In January 1921, he traveled to the United States and enrolled at Union Theological Seminary and Columbia University, majoring in religious philosophy. Upon returning to China in 1924, he served as the executive secretary of the United Schools Association in Beijing while also teaching at Yenching University’s School of Religion. From 1926 onwards, he engaged in full-time teaching at Yenching University. In 1930, he received a year’s leave from Yenching University to serve at the World Student Christian Federation and the YMCA in Geneva. In 1931, he went to America again, continuing his studies and teaching at Union Theological Seminary and Columbia University until he returned to Yenching University in 1932. In 1933, he earned his doctorate with a dissertation titled “Ethical Realism in Neo-Confucian Thought.”

Additionally, in response to the Japanese invasion of China, he reflected on the issues of narrow nationalism, particularly during his studies in the U.S., where he interacted with Japanese students abroad, reconsidering that dismissing the entire concept of national identity was inappropriate. As he became involved in the work of the Pacifist and Love Society, he shifted toward promoting pacifism and internationalism. He argued that enlightened individuals from both China and Japan should strengthen cooperation and pushed for the “China-Japan Christian Conference” to convene in Beijing in 1934.

While Hsu found fulfillment in his student work and teaching at Yenching University, he increasingly felt disconnected from the struggles of the common people. At the same time, he identified the contradiction between private property and the spirit of Jesus, leading him to contemplate abandoning everything to share in the hardships of the people and realize the ideals of a Christian society. Yet, due to family responsibilities, he felt sorrowful that he could not challenge the existing social system. In 1935, he decided to accept the position of Executive Director of the rural experimental area in Lichuan, Jiangxi Province, resigning from his position at Yenching University to immerse himself in rural development. In 1936, he published “Experiences in Rural Work.” After the outbreak of the war in Shanghai, he moved to the rear area in Chengdu and participated in the editing and compilation of the “Compendium of Christian Classics.”(1) Xu Pao-chien (徐寶謙) (吳利明 1981: 171-222; 徐寶謙 1934: 44-50)

  1. Yu Dongchen (呂實強 1985a: 374-385)

Yu Dongchen (1851-?), from Dazhu County in Sichuan, experienced poverty in his youth and made a living as a coal porter. From 1886 to 1890, Dazhu County experienced conflicts between local faiths and Christianity, during which churches were destroyed. In 1888, Yu and his brothers participated in these conflicts, ultimately taking a leadership role in the 1890 altercations, leading over 100 workers from coal kilns and paper mills to kill 12 Christian congregants in Longshui Town and destroy over 200 homes belonging to Christians. He issued a proclamation stating that “the foreigner possesses the nature of a dog or a sheep but wields the power of a tiger or a wolf… the clergy infiltrate the government offices, distorting everything…” Soon after, French diplomatic pressure mounted on the Qing government, prompting General Qi Yuan and Governor Liu Bingzhang of Sichuan to dispatch troops to suppress the unrest. Yu retreated to the Western Hills, which became known as the “Dazhu Church Incident.”

In 1892, Yu descended again to attack Christians, prompting another military response from the government, which captured 37 of his followers. Yu fled once more. In 1898, after being arrested, he was taken to the Rongchang County prison. Soon after, members of Yu’s faction rebelled and stormed Rongchang County, coercing the magistrate to release him. After his release, Yu returned to Longshui Town and issued another declaration, stating, “The destruction of churches in the sixteenth year of Guangxu (1890) was not merely my personal grudge but rather the public outcry of the people.” This incident prompted calls for swift action from foreign diplomats in Chongqing and church authorities. Given the intense anti-Christian sentiment in the region, officials feared that hasty arrests could incite greater resistance and thus resorted to hiring assassins to eliminate Yu secretly. Upon learning of this plot, Yu rallied his followers to the He Bao area in Rongchang County in May and led an attack that burned a Catholic church, killed two congregants, and abducted the local priest, M. Pere Fleury, along with another Chinese priest. Local officials intervened, but the latter managed to escape.

At this point, disparities emerged between General Gong Shou, who advocated for dialogue and negotiation, and Ren Xifen, who favored a dual approach of suppression and negotiation. Yu insisted on total amnesty for all anti-Christian cases and demanded that foreigners be prohibited from using such cases as leverage against local governance. However, the French minister and the bishop remained steadfast. Yu, feeling that the government was insincere, decided to take preemptive action, issuing a proclamation chronicling the plight of the people as well as the foreigners’ arrogance, framing his uprising as a struggle for the nation and the people to eliminate harm. In August, Yu led over 2,000 followers intending to invade the city to burn churches and capture priests. Various congregants fled, and foreign nationals in Chongqing evacuated to boats in anticipation of a possible confrontation. In response to the escalating situation, the government resolved to conduct a thorough crackdown, ultimately resulting in Yu Dongchen being surrounded and captured by government forces, with all his followers surrendering.

Yu’s anti-Christian incidents spanned ten months, engaging over ten counties and resulting in the destruction of more than 20 churches, with casualties among missionaries and congregants. Following the incident, the Hankou consul requested compensation of 25,000 taels for missionaries and 400,000 taels for church repairs, ultimately settling for around 50,000 taels.

  1. Zhou Han (呂實強 1985b: 553)

Zhou Han (1843-1911), courtesy name Tiezhen, was from Ningxiang, Hunan. He enlisted in the military in 1860 after passing the provincial exam. Starting in 1882, he assisted the Imperial Commissioner Liu Jintang in military affairs in Xinjiang. In 1884, he returned to Hunan on leave and settled in Changsha. During this period, Liu Jintang, feeling that “the loss of public virtue has led to disasters,” planned to fund the printing of benevolent books in Hunan for reformation, and Zhou assisted in this initiative. However, due to difficulties in fundraising, it ceased around 1888.

While in Changsha, Zhou witnessed the increasing presence of foreign missionaries and began writing anti-missionary texts, which he published and disseminated. Between 1899 and 1901, he authored and published over a hundred anti-missionary works, mostly concentrated between 1890 and 1892. His writings circulated widely in Hunan, Hubei, and the Yangtze River basin, significantly raising anti-missionary awareness and inciting actions against missionary presence. Zhou’s anti-missionary literature was published under his own name as well as attributed to various historical figures including scholars and notable officials. The content of Zhou’s works predominantly critiqued Christianity from a Confucian traditionalist perspective, addressing the aggressive nature of missionary efforts and the cultural differences between Chinese and Western customs.(1) Conflicts between Wang Zai and Ni Tuosheng

The differences between Wang Zai and Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲) revolved around two main issues: (1) Wang advocated for an itinerant evangelism approach, serving as a free preacher in various locations, while Ni insisted on establishing “local churches”; (2) regarding the ordination of pastors, Wang began to accept the necessity of ordaining pastors for the sake of evangelism, whereas Ni opposed this view. Ultimately, Ni Tuosheng left Fuzhou.

In 1927, Wang was invited to speak at the Guangzhou Spiritual Conference. In 1928, the conferences in Guangdong and Hong Kong began to co-host the Spiritual Conference, with Wang invited to lead on three separate occasions (1928, 1931, 1932). Through the Spiritual Conferences, he met R. A. Jaffray, a missionary from the Alliance Church, who invited him to participate in outreach efforts in Southeast Asia. In the winter of 1928, Wang traveled from Indochina to Singapore and Malaysia, then moving into the Indonesian archipelago, with East Borneo as the last stop. In 1929, Jaffray established the “Southeast Asia Evangelical Mission,” with Wang serving as its president. In October 1929, Wang led revivals in Shanghai, Hangzhou, Qingdao, Jinan, and Beijing, calling for support among believers for the work in Southeast Asia. The Southeast Asia Evangelical Mission was later renamed the “Chinese Overseas Mission.”

During the Anti-Japanese War, Wang lived successively in Singapore and Indonesia. He immersed himself in evangelistic work in Southeast Asia, leading revivals and preaching meetings across various regions.

Wang Zai passed away in California, USA, on December 13, 1975, at the age of 77.

  1. Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲) (李佳福 2001; 吳秀良 2004; 梁家麟 2003; 邢福增 2005)

Ni Tuosheng (Watchman Nee, 1903-1972), originally named Shu Zu, was born into a Christian family in Fuzhou, Fujian. His grandfather, Ni Yucheng, was the first Chinese pastor sent by the American Board to Fujian, while his parents, Ni Wenxiu and Lin Heping, were dedicated Christians, with his mother having a background in the Methodist Church. In 1916, Ni entered Trinity College, administered by the Anglican Church in Fuzhou. Though he grew up in a Christian environment and was baptized as a child in the Methodist Church, he had not genuinely accepted the faith.

In February 1920, during a revival meeting held by E. H. M. Yü in the Fuzhou Congregational Church, not only did Ni’s mother, Lin Heping, experience spiritual rebirth, but Ni also recommitted to his faith and devoted himself to the ministry. Ni received training from the missionary Margaret E. Barber and began to read works by Jessie Penn-Lewis. In March 1921, Ni and his mother were re-baptized.

In 1922, Ni and Wang Zai began to hold breaking of bread meetings in Fuzhou, contributing to a revival in the area. In 1924, the two split over church direction and ordination issues, and Ni departed Fuzhou. He then worked for about eight or nine months at the Ling Guang Newspaper in Nanjing before embarking on evangelism efforts in Southeast Asia and Japan. After returning to China in May 1925, he lived in Gulangyu, Xiamen, and established the “Gospel Publishing House,” producing “Christian News.” In 1926, Ni preached in Xiamen but fell ill and went to Nanjing for recovery, during which he completed “The Spiritual Man.”

In 1927, Ni relocated to Shanghai, renaming the “Gospel Publishing House” to “Gospel Book Room,” where he published works such as “Revival,” “The Christian,” and “Sermon Records.” By January 1928, he rented a space on Hading Road, Wenshu, to begin gatherings. In 1928, “The Spiritual Man” was officially published by Gospel Book Room. At this time, meetings also began to emerge across various regions. Simultaneously, Ni connected with the British Brethren, and in 1931, they sent representatives to visit the meeting place in Shanghai. In 1933, Ni was invited to visit the British Brethren.

In October 1934, Ni married Zhang Pinhui, but faced public opposition from Zhang’s aunt, Zhang Meizhen. The incident affected Ni deeply, leading him to retreat for a year. In the autumn of 1935, he traveled to Yantai in Shandong, where he experienced a “baptism of the Holy Spirit” during a revival meeting, holding a special gathering in Yantai before returning to Shanghai and Quanzhou for more meetings, resulting in revitalization.

In 1938, he published “Reconsiderations of Work,” further establishing his view on “local churches.” In July, he set out to visit Britain, and in October, he visited Denmark. His speeches in Denmark were later compiled into “The Normal Christian Life.” In July 1939, he returned to Shanghai to lead further meetings.

By the end of 1939, he took over his brother Ni Huaizu’s “China Biochemical Pharmaceutical Factory.” This involvement sparked dissatisfaction among his coworkers, who believed he had abandoned the church to engage in business. Even his coworker Li Yuanru urged the Shanghai church elders to halt Ni’s ministry. Faced with this internal pressure, Ni ultimately accepted a decision for his removal in May 1943, marking the second time in his ministry that he was forced to withdraw.

Given that the three German missions had arrived in China earlier than the other seven and conducted their work primarily in the Cantonese dialect, the conference decided to begin collaborative efforts in Central China.

In March 1913, the Lutheran Theological Seminary, supported by the American Mennonite Lutheran Mission, the American Hauge Synod Mission, the Finnish Missionary Society, and the Norwegian Missionary Society, was founded in Laokou, Hubei.   1. Bible Union of China, 1920 (Yao 2003: 55-100)

The Bible Union of China was established in August 1920 by a group of fundamentalist missionaries in China. Following a speech by the prominent North American fundamentalist William H. G. Thomas at the missionary retreat in Guling, around 20 missionaries convened a meeting and formed a national alliance—the Bible Union of China—accompanied by a draft declaration and statement. The statement emphasized that missionaries must “earnestly contend” for the faith as clearly revealed in both the Old and New Testaments, adhering to the fundamental doctrines outlined in the Apostles’ Creed. It reasserted the need for action to protect the church and theological institutions in light of the discrepancies arising from Western theological debates, especially as some began to question the truth and accept a partial social gospel.

The movement aimed to awaken the church, renew understanding of the Gospel’s power, and promote the dissemination and study of the Bible, along with providing materials and articles that uphold biblical fundamental truths. It encouraged national missionary societies and churches to accept only those who rightly handle the word of God as missionaries, remaining vigilant against attempts to influence students and church leaders with erroneous beliefs and activities. However, the statement underwent several revisions, adopting relatively milder language to gain broader support, with key figures in the revisions being J. W. Lowrie, president of the American Presbyterian Church, and D. E. Hoste, the head of the China Inland Mission. Lowrie later became the general secretary of the Union.

The Bible Union was headquartered in Shanghai and published “The Bulletin of the Bible Union of China” in 1921, which was renamed “The Bible for China” in 1924. The first national conference was held in Shanghai in May 1922, where a committee of 100, comprising the leadership structure and executive committee, was elected, with a significant proportion of members being from the Presbyterian and China Inland mission societies. Membership increased from over 400 in 1920 to more than 2,000 by the end of 1922, accounting for about 30% of all missionaries in China during the 1920s.(1) The Bible Union of China and the Emergence of Anti-Modernist Groups

Following the foundation of the Bible Union of China, several other countries established similar anti-modernist organizations, including the Bible League for India, Burma and Ceylon, the Bible Union of Victoria in Australia, the Japan Bible Union, and the Madras Bible League.

In response, liberal missionaries in China countered the efforts of the Bible Union, accusing it of instigating theological disputes, conducting heresy trials, and undermining church unity. Some conservative missionaries also disagreed with the aggressive stance of the union. Even within the union itself, there were radical and moderate factions.

Since the Bible Union was a loosely organized alliance without a robust national structure, its headquarters was staffed by volunteers or part-time personnel, leading to a gradual decline in influence beyond their publications, like “The Bulletin.” By the late 1920s, there were even calls among members for dissolution. However, with the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, the Bible Union ultimately could not sustain its operations.

Chinese Christians accused the missionary of undermining their dignity and beliefs, and referred the matter to their church overseer with a request for his return to the United States. However, the overseer dismissed these concerns, stating that the Chinese believers were overly sensitive. As a result, over 40 members of the China Inland Mission publicly called for separation from the mission.

The May 30 Incident undoubtedly stoked the flames of anti-imperialist sentiment nationwide, pushing the anti-Christian movement to new heights.

  1. The National Christian Conference of China, 1925 (王治心 1925: 1-2)

In 1925, the National Christian Conference of China was established, with Wang Zhixin serving as the initiator. The motivation for its establishment highlighted that “the foreign powers are oppressing our nation through unequal treaties, causing immense suffering” and that this Christian spirit of struggle calls for a resistance against these malevolent forces, promoting the movement to abolish the unequal treaties.

Wang emphasized that the Association would operate without any partisan bias, allowing any Christian from the Republic of China to participate. Even if Christian participants belonged to specific political parties, they could join based on their identity as Christians, provided they did not use the organization for party activities. This was a totally spontaneous organization among Chinese Christians, although Western friends could act as supporters but could not become formal members.

The rationale for forming the Association included four points: (1) Christianity advocates for equality, opposing unequal treaties; (2) many unequal treaties are connected to church incidents but are not directly related to Christianity itself. However, Christianity bears significant responsibility for the calamity that arose upon its introduction to China; (3) many nationalists criticized Christianity as an extension of imperialism, and thus genuine Christians should oppose unequal treaties, exemplifying the peace spirit of Christ and equality; (4) the Eastern Christian Educational Union had previously passed a resolution calling for the abolition of unequal treaties, necessitating the formation of an organization to actualize this resolution.

Thus, the formation of the Association aimed to garner national attention among Christians to support the movement while communicating with international Christian organizations to advocate for the abolition of these treaties. Additionally, it planned to study the contents of the unequal treaties and explore methods used by other countries to abolish such treaties.

  1. Conference with John R. Mott, 1926 (董建吾 1927: 146-159; NCC 1926: 5-10)

In January 1926, John R. Mott, as the president of the International Missionary Council, arrived in Shanghai. The purpose of his visit was to discuss various issues arising from the changing circumstances affecting Christianity with church leaders and to strategize for future advancements, as part of preparations for the upcoming World Missionary Conference.(1) The “Mott Conference,” 1922

In collaboration with the National Christian Council of China, an informal meeting known as the “Mott Conference” was held from January 5 to 7, 1922. John R. Mott and nearly thirty representatives discussed seven primary themes: (1) spiritual preparedness of the Chinese church to meet the present situation; (2) making Christianity indigenous in China; (3) Christian literature; (4) principles guiding missionary policy in China moving forward; (5) relationships between the Christian church and China’s treaties with foreign nations; (6) Christian education; and (7) recommendations for international meetings.

During the conference, Mott particularly urged the representatives to “imprint a deep understanding of the current conditions, trends, and most pressing issues facing China in their minds. Please shed any preconceived notions and share your struggles and greatest needs openly.” The meeting was presided over by Yu Rizhang, with other significant participants such as Cheng Jingyi, Wei Zhuomin, Luo Yunyan, and Zhao Zichen providing sincere input on related topics.

  1. The Conference on Christianizing Economic Relations, 1927 (中華全國基督教協進會 1927; Reilly 2020: 36-43; 陳珠如 2016)

From August 18 to 28, 1927, the National Christian Council of China convened the “Conference on Christianizing Economic Relations.” The conference centered around three major themes: (1) issues relating to industrial conditions, such as wages, working hours, employer relations, apprenticeship systems, labor movements, and the living conditions of workers; (2) rural economic issues, examining tenant farming, market cooperation, and agricultural associations from an ethical perspective; and (3) the relationship between Christianity and the economic questions of private property, inheritance, wages, economic competition, and personal income, applying contemporary social thought and Christian ethics. The conference’s foundational belief was: “To generate the desired new society, the Spirit of God must operate within individuals and groups.” They acknowledged that “the evils of great wealth should be curtailed, focusing on monitoring income and expenditures and promoting the ideal of ‘from each according to their ability, to each according to their needs.’”

The conference notably invited Japanese Christian socialist thinker Toyohiko Kagawa, who gave a devotional talk on “Reflections of Christ on Social Movements” and delivered speeches on “The Church and Economic Issues” and “Japanese Social Movements.” Representatives reported on the conditions of workers and labor movements in Guangzhou, Wuhan, Shanghai, Changzhou, and Wuxi, as well as the peasant movements in Guangdong.

The essence of the conference was focused on how Christianity could respond to China’s economic challenges. “Christians must study the current economic system, critique various theories, and instill the spirit of Christ into economic life, supporting what aligns and reforming what does not.”

  1. The Guangzhou Spiritual Conference, 1927 (李金強、黃彩蓮 2019: 64-71)

The Guangzhou Spiritual Conference was held in July 1927 for a duration of 14 days. It was initiated by Pastor Huang Yuansu from the Christian and Missionary Alliance, stemming from an experience where he and three others, including Pastor Zhao Liutang, were robbed by bandits while returning to Wuzhou, resulting in their captivity for a total of 156 days (from August 18, 1925, to January 21, 1926). During their time in captivity, the four engaged in Bible reading, finding spiritual comfort. After their release, Huang reflected on the experience, likening it to Jonah in the belly of a fish, which led them to a prolonged self-examination and spiritual growth; ultimately, the four experienced significant spiritual advancement. This led to the concept of initiating “spiritual” gatherings.Under the promotion of Pastor Huang Yuansu, a committee was established, including members such as Zhao Liutang, Wu Zikun, Luo Xiaochuan, Zheng Deyin, and Lin Boluo, as well as missionaries like Arthur G. Lindquist from the Alliance Church and R. A. Jaffray from the Christian and Missionary Alliance.

Speakers at the Guangzhou Spiritual Conference included Wang Zai, Huang Yuansu, Philip Hinkey, Davis, Zhao Liutang, Li Qirong, Luo Shengai, and R. A. Jaffray. The first day’s meeting was held at the Zengsha Methodist Church; due to a large attendance, the venue was changed on the second day to the Savior Church of the Anglican Church on Wanfulu.

The enthusiastic response to the Guangzhou Spiritual Conference led to a similar initiative in Hong Kong the following year (1928), where a sister conference was formed. In July, the conference was first held in Guangzhou, followed by an event in August in Hong Kong, officially naming it the first “Guangdong-Hong Kong Spiritual Conference.” Starting in 1929, it was referred to as the first “Guangdong-Hong Kong Spiritual Conference,” and it has been held annually since, becoming a significant event in the Christian community in South China. After 1949, it was renamed the “Hong Kong-Kowloon Spiritual Conference.”

  1. 基督教救國（邢福增 1997a：67-71；1997b：400-403 ）

民國時期基督徒知識菁英勇於面對種種挑戰，致力於文字創作，藉著出版雜誌，如《生命》、《真理》、《真理與生命》、《青年進步》、《紫晶》、《文社》、《田家》、《天風》等，在本色神學及禮儀建構、反省基督教信仰與中國社會重建的關係上，作了大量的探討及實踐，為華人神學留下了豐富而寶貴的遺產。救國或國家重建成為20世紀國人的終極關懷。現實危機衍生了文化危機，逼使關心國事的知識分子從思想文化層面來檢討及探索中國文化的未來。對中國基督徒而言，基督教在中國社會重建中究竟扮演甚麼角色，成為他們致力反省的課題。救國神學成為20世紀本色化或處境化（Contextualization）的重要焦點所在。

檢視20世紀中國歷史，許多知識分子（包括基督徒）均傾向以「一元」的思想模式來思考「救國」與文化重建的問題。他們急於為中國面對的困局尋找出路，並將複雜的國家、社會、文化問題約化為終極的「根本」問題（如民心腐敗、帝國主義）。同時，他們又希望能夠迅速地找到一個根本解決問題的方案與出路，從而促成中國社會的重建。於是，「德先生」（民主）﹑「賽先生」（科學）、「美小姐」（美學）、「孔夫子」（新儒家）、穆姑娘（道德倫理）、孫先生（三民主義）﹑「馬先生」（馬克思主義）紛紛登上歷史舞台，企圖向國人證明其救國功能。正當百家爭鳴、群雄並起之際，「基先生」也代表中國教會參與競賽，期望基督教能夠成為根治中國問題的良方妙藥，從而達致「中華歸主」的宏願。

儘管中國教會認定基督教是拯救中國問題的唯一方案與「獨家出賣」（王治心語），但不同背景的基督徒之間，對基督教如何救國，卻有著殊異的理解。換言之，「基先生」也以不同的形象，呈現在國人面前：（1）耶穌是道德人格的重建者。這種主張認為，中國文化的根本問題端在人格、民心的更新，而耶穌正是理想完美人格的典範。耶穌的愛、犧牲、捨己精神，恰好是醫治中國道德及精神問題的良藥，也是社會重建的文化基礎。（2）耶穌是社會改造者。他們相信，中國的問題，端在於社會的種種缺漏。因此，基督徒應致力參與不同層面的社會改革（如教育﹑農村建設等）。耶穌在世的最大使命，正是鼓吹改革社會的意識，實現天國降臨的基礎。（3）耶穌是社會革命家。這派基督徒強調，要根本解決中國的問題，不能單靠個別層面的改良，唯有以革命手段，建立新的制度，方能完成國家重建的使命。耶穌的榜樣，便是典型的革命家。至於怎樣的革命才符合基督教的理想，又視乎不同的革命立場而異──耶穌既可以支持三民主義的國民革命，也能擁護社會主義革命。

總的來說，在中國處於危急存亡之際，中國文化又在劇變之時，20年代中國基督徒為了證明基督教乃救國的唯一法門，乃致力將其詮釋為具有強烈群體救贖意義的信仰。這樣，耶穌不僅變成「世俗彌賽亞」，失掉其神性的本質，更成為不同的世俗救國方案或意識形態的附庸。耶穌的不同形象，說明中國基督徒首先確認中國問題的根源在那裡，再尋索根治有關問題的出路，最後才用基督教包裝此等方案。歸根究柢，基督教只扮演合理化或神聖化這些世俗方案的角色。耶穌基督在20世紀中國的不同形象，正好反映出中國教會在結合基督教與國家重建時所面對的困局。

  1. 協進會與本色教會（邢福增1997：64-70）

1922年5月，全國基督教大會在上海舉行，會議的主題是「中國教會」。大會會長誠靜怡的開會演詞，強調中國教會的最大問題，在於依靠別人的思想﹑制度和方法，「教會對許多事情從來就是不發生疑問的，也沒有獨立的思想和自主的判斷」。誠氏指出，中國教會一方面要吸取西方教會的優點，另方面亦要把「本國民族的精神，自由的，合宜的表現出來」，不能「盲從別人家所說所為，完全接受外國的風俗，遺傳，形式，制度和方法總不能算是頂好的，就是接受了他們宗教上真理的解釋而不去探其根源，又依著了他們思想而為自己的隨便應用，也不能算是頂好的。」因此，要祛除基督教的洋教形象，基督教的本色化乃是逼切而不能逃避的課題。

大會同時發表了一篇〈教會的宣言〉，宣言的內容由一個中國人組成的委員會（也是大會唯一一個沒有西方傳教士參與的委員會），經過廣泛及長期的討論而敲定。在「敬告同道書」的部分，宣言指出：「中國的歷史，國人的特性，教務的性質，經驗的指示，以及國內種種迅速的變遷，莫不要求一個中國本色的基督教會，俾特宣傳中國本色的基督教。」所謂「本色」，就是指基督教會「一方面仍與世界各宗基督教會，在歷史上有連續不絕的關係，一方面是要實在適合中華民族本有的文化，和精神上的經驗。」職是之故，宣言突顯了中國信徒在反省何謂本色教會的重要性：「我們宣告，時期已到，吾中華信徒，應用謹慎的研究，放膽的試驗，自己刪定教會的禮節和儀式，教會的組織和系統，以及教會佈道及推廣的方法，務求一切都能輔導現在的教會，成為中國本色的教會。」宣言緊接籲請西方傳教士輔助中國信徒「當此大任」，並「使中華信徒在他們試驗中，得有不受限制的自由」。

那麼，全國協進會又如何落實關於建構中國本色教會的反省？畢竟，空談本色化而沒有任何具體的工作，仍是不切實際的。為此，在1924年5月召開的第二屆協進會年會中，即通過成立「本色教會委員會」的常備組織，成員包括劉延芳（委員長）﹑趙紫宸﹑施伯珩（C. G. Sparham）﹑誠靜怡﹑明思德（M.C. Mullen）及李耀邦，就五方面的問題進行詳細的討論：（1）教會與差會之關係；（2）自立問題；（3）教會之組織與管理；（4）教會統一；（5）本色之意見發揮。未幾，劉廷芳即擬定於北京﹑濟南﹑上海﹑漢口﹑福建及廣州等地，組織分區委員會，並就若干事項進行分區調查，祭祖及婚喪禮儀的問題，也包括在議題之內。

1925年5月，協進會召開第三屆全國會議，本色教會委員會在提交報告時，交代了該會過去一年的工作，報告特別指出由於眾幹事因著協進會事務所的改組﹑高等教育議事會﹑中華國內佈道會等工作關係，不得已將本色教會委員會的工作暫時擱置，甚至全體委員的會議也無法召開，會務幾陷於停頓。不過，委員會仍強調有關工作必須繼續，並提出來年工作的方向，其中即包括研究中國文化中具有永久價值的各種要素，如保存尊重父母的美德，及研究中國教會的喪葬及結婚禮節等。最後，年會通過責承本色教會委員會須特別「了解我國的禮節，庶幾我國的基督徒知道紀念及尊重並祖先」，並通過各地分區委員的名單，希望能在各地深化討論，以收集思廣益之效。

豈料，本色教會委員會在接續一年裡，工作仍處於停滯不前的地步，以致當協進會在1926年10月召開第四屆年會時，該委員會竟無法提交任何報告，只能以〈研究本色教會問題報告〉權充取代。而年會也沒有通過任何關於該委員會及任命委員會的成員的議案。換言之，本色教會委員會基本上是名存實亡的，是故在第五屆年會開始，也再見不到這個委員會的縱影。

  1. The Debate Between Old and New Theology Among Missionaries in China (Bates 1974: 135-158; 邢福增 2001: 21-23)

Entering the 20th century, with the rise of Social Gospel and Modernism or Liberal Theology, more and more missionaries began to significantly revise the essence of the Gospel in both North America and overseas mission fields. In response to the subversion of traditional doctrines by liberal theology, fundamentalists began to unite their efforts, becoming the “antithesis” to liberal theology on the stage of 20th-century church history.

Fundamentalism refers to a religious or theological movement that originated in the United States, broadly encompassing conservative church figures who rose to defend the “fundamental” doctrines due to dissatisfaction with liberal theology. They could not agree with the approach taken by liberal theology, which elevated reason as the sole standard for judging whether traditional doctrines should be abandoned in response to the challenges posed by the Enlightenment. They deeply felt the threat that the rise of liberal theology posed to evangelicalism, especially in its denial of biblical authority, and thus united in opposition to theological modernists, often adopting a militant stance against liberal theology. Fundamentalists primarily defend five core doctrines: (1) The inspiration and Infallibility of Scripture; (2) The Deity of Christ, including His Virgin Birth; (3) The Substitutionary Atonement of Christ’s Death; (4) The Literal Resurrection of Christ from the Dead; (5) The Literal Return of Christ in the Second Advent.

The theological debate between fundamentalism and liberalism became an important event in the history of missionary work in the 20th century, and the situation in China was no exception. In 1920, the famous North American fundamentalist W. H. Griffith Thomas visited China. After returning to his home country, he expressed deep concern about the modernist tendencies among missionaries in China and published an article titled “Modernism in China,” which sparked broader debates. For fundamentalist missionaries, almost all substantial national Christian organizations in China at that time had been dominated by liberalism. For example, the China National Christian Council and the China Christian YMCA had evidently become strongholds of modernism. In 1926, the China Inland Mission and the Evangelical Missionary Alliance withdrew from the China National Christian Council due to theological differences, intensifying the opposition between old and new theology.

姚西伊 pointed out that the focus of this theological debate in China primarily revolved around three major issues: the authority of the Bible, evangelism and social participation, and how to view Chinese religions. In contrast, the controversy over evolution and creation that triggered intense debates in North America did not become a major “hotspot” in China (姚西伊 1999: 194-204). 连曦 further emphasized that it was “religious syncretism” rather than higher criticism that became one of the significant characteristics of liberal theology in China. The debate on new and old theology in China was not merely an imported “foreign product” from North America; the theological debates of the 1920s and 1930s in China also had a reflex influence on North America (Lian 1997: 171-206).(2) Christian Salvation of the Nation (邢福增 1997a: 67-71; 1997b: 400-403)

During the Republican period, Christian intellectual elites bravely faced various challenges and dedicated themselves to literary creations. Through the publication of magazines such as “Life,” “Truth,” “Truth and Life,” “Youth Progress,” “Amethyst,” “Literary Society,” “Tian Jia,” and “Heavenly Wind,” they engaged in extensive discussions and practices regarding contextual theology and liturgical construction, reflecting on the relationship between Christianity and the reconstruction of Chinese society, leaving a rich and valuable legacy for Chinese theology. The salvation of the nation or national reconstruction became the ultimate concern of the Chinese people in the 20th century. The realities of crisis led to a cultural crisis, forcing concerned intellectuals to examine and explore the future of Chinese culture from an ideological and cultural perspective. For Chinese Christians, the question of what role Christianity plays in the reconstruction of Chinese society became a topic they were deeply engaged in. Salvation theology became an important focal point of contextualization in the 20th century.

Examining the history of China in the 20th century, many intellectuals (including Christians) tended to think of the issues of “salvation of the nation” and cultural reconstruction in a “monistic” ideological framework. They were eager to find solutions to the predicaments faced by China, reducing complex national, social, and cultural issues to fundamental “core” problems (such as corruption of public sentiment and imperialism). At the same time, they hoped to quickly identify a fundamental solution to these problems, thereby facilitating the reconstruction of Chinese society. Thus, “Mr. Democracy” (Democracy), “Mr. Science” (Science), “Miss Aesthetics,” “Confucius” (New Confucianism), “Miss Moral Ethics,” “Mr. Sun” (Three Principles of the People), and “Mr. Marx” (Marxism) all took the historical stage, attempting to prove their salvific function to the nation. At a time of vibrant discourse and rising talents, “Mr. Christianity” also represented the Chinese church in the competition, hoping that Christianity could become a good remedy for addressing China’s problems, thereby achieving the grand vision of “China returning to the Lord.”

Although the Chinese church regarded Christianity as the only solution to China’s issues and a “unique offering” (as said by 王治心), there were differing understandings among Christians from various backgrounds regarding how Christianity could save the nation. In other words, “Mr. Christianity” appeared before the people in different forms: (1) Jesus as the Rebuilder of Moral Character. This view holds that the fundamental problem of Chinese culture lies in the renewal of character and public sentiment, with Jesus being the model of ideal and perfect character. Jesus’ love, sacrifice, and spirit of self-denial are precisely the cure for China’s moral and spiritual problems and serve as the cultural foundation for social reconstruction. (2) Jesus as the Social Reformer. Proponents believe that China’s issues stem from various shortcomings in society. Therefore, Christians should engage in social reforms at different levels (such as education and rural construction). Jesus’ greatest mission during His time on earth was to promote awareness of social reform, laying the foundation for the coming of the Kingdom of Heaven. (3) Jesus as the Social Revolutionary. This group of Christians emphasizes that to fundamentally solve China’s problems, one cannot rely solely on improvements at the individual level; instead, it is necessary to establish new systems through revolutionary means to fulfill the mission of national reconstruction. Jesus himself serves as a typical revolutionary model. The nature of the revolution that aligns with Christian ideals varies according to differing revolutionary positions—Jesus can support both the national revolution of the Three Principles of the People and the socialist revolution.

In summary, at a time when China was in a critical survival situation and undergoing drastic changes in culture, Chinese Christians in the 1920s worked to demonstrate that Christianity was the only way to save the nation by interpreting it as a faith with a strong communal redemptive significance. In this way, Jesus not only became the “secular Messiah,” losing His divine essence, but also became an accessory to various secular salvation plans or ideologies. The different images of Jesus illustrate that Chinese Christians first confirmed the roots of China’s problems and then sought solutions before packaging those solutions with Christianity. Ultimately, Christianity only played the role of legitimizing or sanctifying these secular plans. The diverse representations of Jesus Christ in 20th-century China reflect the dilemmas faced by the Chinese church as it sought to integrate Christianity with national reconstruction.

In May 1922, the National Christian Conference was held in Shanghai, with the theme “The Church in China.” The conference president, Cheng Jingyi, emphasized in his opening address that the primary problem of the Chinese church lay in relying on the thoughts, systems, and methods of others, stating, “The church has never questioned many things, nor does it possess independent thought and judgment.”诚氏指出，中国教会一方面要吸取西方教会的优点，另一方面也要把“本国民族的精神，自由的，合宜的表现出来”，不能“盲从别人家所说所为，完全接受外国的风俗，遗传，形式，制度和方法总不能算是顶好的，就是接受了他们宗教上真理的解释而不去探其根源，又依着了他们思想而为自己的随便应用，也不能算是顶好的。”因此，要祛除基督教的洋教形象，基督教的本色化乃是迫切而不能逃避的课题。

大会同时发表了一篇《教会的宣言》，宣言的内容由一个中国人组成的委员会（也是大会唯一一个没有西方传教士参与的委员会），经过广泛及长期的讨论而敲定。在“敬告同道书”的部分，宣言指出：“中国的历史，国人的特性，教务的性质，经验的指示，以及国内种种迅速的变迁，莫不要求一个中国本色的基督教会，以特宣传中国本色的基督教。”所谓“本色”，就是指基督教会“一方面仍与世界各宗基督教会，在历史上有连续不断的关系，一方面是要实在适合中华民族本有的文化，和精神上的经验。”因此，宣言突显了中国信徒在反省何谓本色教会的重要性：“我们宣告，时期已到，吾中华信徒，应用谨慎的研究，放胆的试验，自行裁定教会的礼节和仪式，教会的组织和系统，以及教会布道及推广的方法，务求一切都能辅导现在的教会，成为中国本色的教会。”宣言紧接呼请西方传教士辅佐中国信徒“当此大任”，并“使中华信徒在他们试验中，得有不受限制的自由”。

那么，全国协进会又如何落实关于构建中国本色教会的反省？毕竟，空谈本色化而没有任何具体的工作，仍是不切实际的。为此，在1924年5月召开的第二届协进会年会上，便通过成立“本色教会委员会”的常备组织，成员包括刘延芳（委员会主任）、赵紫宸、施伯珩（C. G. Sparham）、诚静怡、明思德（M.C. Mullen）及李耀邦，就五方面的问题进行详细的讨论：（1）教会与差会之关系；（2）自立问题；（3）教会之组织与管理；（4）教会统一；（5）本色之意见发挥。未几，刘廷芳即拟定于北京、济南、上海、汉口、福建及广州等地，组织分区委员会，并就若干事项进行分区调查，祭祖及婚丧礼仪的问题，也包括在议题之内。

1925年5月，协进会召开第三届全国会议，本色教会委员会在提交报告时，交代了该会过去一年的工作。报告特别指出由于众干事因协进会事务所的改组、高等教育议事会、中华国内布道会等工作关系，不得已将本色教会委员会的工作暂时搁置，甚至全体委员的会议也无法召开，会议几陷于停顿。不过，委员会仍强调有关工作必须继续，并提出来年工作的方向，其中即包括研究中国文化中具有永久价值的各种要素，如保存尊重父母的美德及研究中国教会的丧葬及结婚礼节等。最后，年会通过责承本色教会委员会须特别“了解我国的礼节，庶几我国的基督徒知道纪念及尊重并祖先”，并通过各地分区委员的名单，希望能在各地深化讨论，以收集思广益之效。

岂料，本色教会委员会在接续一年里，工作仍处于滞后状态，以致当协进会在1926年10月召开第四届年会时，该委员会竟无法提交任何报告，只能以《研究本色教会问题报告》权充取代。而年会也没有通过任何关于该委员会及任命委员会成员的议案。换言之，本色教会委员会基本上是名存实亡的，因此在第五届年会开始，也再见不到这个委员会的踪影。

为什么委员会的工作在刚起步不久，竟会陷于停滞的状态？据《研究本色教会问题报告》的解释，主要是由于时局变化的冲击所致。1925年5月，发生了举国震惊的“五卅惨案”，引发了全国各地的反帝示威，将本来已趋于高涨的反帝国主义浪潮，推至另一高峰。In the context of unstable circumstances, “the regional committees have yet to be established, the situation has come to a standstill, contributions are nonexistent, and any talk of progress is truly regretful.”

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Cai Yuanpei (Zhang Qinshi 1928: 1-9, 199-201)

Cai Yuanpei (蔡元培, 1868-1940), a native of Shaoxing, Zhejiang, was distinguished as a scholar in the imperial examinations in 1892 and was appointed as a junior member of the Hanlin Academy. He became interested in current affairs and Western learning in the afternoon of his life, later sympathizing with the revolution. After the founding of the Republic of China, he served as the Minister of Education. When Yuan Shikai became president, he continued to serve as the Director General of Education for the Beijing government. However, he resigned a few months later due to dissatisfaction with Yuan’s autocratic rule. In 1916, he was appointed president of Peking University, promoting the spirit of intellectual freedom and becoming an important figure in the New Culture Movement, supporting students during the May Fourth Movement. In October 1927, he became the president of the Academia Sinica (the Ministry of Education), and the following year, he became the president of the Central Research Institute. After the September 18 Incident, he supported the resistance against Japan and participated in the Shanghai Cultural Salvation Association. At the end of 1937, he moved from Shanghai to Hong Kong, where he passed away in 1940.

In 1917, Cai published an article titled “Using Aesthetic Education Instead of Religion” in “Xin Qingnian” (New Youth), discussing the role of religion from the perspectives of intellect, will, and emotion. He pointed out that due to advancements in science, knowledge and will had become “independent,” and the remaining emotional role of religion could be replaced by “aesthetic education.” This article ignited discussions in the intellectual community regarding religious issues during the New Culture Movement.

In April 1922, the Beijing Non-Religious Alliance held a non-religious conference, where Cai Yuanpei delivered a speech stating, “All religions today are bound by outdated doctrines, using absurd rituals and exaggerated propaganda to elicit blind faith from the uneducated to sustain the lives of the missionaries.” Therefore, religion is undoubtedly “an external force invading the individual’s spiritual realm,” violating human rights. He strongly opposed church schools and the YMCA, which “use various suggestions to tempt underage students to believe in their Christianity.” For this reason, he advocated for “educational independence,” asserting that it must be “transcendent to all denominations” and specifically stated that (1) universities could offer courses in the history of religion and comparative religion but should not have theological studies; (2) no school should have courses that promote doctrines or conduct prayer services; (3) those who engage in proselytism should not participate in educational endeavors. In response to the World Student Christian Federation annual meeting, Cai questioned why these students “would wish to carry a Christian title,” and he also questioned Tsinghua University about why it “would provide a venue for a religious alliance.” He supported the establishment of a “Non-Religious Alliance,” reiterating that this did not hinder “freedom of belief,” because “to believe is freedom, and to not believe is also freedom.” “Since we have this ‘non-religious’ belief and encounter the necessity of this ‘non-religious’ movement, we are free to conduct our movement without any reservations!”

Cai Yuanpei’s advocacy for the separation of religion and education was implemented when he became the president of the “Academy” in 1927. In December, the Academy announced the “Regulations for the Establishment of Private Universities and Specialized Schools” and “Regulations for the Establishment of Private Secondary Schools and Primary Schools,” with the underlying spirit being the separation of religion from education. Church schools at all levels could not avoid the demand to relinquish educational authority; thus, Christianity’s involvement in education in China entered a new phase.

  1. Li Weihan (Li Weihan 1987: 6-8)

Yun Daiying (惲代英, 1895-1931), hailing from Wuchang, Hubei. During his early years of study in Wuchang, he was exposed to the Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA). Although he appreciated certain aspects, he ultimately refused to become a Christian. In 1919, he joined the Young China Association and the following year organized the Chinese Socialist Youth League. He later joined the Chinese Communist Party and became one of its early members. After the split between the Nationalist and Communist parties, he participated in the uprisings in Nanchang and Guangzhou. He was arrested in Shanghai in 1930 and executed by firing squad in Nanjing in April 1931.

In 1923, Yun Daiying published articles in “China Youth” in November and December opposing Christianity. The first article countered the YMCA’s notion of “personality saving the nation,” questioning “who has ruined China with an absence of personality?” One culprit pointed out was “the benefactors of the YMCA, the British and Americans.” These “consumers of Christianity,” “rely on the protection of foreign capitalists,” and when they say, “personality saves the nation,” it sounds all too grand! (9) Christianity is “a tool of foreigners to soften China”; (10) Christians enjoy protection under extraterritoriality.

In May 1926, he delivered a speech titled “Jesus, Confucius, and Revolutionary Youth” at Lingnan University, a time when the Non-Christian Movement was taking place. In his speech, he praised many teachers and students at Lingnan as comrades in anti-imperialism, expressing his pleasure to speak at “this university sympathetic to anti-imperialism.”

In the speech, Yun Daiying plainly criticized Jesus, asserting that Jesus was a “sage” surpassing Confucius, because while Confucius “only preached persuasion,” Jesus possessed a “revolutionary spirit,” “similar to our revolutionary party today, closely spreading awareness to the masses,” even reproaching oppressors. However, neither Confucius’ “persuasion” nor Jesus’ “rebuke” could save the world; only revolution, “the act of striking,” could. Moreover, today, Jesus’ disciples, faced with Jesus’ rebuke, met their deaths. Ultimately, they “learn the teachings of Jesus” but do not have the “courage” to oppose imperialism as Jesus did.

Yun Daiying acknowledged that there were many good people within the church, “who can neither stop the acts of violence by imperialism and warlords… nor dare to advocate for Chinese civilians to resist imperialism or warlords’ revolutionary actions.” Instead, they “cling to the teachings of peace and submission” and “propagate doctrines of peace and tolerance.” Therefore, his opposition to Christianity stemmed from the fact that many good Christian people not only “do not engage in revolution” but also “advocate against revolution,” even “enticing many to stray from the path of overthrowing imperialism and warlords.” In his writings, Yun did not shy away from advocating the abolition of foreign-run schools, arguing that “foreign teachers love their own countries.” In establishing schools in China, they “may seek happiness for us Chinese, however, they also absolutely do not wish for the Chinese students they educate to oppose their home countries,” and they “do not desire goodwill from us towards their imperialist governments.” In these schools, only the imperialists’ government is presented as being “good” to us while hiding their own wrongdoings. In June 1926, Yun Daiying published an article titled “Against the Cultural Invasion of Imperialism” in “Guangdong Youth,” providing a detailed analysis of cultural invasion. He categorized the cultural policies used by imperialists to “soften and subdue weak nations” as part of “cultural invasion,” which included proselytizing, church schools, accepting international students, publishing, and public speaking. Through these avenues, imperialists conveyed four types of ideas: (1) Propagating superstitions about God, Jesus, heaven, and hell, which distracts weak nations from their immediate sufferings and leads them to hope for future happiness in the vague and insubstantial; (2) Praising the civilization of imperialist countries while concealing their societal evils, causing weak nations to be dazzled and confused by these claims, willingly submitting and acknowledging imperialist countries as centers of civilization; (3) Promoting a one-sided doctrine of peace and brotherhood, while failing to point out or oppose the crimes of imperialism against weak nations. Thus, their so-called peace and brotherhood merely help imperialists ensure that weak nations do not resist their oppression; (4) Celebrating the alleged virtues of imperialists towards weak nations while failing to mention the oppression imposed by imperialists, instead highlighting a few matters that superficially appear beneficial to weak nations, thereby invoking gratitude and ensuring that weak nations remain passive subjects.

Yun Daiying pointed out that there are 8,000 foreign missionaries dispersed throughout China, establishing churches and recruiting congregants, with more than 300,000 communion members nationwide. Additionally, there are over 40 YMCA locations in various cities, boasting around 8,000 members. Churches have established over 2,000 primary and secondary schools, with a total of 100,000 students. All of these are “nurtured and deceived directly or indirectly by imperialism, entirely unaware or unwilling to resist imperialism.” Anglo-American imperialists are actively expanding church operations and are using the Boxer Indemnity as funding for cultural invasion, fundamentally destroying the spirit and movements of the Chinese nation. Therefore, there is an urgent need to oppose this “cultural invasion.” In conclusion, he called for “Christian churches and church schools to immediately sever their ties with imperialism,” “oppose imperialists operating any cultural enterprises in China,” and urged “Christian believers and church students to rise and join the anti-cultural invasion movement.”

  1. Zhang Zhenzhi/Zhang Zhenzhen (Zhang Zhenzhen 1929: 1-110)

Zhang Zhenzhi (pen name Zhenzhen, dates of birth and death unknown), a member of the Kuomintang, worked at the Central Party Headquarters. He authored several works, including “The Pathology of Contemporary Chinese Society” (1929), “The Trinity of Party, Government, and People” (1929), “On the Ease of Knowing and Acting,” “Revolution and Religion” (1929), and “A Critique of Hu Shi’s Anti-Party Report” (1930). As he stated in the “Foreword”: “The purpose of this book is to clarify the distinctions between religious thought and revolutionary thought. The book is divided into two parts, the first being “Discussion on the Issue of Christianity,” which critiques Christianity and includes a debate between him and Christian Zhang Shizhang in the supplement to the “Republic Daily.” The second part is “Folklore Conversations,” primarily critiquing folk religions and beliefs.

In Zhang Zhenzhi’s view, the sins of Christianity in China include: (1) Serving as a protective cover for the economic and military aggression of imperialists; (2) Encompassing congregants such that their properties gradually become church property; (3) Involving pastors and priests in political interference in the interior, with no Chinese or Western officials daring to take action; (4) Using believers to oppress non-believing citizens. Thus, he believes that “overthrowing Christianity” is justifiable. He even advocated for the confiscation of all church institutions for use as labor schools. He also rejected the principle of “freedom of belief,” positing that belief itself is “superstition”; under “superstitious belief,” freedoms are non-existent. Therefore, “a person has freedom only when they do not believe; once they believe, their freedom is taken away by the religion.” As a member of the Kuomintang, he further opposed “cooperation between the party and religion.”Because the Kuomintang could not collaborate with Christianity that is related to imperialism. As a revolutionary party, the Kuomintang only believed in one ideology and worshiped the party leaders; “there is only the freedom of the party, not the freedom of the individual.” Thus, both the party and the religion are “unique,” and religion stands in opposition to the party, which necessitates its overthrow.

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. Shanghai “Anti-Christian Student Federation,” 1922 (Zhang Qinshi 1928: 187-190; Lutz 1988: 59-60)

In response to the World Student Christian Federation convening its 11th annual meeting in Beijing in April 1922, an organization called the “Anti-Christian Student Federation” was established in Shanghai in March. On March 9, the federation sent a telegram to “students of Tsinghua School in Beijing and students from all schools across the country,” indicating that the concept of the separation of church and state, as well as the separation of education and religion, has been popular in Europe since the Renaissance. However, since modern times, Christianity has become the “vanguard of colonialism,” with “the gospel enslaving us spiritually.” Facing the threat of “Christianization and capitalization,” the World Student Christian Federation gathered in “the capital of our weak nation,” hence appealing to “you all, to express your opinions and jointly denounce oppression, with the aim of ensuring the purity of our youth’s educational sphere.”

On the same day, the Shanghai “Anti-Christian Student Federation” issued a declaration denouncing Christianity and the church as roots of many historical evils. Modern Christianity and the church are further identified as a demon that aids the “bourgeoisie” in plundering and oppressing the “proletariat,” thus becoming part of “a brutal, oppressive, and miserable capitalist society,” a “demon that aids the tyranny.” The declaration pointed out that churches established by various countries in China aimed to “tempt the Chinese people into welcoming capitalism” and “to cultivate the good dogs of capitalists.” Therefore, while opposing capitalism, the Chinese people must also oppose “this modern Christianity and church that supports capitalism and deceives the common people.”

The declaration concluded that the “World Student Christian Federation” is also a product of modern Christianity and the church, and its annual meeting is a “robbery conference that humiliates our youth, deceives our people, and plunders our economy.” Hence, it was decided to “declare war” on it.

In the 4th issue of “Pioneer” published on March 15, 1922, there was “Anti-Christian Student Federation Announcement.” This was the official publication of the Chinese Socialist Youth League, containing: “Anti-Christian Student Federation Declaration, Telegram, Constitution,” “Christianity and World Transformation” (Chi Guang), “Christianity and Capitalism” (Lu Shu), “Christianity and Communism” (Qi Yuan), and “Christianity and Women” (Zhe Hua), among other articles.

In April 1922, the Anti-Christian Student Federation became a permanent organization, simultaneously soliciting more members under both the names of “Anti-Christian Student Federation” and “Anti-Christian Federation.” On April 20, a notice titled “Anti-Christian Federation Seeks Comrades” was published in the supplementary section “Awakening” of Shanghai’s “Republic Daily,” indicating that invading powers exploit Christianity to “enslave the spirit of colonized and invaded nations.”

  1. Beijing “Anti-Religion Federation,” 1922 (Zhang Qinshi 1928: 193-199; Lutz 1988: 59-60)

Following the Shanghai Anti-Christian Student Federation, the “Anti-Religion Federation” was established in Beijing. On March 21, 1922, Li Shizeng, Xiao Zisheng, Li Dazhao, Miao Boying, and 79 others co-signed and sent a telegram to newspaper offices, schools, organizations across the country, and the Chinese people, stating that “the poison of religion is rampant…heretical teachings are widespread,” and that “the world Christian students” are about to “propagate superstition” in the capital. Therefore, they organized the “Anti-Religion Federation,” aiming for “the spirit of science to shine forth with evolution.” On April 1, a second telegram was issued, reiterating that the federation is not “exclusive,” but merely “non-religious” in the sense of “science defeating religion.”

In its declaration, it clearly stated that religion and scientific truth are incompatible and completely contrary to humanitarianism, thus “religion and humanity cannot coexist.” Although positioning itself as “non-religious,” it specifically targeted Christianity, stating that it incites youth through youth associations, and various activities are simply “poisonous anesthetics and hypnotism.” It welcomed “all who are not superstitious of religion, or wish to eliminate the harm of religion” to become comrades of the Anti-Religion Federation.

  1. Shanghai “Anti-Christian Federation,” 1924 (Zhang Qinshi 1928: 376-380; 293-315)

In August 1924, the “Anti-Christian Federation” was reorganized, with Tang Gongxian (Chinese Communist Party) serving as chairman, and Wu Zhihui (Kuomintang) drafting the constitution. Among the five executive members, three were members of the Communist Party (Tang, Ke Bianian, Zhang Qiuren).

On August 19, the declaration and constitution of the Anti-Christian Federation were published in the “Awakening” supplement of the “Republic Daily.” The declaration began by outlining the reasons for “opposing Christianity,” including both “general meanings” and “specific meanings.”In the “general sense,” opposing Christianity is similar to opposing other religions, primarily targeting the “superstition” inherent in religions, which “calls for a false concept of happiness,” as the happiness hoped for lies in the future, thus “disregarding the material struggles of the present world and believing that such struggles are futile.” “All religions have these same evils, and Christianity, with its particularly powerful organization, is notably harmful, thus we should especially oppose Christianity.”

Moreover, opposing Christianity has its “specific meaning,” as Christianity has been used as a tool by the “privileged class” both in the “feudal era” and the “capitalist era.” Particularly in the capitalist era, it has anesthetized the working class in our country, leading them to believe that social wealth disparity is “ordained by divine will,” and thus “class struggle” would violate divine will; or it has “anesthetized the populations of conquered colonies and semi-colonies,” leading them to believe that the military forces in the colonies come “to deliver God’s blessings and to offer education and all culture,” consequently “not thinking of resistance.” This is precisely “the greatest reason why we, the Chinese people, must specifically oppose Christianity.” Missionaries coming to China, whether “preaching or educating,” intentionally or unintentionally “propagandize their international capitalist worldview, which undermines China’s national consciousness and patriotism.”

After the reorganization of the Anti-Christian Federation in 1924, activities opposing Christianity were planned more systematically, including the publication of materials and organizing lectures, as well as conducting research on Christianity and its enterprises. The Kuomintang’s publication “Republic Daily” featured a supplementary section “Awakening,” which regularly published “Anti-Christian Special Issues” as the official publication of the Anti-Christian Federation. For example, the “Editorial” in January 1925, titled “An Unprecedented Anti-Christian Movement,” stated that “unless imperialism is fundamentally overthrown, the power of Christianity cannot be fundamentally eradicated. If we genuinely want to oppose Christianity, we must strive to oppose the imperialism movement.” “Opposing Christianity is also a method of opposing imperialism—an indirect method of opposition; at the same time, it can reduce the power of imperialism and expose the evils of imperialism.” “We hope that our compatriots, unwilling to submit to the forces of imperialism and Christianity, will make extra efforts in the anti-Christian movement, making this Christmas a festival of the anti-Christian movement.” Similarly, publications from the Communist Party (such as “The Guide,” “Chinese Youth”) also published numerous articles criticizing Christianity.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Anti-Christian Movement, 1922-1927 (Lutz 1988; Yang Tianhong 1994: 122-220, 327-422; Xing Fuzeng 1994: 135-136)

In the 1920s, the significant impact faced by Christianity in China was undoubtedly the “Anti-Christian Movement” (1922-1927). “Anti-Christian” means opposition to Christianity; however, in this context, “opposition” or “anti” does not merely refer to general doubts or objections to Christian beliefs, but to a mass movement mobilizing against Christianity through propaganda and even violent means. The organizers of this movement were political parties and their peripheral groups, and their accusations against Christianity had a strong ideological struggle with “anti-imperialism.”

The Anti-Christian Movement can be divided into two phases. The first phase erupted in April 1922 in response to the World Student Christian Federation’s annual meeting held in Beijing. The Anti-Christian Student Federation and the Anti-Religion Federation were established successively in Shanghai and Beijing, and then spread to other cities (including Beijing, Wuhu, Taiyuan, Baoding, Tianjin, Hangzhou, Tangshan, Nanchang, Xiamen, Wuchang, Guangzhou, Changsha, etc.), establishing various Anti-Christian Student Federations or Anti-Religion Federations between March and April. Some scholars, based on Soviet archives, have pointed out that the Anti-Christian Movement in 1922 was not a spontaneous movement, but was initiated and led by the Chinese Communist Party under the direct guidance of the Soviet Communist Party, the Far East Bureau of the Communist International, and the Youth International, with members from the Kuomintang participating in this political struggle (Tao Feiya 2003a: 122).

The focus of the first phase of the Anti-Christian Movement was directed towards the annual meeting of the World Student Christian Federation. Although the struggle also targeted Christianity, the Shanghai Anti-Christian Student Federation renamed itself to Anti-Christian Federation in April. However, as the annual meeting concluded, the targets of the struggle or opposition were lost, affecting the movement’s mobilization capacity.

In 1924, the Shanghai “Anti-Christian Federation” was reorganized, marking the beginning of the second phase of the Anti-Christian Movement. This phase was closely related to the first cooperation between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party. In January 1924, the Kuomintang held its first national congress in Guangzhou, where Sun Yat-sen proposed the three major policies of “unite with Russia, unite with the communists, and support the peasants and workers,” allowing Communist Party members to join the Kuomintang in their individual capacity. The “Declaration of the First National Congress of the Kuomintang” clearly pointed out that imperialism and warlords were the roots of China’s turmoil, stating that only by opposing imperialism could China achieve national freedom and independence. As a result, both parties actively engaged in leading anti-imperial struggles throughout the country, under the anti-imperialist demands, “anti-Christian” became a target of the “anti-imperialist” struggle. The “Anti-Christian Federation” became an important platform for the Kuomintang-Communist cooperation against imperialism, and both parties used their respective propaganda publications (such as the Kuomintang’s “Republic Daily” and the Communist Party’s “Chinese Youth,” “The Guide”) to publish numerous articles accusing Christianity of being a tool of imperialism in China. At this time, “cultural invasion” became a significant discourse in the Communist Party’s critique of Christianity (Tao Feiya 2003b).

In December 1924, the Anti-Christian Federation called for the establishment of Anti-Christian Federations across various regions and designated the week before and after Christmas as “Anti-Christian Movement Week,” mobilizing crowds for parades, distributing Anti-Christian leaflets and publications, and holding Anti-Christian meetings. As a result, areas such as Changsha, Guangzhou, Suzhou, Jiujiang, Shanghai, Shandong, Shaoxing, Taiyuan, Ningbo, and Qingdao responded. In January 1925, an editorial in the “Awakening” section of the “Republic Daily” reiterated that “opposing Christianity is also a way to oppose imperialism…it can extinguish the power of imperialism and expose its evils” (Chen Tong 1925).

In 1925, following the “May Thirtieth Incident,” the national anti-imperial sentiment peaked, leading to strikes, boycotts, and class walkouts across various regions, and the anti-Christian mass movement also leaned towards violence. In 1926, as the National Revolutionary Army launched its Northern Expedition, the areas reached by the Northern Expedition Army also stimulated mass movements, with churches, schools, and hospitals being impacted, and even occupied by the Northern Expedition Army. In the context of a severe political situation, missionaries began to evacuate from various locations.

By 1927, a new situation arose in China. On one hand, the National Government was established, with Nanjing as its capital. On the other hand, the Kuomintang carried out a party purge, leading to the breakdown of the first Kuomintang-Communist cooperation. At this time, the Kuomintang had become the ruling party of the unified regime, publicly affirming religious freedom while opposing anti-religious events characterized by mass movements. For instance, in 1927, the Central Executive Committee of the Kuomintang passed an order stating that “the public should not misunderstand the fall of imperialism as a justification for an anti-foreign and anti-religious nature, nor should any force be used to oppress or infringe upon the freedom of faith of Chinese and foreign people.” Against this backdrop, the originally initiated Anti-Christian Movement by the Kuomintang and the Communist Party was on the verge of conclusion.However, within the Kuomintang, there remained a strong anti-Christian sentiment. This form of “opposition” no longer took place through mobilizing mass movements but operated on an ideological level. After 1927, many Kuomintang members continued to oppose Christianity in their personal or party capacities, harboring animosity towards believers under the pretext of religious sentiments. In December 1928, the Kuomintang’s publication “Republic Daily” still released an “Anti-Christian Special Issue.” In January 1929, it further issued a substantial study on unequal treaties, exceeding a hundred pages, detailing how Christianity conducted cultural invasion using extensive textual explanations and various charts.

  1. Educational Rights Movement, 1922-1927 (Yang Tianhong 1994: 241-276; Yang Cuihua 1984: 235-289; Lutz 1988: 91-159)

The Educational Rights Movement was an important aspect of the Anti-Christian Movement and ultimately implemented through government regulations. Its primary focus targeted the educational enterprises established by Christianity in China.

According to the “Christian Education in China: The Report of the China Education Commission of 1921-1922” published in 1922, there were 6,890 church-run primary and secondary schools across the country, with a total of 199,604 students. If various types of Christian educational institutions (such as Bible schools, seminaries, special education, orphanages, nurse training, etc.) are included, there were a total of 7,382 Christian educational institutions, serving 214,254 students. At that time, the total number of students at all levels nationwide was 5,700,000. The number of students enrolled in Christian educational institutions accounted for only 3.76% of the total student population. However, in higher education, students attending Christian universities (2,017) constituted 7.9% of students in national universities (25,373) (China Education Commission 1922: 371-374).

After the establishment of the Republic, the issue of Christian-run education began to attract the attention of Chinese intellectuals. Many educators (such as Cai Yuanpei) advocated for the separation of religion from education, especially criticizing the religious courses and activities in church schools. The proselytization to minors was particularly condemned by educational scholars. Amid the wave of nationalism in the 1920s, students, driven by patriotic feelings, participated in student movements, and church schools were no exception. However, the missionaries leading the church schools took a high-handed approach to prohibit such activities, creating conflicts and opposition between students and the school authorities. During this period, some students began to protest against “enslavement” education. Consequently, church schools found themselves at odds with Chinese nationalism.

In the 1920s, the intellectual community further proposed recommendations regarding the relationship between religion and education in China. In December 1921, the China Educational Improvement Society was established, with board members including Cai Yuanpei, Huang Yanpei, and Zhang Boling. At their annual meeting in July of the following year, Hu Shi suggested that “no religious education (including theory and rituals) should be present in any primary school (including kindergartens),” which was supported by Tao Menghe and Ding Wenjiang. After the meeting, the China Educational Improvement Society officially communicated its position to various church schools and religious group schools. In July 1924, at the third annual meeting of the China Educational Improvement Society, a proposal was made to reclaim educational rights: “Request to strive for the recovery of educational rights,” “No person without citizenship of the Republic of China shall conduct national education on the territory of the Republic of China,” and “Request to prohibit foreigners from establishing schools in China.” The amendments to the final three proposals were approved, primarily requesting the government to formulate strict school registration regulations, stipulating that “any foreigner using schools to carry out aggression, upon thorough investigation, should have their operations ordered to cease by the government.”

In October 1924, the tenth annual meeting of the National Education Union was held, passing proposals related to reclaiming educational rights. In 1925, the publication “Chinese Educational Circle” released a special issue on the “Educational Rights Movement,” reaffirming that education is a matter of national sovereignty. However, due to the warlord divisions in China at that time, the lack of a unified national government meant that the recommendations made by educational organizations to the government lacked a realistic basis for implementation.

In February 1926, the Guangzhou National Government established an Educational Administrative Committee, with Xu Chongqing, a supporter of reclaiming educational rights, in charge. In June, a draft policy separating religion and education was published.In October, regulations such as the “Regulations for Private Schools,” “Regulations for the Establishment of Boards of Private Schools,” and “School Registration Regulations” were issued, requiring church schools to register and prohibiting them from offering religious courses or forcing students to participate in religious activities. In July, under the leadership of Chiang Kai-shek, the Kuomintang began the Northern Expedition. As the National Revolutionary Army advanced, various local warlord regimes successively changed banners. By April 1927, the National Government was established with Nanjing as its capital. The Northern Expedition concluded the following year, leading to a unified government in China. The Nanjing National Government established a “University College,” with Cai Yuanpei as its president. Based on the foundation of a unified government, the demand for the reclamation of educational rights was finally realized. Christian education was incorporated into the Chinese educational system, requiring all church schools to register with the government. School principals had to be Chinese nationals, and the board of directors had to consist of more than half Chinese members, with foreigners prohibited from being board chairpersons. Religious courses could not be classified as required subjects, and students could not be compelled to participate in religious ceremonies.

By 1931, except for St. John’s University, West China Union University, and South China Women’s College of Arts and Sciences, the other ten Christian universities had registered with the Chinese government. At the primary and secondary school levels, by around 1929, approximately 70% of church schools had completed or were in the process of registering.

  1. The Evacuation, 1927 (Boynton 1928: 155-159; Zhang Junjun 1928: 1-6; Jiang Diao Zhi 1928: 6-10)

In 1924, the number of missionaries in China reached a historical peak of 7,663. However, during 1927, nearly 3,000 missionaries left China (including those on leave in 1928 and some who returned early in 1929), resulting in a sharp decline in the number of missionaries to 3,113. The primary reason for the withdrawal of missionaries was the instability of the political situation in China. In July 1926, Chiang Kai-shek was appointed commander of the National Revolutionary Army and issued the mobilization order for the Northern Expedition, launching an offensive from Guangdong. Within six months, he defeated the two major warlords, Wu Peifu and Sun Chuanfang, expanding the National Government’s control from Guangdong to the provinces of Hunan, Hubei, Jiangxi, and Fujian. In January 1927, the Northern Expedition Army forcibly took over the British concessions in Hankou and Jiujiang, prompting foreign nationals across various locations along the Yangtze River to retreat to Shanghai. In March, Shanghai and Nanjing fell into the hands of the revolutionary army. After entering Nanjing, on March 24, radicals attacked British, American, and Japanese consulates and foreign churches, schools, hospitals, and shops, resulting in the tragic deaths of some missionaries in what became known as the “Nanjing Incident.”

In provinces reached by the Northern Expedition Army, missionaries retreated to nearby provinces to observe the situation. Following the recovery of the British concession in Hankou, more missionaries withdrew. Simultaneously, various regions experienced anti-Christian mass movements. For instance, in Christmas 1926, Hunan hosted an “Anti-Christian Week,” featuring marches and speeches, with attacks on churches, and missionaries being paraded and bound in public, along with churches, church schools, hospitals, and youth associations being occupied. Under these circumstances, missionaries evacuated from Hunan province, leaving only about 30 to 40 behind. Most churches and schools in Changsha, Xiangtan, Hengzhou, and Yuezhou were closed. Zhang Junjun of the Hunan Zhendao Association described the impact faced by Hunan churches as “a stormy and rapid downpour.” Furthermore, due to the Nanjing Incident, local churches faced even more severe impacts, with most churches in the city occupied by revolutionary forces, and churches and schools being looted. The Nanjing Christian Association noted, “The behavior against churches by the revolutionary army upon entering the city resembled a storm and torrential rain, a terrifying wave sweeping through and overwhelming the land, comparable to the disasters of the Boxer Uprising.”

C. L. Boynton pointed out that the reasons for the missionaries’ departure included: (1) being advised by Chinese colleagues to leave; (2) receiving immediate evacuation orders from consuls; (3) concerns about creating more international disputes if they stayed; and (4) being ordered to evacuate by churches or mission societies.

As the situation stabilized, missionaries began to return to their fields. Of the more than 3,000 who left China in 1927, by 1928, approximately 500 to 1,000 returned. By the eve of the War of Resistance, the number had risen again to 6,020.
