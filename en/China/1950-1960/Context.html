China: Context (1950-1960)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

Regulations Related to Religion

(1) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Issues Concerning Catholicism and Christianity, 1950 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 1911-1914)

In August 1950, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China issued the "Instructions on Issues Concerning Catholicism and Christianity," which defined the issues related to Catholicism and Christianity as both a "religious issue" and a tool used by imperialism to conduct cultural invasion in our country. Regarding the former, the document stated that while Marxists believe religion is "harmful to the awaking of the people," they oppose "relying solely on administrative orders and simplistic impatient methods" to address religious issues, because religious issues also have a "mass nature" that requires careful steps; otherwise, it would lead to significant resentment and hostility among the large group of followers toward the CCP. Therefore, the Party's policy is to insist on protecting the freedom of belief while also conducting patriotic propaganda among the faithful within Christianity and Catholicism to "expose the imperialist cultural invasion and espionage schemes," so that it can "transform from tools of imperialism into a religious cause of the Chinese people." The "Instructions" placed special emphasis on the role of "patriotic believers," who must be "led and supported" to "unite" the majority of devout believers and "oppose" the few reactionaries still colluding with imperialism. It is worth noting that the "Instructions" emphasize opposition to imperialism does not equate to "conducting a mass anti-religion movement," reiterating that "implementing the common program, realizing land reform, striving for fundamental improvements in finance and economy, and widely promoting materialism and scientific knowledge will gradually reduce the space for religion." This is the first document after the establishment of the PRC that elaborated on its policies regarding Christianity and Catholicism, reflecting the tone of "New Democracy," limiting struggles to "exposing imperialist cultural invasion and conspiracy activities," "opposing the few reactionaries still colluding with imperialism," and "freeing the church from the influence of imperialism and its economic relations," also reaffirming that related struggles "should not implicate the entire church, church buildings, or church schools," and must ensure "the majority of believers also unite under the banner of patriotism."

(2) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Handling Procedures for Cultural and Educational Relief Organizations and Religious Groups Receiving US Subsidies, 1950 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 1923-1925)

With the military confrontation between China and the US on the Korean Peninsula, on December 16, the United States announced the implementation of an economic embargo against China and froze Chinese public and private properties in the US. On December 28, the Chinese government also ordered the regulation of American properties in China and froze American public and private deposits in China, issuing the "Instructions on Handling Procedures for Cultural and Educational Relief Organizations and Religious Groups Receiving US Subsidies," which stated that due to long-term imperialist aggression, many cultural and educational relief organizations and religious groups in China received US subsidies. Now, "as the anti-American patriotic movement has reached a climax," coupled with "the American imperialists freezing our funds," it was decided to adopt registration and takeover methods, "to completely sever their ties with American imperialism." The State Council of the Central People's Government will announce related registration methods, requiring these organizations to register. The instructions also proposed three handling methods for takeovers: (1) Government takeover as state agencies: mainly targeting some schools, hospitals, and all relief organizations; (2) Commissioning Chinese private groups to run them, with state subsidies: mainly targeting most universities, primary and secondary schools, and some hospitals; (3) Implementing self-management by Chinese people: mainly targeting some economically self-sufficient primary schools and all religious organizations. The instructions also stated: "It is necessary to launch a mass anti-American patriotic movement in all cultural and educational relief organizations and religious groups receiving US subsidies, especially in church schools, to expose imperialist invasion conspiracies... to hold accusations and demonstrations against American imperialism, expose and combat the few reactionaries, and unite the vast majority of the masses." This is the most important step to ensure that these organizations "forever sever ties with imperialist influence." During the process, "it is crucial to strictly ensure no anti-religion or exclusion of foreigners," and that if foreigners sincerely "serve our country with their knowledge and skills and comply with the laws of the Chinese government, they may be retained depending on the situation."

On December 29, the 65th executive meeting of the State Council approved the "Decision of the State Council of the Central People's Government on Handling the Policies for Cultural and Educational Relief Organizations and Religious Groups Receiving US Subsidies," and the "Regulations on the Registration of Cultural and Educational Relief Organizations and Religious Groups Receiving Foreign Subsidies and Foreign Investments." On January 14, 1951, the Cultural and Educational Committee issued the "Implementation Measures for the Registration of Cultural and Educational Relief Organizations and Religious Groups Receiving Foreign Subsidies and Foreign Investments."

(3) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on the Establishment of a Religious Issues Committee and Religious Affairs Office, 1951 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 1927-1928)

On January 9, 1951, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China issued "Instructions on the Establishment of a Religious Issues Committee and Religious Affairs Office," stating that "in order to systematically eliminate the influence of imperialism in China, to combat imperialism's conspiracy to strengthen its influence on the Chinese people through religion (Catholicism, Christianity, and Buddhism), and to promote the anti-imperialist patriotic movement and self-governance, self-financing, and self-propagation among religious groups, the decision of the State Council regarding the handling of cultural and educational relief organizations and religious groups receiving US subsidies must be implemented, and specialized departments for handling religious affairs must be established within the Party and government." Its responsibilities include fully understanding the situations of various religions, strengthening communication with religious figures, assisting the Party and government in determining policies on religious issues, and addressing specific matters related to religion. Accordingly, the instructions decided: (1) On the Party organizational side, a "Religious Issues Committee" must be organized under the Central Committee and various central bureaus and branches, composed of responsible Party members from the propaganda department, United Front Work Department, Youth League, and governmental agencies such as public security, foreign affairs, internal affairs, intelligence, ethnic affairs, and relief, to be led by the propaganda department, initially guiding and researching policies and guidelines on the issues of Christianity, Catholicism, and Buddhism, and designating specific individuals to handle daily work; (2) On the government side, a "Religious Affairs Office" will be established under the Cultural and Educational Committee of the State Council and the Cultural and Educational Committees of the People’s Governments in major administrative regions (Military and Political Committees) to handle and research policy work related to Christianity, Catholicism, and Buddhism. As for work involving Islam and Lamaism, it will be the responsibility of the Ethnic Affairs Commission, with the Religious Affairs Office providing assistance.

(4) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Actively Promoting the Religious Reform Movement Instructions and Re-Instructions, 1951 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 1929-1932, 1936-1937)

The First National Religious Work Conference was held in Beijing from March 15 to 19, 1951. Before the conference, on March 5, the Central Committee issued "Instructions on Actively Promoting the Religious Reform Movement," categorizing the reform movements in Christianity and Catholicism as an important part of the "anti-imperialist political struggle," with the primary objective of eliminating the influence of imperialism on China's cultural invasion. To successfully advance the reform movement, the government must act proactively and negotiate with upper-level Chinese leaders, while under lower-level believers carry out patriotic education, "mobilizing them to pressure the upper levels to implement self-governance, self-financing, and self-propagation." The "Instructions" clearly stated that when promoting the religious reform movement in various places, "it must be pointed out that the churches in China have been used by imperialism as tools to invade China and undermine the new China," while distinguishing "ordinary believers from the few reactionaries among believers" to "win over the majority." At the same time, it should emphasize that "good believers must be good patriotic citizens." Education for believers should focus on patriotism, "avoiding discussions of materialism or idealism, and not harming the religious feelings of believers." It further stated: "In this movement, we must unite the vast majority of the religious community, organize, develop, and consolidate a united front against imperialism throughout the national religious circles," while patiently "inspiring their patriotism, alleviating their concerns, and avoiding impatience, which could lead to oppression against religion and exclusion." Regarding law-abiding foreign missionaries who have been in China for many years, as long as they "do not engage in any political activities and do not manage church affairs, they may continue to stay." As for the Vatican, under the conditions of "non-interference in China's religious reform movement and non-political activity in China, Chinese believers and the Vatican may still have purely religious ties." The instructions indicated that a meeting would be held in April for Christianity to discuss further realization of the "Three Selfs," and it is hoped that a Catholic meeting could also be convened in the latter half of the year.

In April, the Central Committee issued further "Instructions on Actively Leading the Religious Reform Movement," indicating that in areas where religious activities have been absent for many years, "religious activities should no longer be resumed, nor should any religious reform movement be conducted, and no outside missionaries or reorganization of church affairs should be allowed," because the fundamental principle regarding religious activities is "to transform rather than develop or prohibit." At the same time, those "remaining faithful believers" should be regarded as an important target of the united front, guiding them to "completely separate politically from the reactionaries of imperialism and be led by us," directly publicizing the anti-American and anti-imperialist patriotic campaign, promoting the religious policies of the People's Government, exposing the crimes of imperialism utilizing Catholicism and Christianity for invasion, and encouraging them to participate in learning and accusations, thus attracting them into the popular democratic united front.

(5) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Handling the Relationship Between Chinese Catholics and the Vatican, 1951 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 1934-1935)

On March 7, 1951, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China issued "Instructions on Handling the Relationship Between Chinese Catholics and the Vatican," stating that the Vatican is one of the centers of imperialist anti-communist conspiracies. Therefore, the principle of reform for Catholicism must be to sever ties with the Vatican in order to be truly realized. However, most Chinese bishops, priests, and believers have not yet recognized the necessity of breaking away from the Vatican, and resolving this issue will still require considerable time. Thus, in order to win over the "intermediate forces" within Catholicism, it is appropriate to clarify that "it is not prohibited for Chinese Catholics to maintain purely religious ties with the Vatican, but we firmly oppose any actions or statements by the Vatican that are against the democratic cause of the Chinese people and suppress or undermine the implementation of true self-reliant reforms within Chinese Catholicism." Hence, it is suggested that in the future, when Catholic leaders issue statements, it is best to not mention the Vatican (or the Pope); if mentioned, it should declare that the Vatican must not interfere in Chinese politics and must not conduct anti-People’s Government, anti-communist, anti-patriotic, or anti-reform propaganda and activities.

(6) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Issues Concerning Buddhism Among the Han Nationality, 1951 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 1940-1942)

On June 12, 1951, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China issued "Instructions on Issues Concerning Buddhism Among the Han Nationality," stating that the relationship between Buddhism and imperialism is less pronounced than that of Catholicism and Christianity, but imperialism continues to employ Buddhism as a tool for aggression and enslaving people in Southeast Asia. In China, Buddhism has long become a tool for the feudal ruling class to oppress and exploit peasants, and it still holds significant influence among the Han people. Buddhism among the Han Nationality, unlike the Lamaist issues related to ethnic problems, can be treated as a "purely religious issue." Currently, some regions have monks and laypersons participating in patriotic demonstration movements; the most important task for Buddhism is to eliminate spies, agents, and other reactionaries, thus purging imperialist political influence among Buddhists. Ordinary practitioners who merely offer incense and worship should not be viewed as Buddhists. The fundamental principles for Buddhist work include: (1) Monks and nuns should not be left to their own devices, and not be simplified or rushed into action; they must be transformed through labor and patriotic movements, but mobilization for secularization should not be rushed; (2) When the government requisitions or borrows temple properties, prior negotiation should take place, and no occupation of Buddhist halls or shrine rooms should occur, with proper protection for temples with historical or cultural value; (3) Local Buddhist organizations should register according to the regulations for social organizations, and those without revolutionary activities may exist legally; (4) Reforms in Buddhism must be proposed and carried out by the Buddhists themselves, with necessary support from the government; (5) Active members among Buddhists should be cultivated; (6) Investigation and research on the situation of Buddhism should be strengthened in various places; (7) Taoism can refer to the policy guidelines of Buddhism.

(7) Constitution of the People’s Republic of China, 1954 (Documents Research Office of the Central Committee, 1993: 466)

On September 20, 1954, the First Session of the First National People's Congress passed the "Constitution of the People’s Republic of China," which states in Article 8: "Citizens of the People’s Republic of China enjoy freedom of religious belief."

(8) Central Committee's Approval of the "Report on the Leadership Relationship, Organizational Structure, and Business Scope of the Religious Affairs Bureau of the State Council" 1955 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 2054-2055)

On October 18, 1955, the Central Committee approved the report from the Party Group of the Religious Affairs Bureau of the State Council regarding the leadership relationship, organizational structure, and business scope of religious work. According to the report, the Party Group of the Religious Affairs Bureau of the State Council believed that the leadership of religious work within the Party and government showed inconsistencies, which was detrimental to implementing the Party's religious policies, and recommended the following adjustments: (1) Within the government, Han nationality Buddhism and Taoism will be reallocated under the management of all levels of religious affairs departments; (2) Religious work within the Party should be unified under the leadership of Party committees at all levels, with policies concerning the United Front handled by the United Front Work Department and publicity policies by the Propaganda Department; issues relating to the religious problems of ethnic minorities should be jointly handled by local religious affairs departments and ethnic affairs departments at all levels; (3) Each city Party committee and autonomous region Party committee shall establish religious work committees composed of leaders from the Party's propaganda department, United Front Work Department, and government people's committees, public security, religious affairs offices, Youth League, and Women's Federation, directly responsible to the Party committee. Each level of the Party committee should appoint a standing committee member responsible for religious work, and the government people's committee should also designate a Party member to lead the religious work.

(9) Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on the Issue of Religious Work's Party Affiliation, 1957 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 2078)

On March 14, 1957, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China issued a notice regarding the issues of religious work's affiliation within the Party, deciding that the work related to Catholicism, Christianity, Buddhism, Taoism, and Islam would be uniformly overseen by the United Front departments at all levels within the Party and managed by religious affairs departments at all levels within the government.

(10) Central Committee Approved the "Opinions of the United Front Work Department on Conducting a Socialist Education Movement in the Han Nationality Religious Community," 1957 (Edited by the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, 1958: 2079-2081)

On November 2, 1957, the Central Committee approved the "Opinions of the United Front Work Department on Conducting Socialist Education in the Han Nationality Religious Community." The document first summarized the situation of religious reform after the establishment of the PRC, noting that the imperialist forces and counter-revolutionaries had been largely eliminated; however, "in the transition through the socialist revolution, that is, in the struggle between two paths," the situation within the religious community remained "very complex," with many believers having "insufficient political and ideological awareness of socialism." The truly supportive leftist elements within the upper levels were "still very few," and most still held varying degrees of skepticism towards the Party and socialism, with some even firmly opposing it. Therefore, the Central Committee decided to seize the opportunity of the nationwide rectification movement to carry out socialist education targeting religious professionals in the Han religious community (especially within Catholicism and Christianity). The document explicitly stated that "certain rightists" should be appropriately exposed and criticized during the learning process, and the criteria for "rightists" established by Mao Zedong and the Central Committee fully apply to the religious community.

If rightist elements in the religious community have significant influence domestically and abroad, "or if they do not manifest severely but may be utilized by us," they may be handled by "talking rather than fighting, or possibly engaging in criticism in an appropriate manner afterward, with protection for those passing through." Even if they have been "consistently reactionary" in the past, but during the period of free expression have not engaged in rightist actions, only general dissatisfied remarks, they may be "neither expelled nor criticized." As for those who can be criticized or are "unavoidably to be fought against," efforts should be focused on combating them.

Religious leaders or organizations that have been arrested, prosecuted, tried, or sentenced:

(1) The Suppression of the Yiguan Dao (Li Wanqu 1991: 241-275; Wang Qinduan 2022: 43-52)

The Yiguan Dao is a folk religious sect originating from the early Qing Dynasty, referred to by religious studies scholars as a "religious rescue organization." As early as the beginning of 1949, the People's Government in North China had declared the Yiguan Dao an illegal organization and ordered it to be suppressed. However, at that time, due to the ongoing civil war and various takeover tasks, no action was taken. In October 1949, during the first national public security conference, Nie Rongzhen, the commander of the North China Military Region and mayor of Beijing, specifically mentioned the Yiguan Dao, noting that its organization was widespread and that the number of followers in North China alone reached 500,000, posing a political opposition to the CCP. Therefore, it needed to be addressed when conditions were mature.

By October 1950, the Beijing Municipal Public Security Bureau had gained a basic understanding of the Yiguan Dao, submitted a report to the Municipal Committee, formulated policies, and drafted a plan for suppression. In November, the Beijing Municipal Committee reported to the Central Committee and the North China Bureau, proposing to execute measures before the year's end. On December 3, the Municipal Committee further reported to the Central and North China Bureau, deciding to "thoroughly strike and eliminate" the Yiguan Dao.

On the night of December 18, a unified action was taken across Beijing, arresting more than 130 key members of the Yiguan Dao in one night. The next day, on December 19, the "People's Daily" published an editorial titled "Resolutely Suppress the Yiguan Dao," stating: "This is a necessary measure to maintain public security in the capital, protect production, combat counter-revolutionary and destructive activities, and counter revolutionary rumors." It issued a stern warning to all heads of counter-revolutionary sects to cease all illegal activities and to register promptly according to the regulations of the Municipal People's Government. On the same day, posters signed by Mayor Nie Rongzhen, Vice Mayors Zhang Youyu and Wu Han were posted throughout Beijing, announcing that all sects would be immediately dissolved and any activities ceased, while key figures such as point transmitters and other political troublemakers would be immediately arrested. Those outside the point transmitter ranks but above family altar leaders were required to self-register at the municipal public security sub-bureau, submit their organizational relationships, provide evidence, and relinquish any public property, while committing to cease all activities with the government, under severe legal penalties for violators. Work groups composed of Party, governmental, and mass organizations, unified by the Municipal Committee, entered factories, mining areas, villages, and streets where Yiguan Dao followers were present to conduct propaganda and mobilization; local police stations or district and county committees held mass meetings, supported by newspapers, broadcasts, and exhibitions, to publicly expose the crimes of the Yiguan Dao. On December 20, 1950, the "New Min Bao" published news of various sectors supporting the government's suppression of the Yiguan Dao.

(6) The Suppression of the Legion of Mary, 1951 (Liu Jianping 2012: 193-214; Mariani 2011: 75-94; Wong 2021: 197-234)

The Legion of Mary, also known as the "Servants of Mary" or "Mary's Charity Prayer Association," is an international religious organization formed by Catholics engaging in missionary and prayer activities. Its goal is "to assist the Virgin Mary and the Church by prayer and active work under the guidance of Church authorities, to crush the serpent’s head, promote the Kingdom of Christ, and lead each member towards the path of sanctification." The Legion has a complete organizational structure. Its highest governing body is the "Concilium Legionis," which oversees various "senates" made up of representatives from its subordinate units, while the grassroots units are called "praesidia," where each member must faithfully adhere to the legitimate regulations of the praesidium.

In 1948, Riberi established the Legion of Mary in Shanghai, with its headquarters based at the Catholic Central Bureau in China. Subsequently, Legions were also established in places like Beiping, Tianjin, and Hankou.

As the Chinese Catholic Three-Self Reform Movement, led by the CCP, advanced vigorously, local Legions widely regarded "reform" as "fracturing the Church," and "signing" as equivalent to "putting a seal," strongly resisting the reform and opposing the signing.

On June 15, 1951, the Central Committee issued instructions regarding the handling of Catholic issues, explicitly stating the goal of "destroying the national organization of the Legion of Mary" within 1951. On June 24, the "People's Daily" published an editorial titled "Crushing New Conspiracies of Imperialist Elements to Undermine the Catholic Reform Movement," categorizing the Legion of Mary as "an international aggressive tool manipulated by imperialism that is anti-communist, anti-Soviet, and anti-people," and considered it "a secret reactionary organization directly led by Riberi, the imperialist head in Catholicism, and the Catholic Affairs Commission under his direct command." The editorial called for Catholic believers across the nation to "unite to expose the new conspiracies of imperialist elements, raise political vigilance," and resolutely "eliminate the cultural invasive influence of imperialism, especially American imperialism," and "persist in the self-reliant reform movement to the end."

Tianjin was one of the first cities to suppress the Legion of Mary. On July 13, 1951, the Tianjin Military Control Commission declared the Legion of Mary an imperialist-controlled reactionary organization and proceeded with its suppression.

On October 8, the Shanghai Military Control Commission announced the "suppression of the reactionary organization Legion of Mary," and issued the "Registration and Withdrawal Procedures for Members of the Legion of Mary in Shanghai," which stipulated that "anyone holding a position as an officer or above in the branches or higher-level organizations of the Legion of Mary must register at the designated registration office." Ordinary members of the Legion were required to "personally go to the public security bureau at their jurisdiction to formally declare their withdrawal." Subsequently, suppression efforts in Changsha, Hefei, Wuhan, Beijing, Nanjing, Guangzhou, Taiyuan, Xi'an, Lanzhou, and other locations gradually commenced.

By the end of 1954, the CCP had already suppressed the Legion of Mary in more than 30 major and middle-sized cities and over 100 counties across 21 provinces, with over 17,000 members and officials officially withdrawing from the organization, ultimately resulting in the Legion of Mary being "basically defeated" nationwide.

(7) The Dissolution of the Jesus Family, 1952 (Tao Feiya 2004; Luo Weihong 2014: 676-679; Lian 2010: 64-84)

The Jesus Family, founded by Jing Dianying in 1927, was a Chinese indigenous sect characterized by its charismatic features, emphasizing a communal lifestyle. By 1949, there were 104 "houses," with approximately 12,000 believers. After the establishment of the PRC, the Jesus Family actively supported the Three-Self Reform Movement, with 381 of the 1,527 signatories from Shandong Province belonging to the Jesus Family. By April 1951, the total number of signatories from the Jesus Family reached 1,747, accounting for one-fifth of the total signers in Shandong. Jing Dianying was elected as a member of the presidium at a conference convened by the State Council in April 1951 to address Christian groups receiving American subsidies. During the Korean War, the Jesus Family organized a volunteer medical team to aid in the resistance against America and support North Korea, becoming the only Christian sect to reach the frontlines.

In June 1951, at a Three-Self Reform symposium in Tai'an, Jing was a member of the presidium, but accusations against the Jesus Family began to arise, prompting Jing to make a self-criticism. Internal conflicts within the Jesus Family, particularly regarding paternalism and Jing's personal lifestyle, gradually came to light. Shortly thereafter, an accusation meeting was held at the Jesus Family's hometown in Mazhuang, where a "Family Management Committee" was established, abolishing the paternalistic structure. In December, Jing was summoned to Shanghai for study and to account for issues relating to the Jesus Family and his personal history.

In March 1952, the chairman of the Family Management Committee wrote to Wu Yaozong, chairman of the Three-Self Reform preparatory committee, reporting the internal issues within the Jesus Family and requesting leadership for the reforms. In April, national Three-Self organizations dispatched a work team led by Shen Derong along with Zhu David and Jin Qichang to Tai'an, collaborating with local Christian and government sectors to settle in Mazhuang. After the team settled in, they held symposiums, conducted individual discussions, and established a study committee to organize believers' learning. Shen Derong and officials from the Tai'an United Front Work Department made reports on topics such as "how imperialism manipulated Christianity to invade China," "the direction of the Three-Self Reform Movement," "the People's Government's religious policies," and "the new democratic society and its future." In May, an exhibition was held to expose leadership issues involving Jing Dianying and Zuo Shunzheng. From May 27 to 29, a three-day accusation conference was held, during which Jing returned to Mazhuang from Shanghai accompanied by Cui Xianxiang of the Three-Self Reform preparatory committee to receive public criticism. While over 120 individuals signed up to accuse him, ultimately, 60 took the stage to do so.

Following the accusation meeting, it was decided to "divide the Jesus Family," restoring 138 households based on blood relations, and categorizing them by profession into agricultural households, industrial households, medical personnel households, teaching households, and returning to production households, and reallocating properties accordingly. By October 1952, the "division" was completed, officially announcing the "dissolution" of the Jesus Family. The "Mazhuang Jesus Family" was renamed "Beixinzhuang Christian Church." The work team declared that "the imperialist and reactionary feudal forces' control and exploitation of the Mazhuang Jesus Family had come to an end," and ultimately, Jing Dianying was arrested and imprisoned in Tai'an.

As the "old house" was dissolved, various "small houses" across the nation also conducted Three-Self Reform learning, went through divisions, and disbanded their original organizations. The Jesus Family became the first Christian sect to be fully "suppressed" after the establishment of the PRC.

(8) The Case of the Anti-Revolutionary Group Led by Gong Pinmei, 1955 (Mariani 2011; Liu Jianping 2012: 289-309; Xing Fuzeng 2019: 339-342; Shanghai Public Security Journal Editorial Committee 1997: 113-114)

Gong Pinmei (Ignatius Kung, 1901-2000) was consecrated as Bishop of the Suzhou Diocese in October 1949 and later became the Bishop of the Shanghai Diocese in 1950, also serving as the acting Bishop of Suzhou. In 1951, he took on the role of acting Bishop of Nanjing. On the evening of September 8, 1955, he was arrested on charges of "counter-revolutionary crimes." The government announced that it had dismantled an anti-revolutionary collective led by Gong, arresting 183 key members.

In 1950, the CCP attempted to promote a patriotic and reform movement within the Catholic Church, which faced strong opposition from the Vatican. Due to the strict hierarchical structure of the Catholic Church, it effectively resisted political interference. The greatest resistance to the CCP's work came from Chinese clerics and bishops. Statistics indicated that by early 1953, of over 2,600 Chinese bishops and priests, only 69 priests and 25 bishops or auxiliary bishops participated in the patriotic movement, with not a single official bishop among them. Due to resistance from Chinese Catholic bishops and priests, the CCP struggled to fully implement its patriotic movement within the Catholic Church. In comparison, a patriotic organization had already been established for Protestant Christianity under state support in 1951, while the proposed "Patriotic Association" for Catholicism had not materialized due to the CCP's failure to garner sufficient support from priests and bishops. In 1955, Pope Pius XII declared the suspension of Bishop Li Weiguang, the acting bishop of Nanjing who supported the patriotic movement, making him the first clergy member to be suspended by the Vatican after the establishment of the PRC.

In the eyes of the CCP, the Shanghai Diocese of the Catholic Church became a primary battleground for the struggle against Catholicism, with Gong Pinmei being a key target. According to the judgment: "Based on the series of evil activities conducted by the anti-revolutionary traitorous group led by Gong Pinmei, this court confirms that the anti-revolutionary traitorous group headed by the defendant Gong Pinmei, disguised as religious figures, colluded with imperialism, betrayed the motherland, and served as an important tool for imperialism to overthrow our people's democratic government, constituting a serious act of treason."

As the nationwide campaign against counter-revolutionaries intensified, the CCP assessed that the patriotic forces within the Catholic Church were ready, convening a representative conference for Chinese Catholics on July 15, 1957. During the meeting, the Chinese Catholic Patriotic Association (later renamed the National Catholic Patriotic Association) was established with government support.

In March 1960, the Shanghai Intermediate People's Court sentenced Gong Pinmei to life imprisonment and stripped him of political rights for life. In May 1985, Gong confessed his crimes in prison and expressed his willingness to abide by the law and contribute as a patriotic believer for the rest of his life. On July 3, the Shanghai Higher People's Court announced his conditional release. On January 5, 1988, the court decided to waive the remainder of his probation and restore his political rights. On May 11, he was approved to go to the United States for medical treatment.

In 1991, Pope John Paul II declared Gong a cardinal "in pectoral," appointed in 1979. During his time in the U.S., Gong established the "Cardinal Kung Foundation" to focus on the situation of the underground Catholic Church in China.

Gong passed away on March 12, 2000, in the United States, at the age of 98.

(9) The Case of the Anti-Revolutionary Group Led by Wang Mingdao, 1955 (Xing Fuzeng 2010: 97-147; Liu Jianping 2012: 235-288)

In 1955, leftist literary theorist Hu Feng was criticized for his independent views and soon was defined by the central government as part of a "struggle against the Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group." This national campaign against Hu Feng further evolved into a sweep against counter-revolutionaries across the country. On July 1 and August 25, the central authorities successively issued directives on launching struggles to purge hidden counter-revolutionaries.

Amidst the anti-counter-revolutionary climate, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Bureau convened over 500 individuals from the Christian community on June 14 to expose the "Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group," demanding that the religious community reveal counter-revolutionaries. Several Christian individuals spoke out, emphasizing the need to be vigilant against hidden counter-revolutionaries within Christianity. On June 20, national and Shanghai Three-Self Movement committees held a symposium to denounce the "Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group."

On July 11, "Tianfeng" published an editorial titled "Strengthen Unity, Distinguish Right from Wrong," which for the first time specifically criticized Wang Mingdao, a prominent figure in Beijing who vocally opposed the Three-Self Movement. His errors included: (1) fundamentally denying that Western imperialism used Chinese Christianity, claiming that the ideological poison of imperialism was the "truth" of the Bible; (2) attempting to incite a conflict of faith between the "fundamentalists" and "modernists" within Christianity to undermine and cancel the foundation of the patriotic movement; (3) promoting himself as a servant of God while condemning Christians who participated in the Three-Self Movement as “spiritually degenerate” and “unbelievers.” Therefore, in light of Wang Mingdao's "pretense of faith as an excuse for sabotaging anti-imperialist patriotism," the editorial called on patriotic and devout Christians to "draw a clear line between themselves and Mr. Wang Mingdao based on the political principles of anti-imperialism and patriotism," and to actively engage in the struggle.

He described the counter-revolutionaries in the religious community as primarily "appearing with piety and zeal," while in reality, they were merely "disguised under the cloak of religion" to deceive the faithful. On August 15, "Tianfeng" published a brief commentary titled "Expose Wang Mingdao's Reactionary Words and Actions," calling for "a resolute exposure of Wang Mingdao's reactionary words and actions, immediately drawing a line from him." Following Wang's arrest, the Three-Self organizations and churches across the country launched denunciation campaigns against the "Wang Mingdao counter-revolutionary group" between August and November, with "Tianfeng" continuously publishing articles criticizing Wang. However, because he did not fulfill his commitment, on April 29, 1958, the government arrested Wang and his wife again. In April 1961, the Beijing People's Procuratorate officially charged them under the "Regulations on Punishing Counter-Revolutionaries." The case of the "Wang Mingdao Counter-Revolutionary Group" became a focal point of the anti-revolutionary struggle within the Christian community, marking the government's target at those hardliners who refused to participate in the Three-Self Movement based on differing beliefs. At that time, aside from Wang Mingdao, the Christian meeting places were among the most organized and populous factions against the Three-Self Movement, undoubtedly becoming the next target of the government.

Starting in mid-January 1956, the authorities in Hunan (including Changsha, Hengyang, Zhuzhou, Changde, Cili, Xiangtan, etc.), Guangdong (Guangzhou), Zhejiang (Hangzhou, Ningbo, Wenzhou, Pingyang, Xiaoshan, Ninghai, Ruian, Huangyan, Jinhua, Pan'an, Shaoxing, Jiaxing, Jiashan, etc.), and Fujian (Fuzhou, Xiamen, Quanzhou, Jinjiang, Putian, Fuzhou, Minhou, Fuding, Gutian, Liancheng, Zhangpu, etc.) began to arrest leaders of Christian meeting places on counter-revolutionary charges. Following the arrests, widespread denunciation meetings were held, calling on the Christian community to expose hidden counter-revolutionaries within their meeting places, with participants in various locations stepping forward to take part in the denouncements. Thus, a nationwide campaign to rectify meeting places was launched.

(11) The National Five Major Rightists in Christianity, 1957 (Xing Fuzeng 2017: 21-35)

In June 1957, the rapid anti-rightist campaign unfolded nationwide, expanding progressively. In November, the Central Committee approved the "Opinions of the United Front Work Department on Conducting Socialist Education in the Han Nationality Religious Community." The central decision aimed to initiate socialist education directed at religious practitioners within the Han nationality religious community (especially Catholicism and Christianity). The document clearly stated that during the learning process, "appropriate exposure and criticism of certain rightists" should occur, applying the standards established by Mao Zedong and the Central Committee for identifying "rightists" to the religious community.

To initiate the anti-rightist struggle within Christianity, from October 28 to December 4, 1957, the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee held its tenth (expanded) meeting in Beijing, focusing on exposing and refuting a minority of bourgeois rightists within Christianity, who "disguise themselves as religious figures and utilize deceitful tactics, using the excuses of 'for faith' and 'for the church' to undermine the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, disseminate reactionary rhetoric, deceive and incite well-meaning believers, and aim to achieve their anti-Party and anti-socialist objectives." After several days of group discussions, the meeting ultimately "uncovered" five rightists in the Christian community disguised under the guise of religion: Liu Lingjiu (president and editor-in-chief of "Tianjia"), Zhou Qingze (pastor of the Kulangsu Church of the Chinese Christian Church), Dong Hong'en (pastor of the Shanghai Christian Church), Fan Aishi (chairman of the Ningbo Three-Self Association and chairman of the Chinese Methodist Church's Ningbo Diocese), and Zhou Fuqing (elder of the Shanghai Lingliang Church). According to a report from the Central United Front Work Department, these five were "notable for their reactionary words and deeds, with minimal religious influence, and were deemed manageable for a thorough struggle against."

In addition to the aforementioned five "big rightists" at the national level, Chen Chonggui also faced serious criticism during this meeting. Wu Yaozong, together with eight other church leaders (including Chen Jianzhen, Jiang Chuan, Wu Yifang, Ding Yuzhang, Zhao Zichen, Tu Yuqing, Liu Liangmo, and Shi Ruzhang), jointly stated that Chen Chonggui's remarks in the Political Consultative Conference were "anti-Party slander," and were "using the pretext of speaking for the church to attack the Party and socialism, inciting Christians to oppose the Party, government, and the people of the nation, slandering the Soviet Union, promoting admiration for the US, and disparaging the Three-Self Patriotic Movement while inciting discord among patriotically united Christians from various sects." They further indicated that Chen's statements had caused adverse consequences in churches across the country and had become a banner for other rightists in Christianity (such as Liu Lingjiu and Ma Xingge) to "attack the Party." Due to Chen's reluctance to review and admit mistakes after the meeting, he was ultimately declared a rightist in the spring of 1958, but without public announcement in the newspapers.

The aforementioned meeting merely marked the beginning of the anti-rightist struggle within Christianity. Following this, provinces and cities convened meetings or symposiums under the guise of socialist education learning, further identifying more rightists.

From February 8 to 19, 1956, an exhibition showcasing evidence against the Ni Tuosheng counter-revolutionary group was held, with nearly 10,000 believers in Shanghai attending. The audience for the exhibition was expanded to include representatives from other meeting places from the 21st to the 24th. Starting in February 1956, multiple issues of "Tianfeng" reported in detail on this theme and reprinted various denunciation documents. On February 22, a large meeting was held at the Nanyang Road meeting place to denounce the crimes of the Ni Tuosheng counter-revolutionary group, with over 3,000 participants, including representatives from Nanjing, Changsha, and Xuzhou.

On June 21, 1956, the Shanghai Higher People's Court held a trial for the Ni Tuosheng anti-revolutionary case. Ni confessed to the charges in court and was ultimately sentenced to 15 years of hard labor and deprivation of political rights for five years. In October 1958, the Shanghai Higher People's Court sentenced Li Yuanru and Wang Peizhen to 15 years in prison, while Zhang Yuzhi received a 12-year sentence. One of the charges against them was that after Ni's arrest, they "intensely engaged in counter-revolutionary activities." Others captured from various meeting places also faced sentencing during 1958.

It is noteworthy that Ni Tuosheng had been arrested by the government as early as April 1952. The struggles against the Three-Self Movement across various meeting places from 1953 to 1955, conducted under the pretense of faith, were actually unrelated to Ni Tuosheng, who was incapable of leading the opposition. The anti-revolutionary group case in 1956 continued to be associated with "Ni Tuosheng" because of his significant role within the meeting places, leading the government to perceive him as the head of the anti-revolutionary faction. Furthermore, the CCP sought to use the various charges against Ni, particularly concerning moral issues, as key evidence to strike at the meeting places. The high-profile trial of Ni Tuosheng (in contrast to Wang Mingdao) and the public exposure of his alleged crimes certainly imposed a severe, even fatal, impact on the meeting places.

(11) The National Five Major Rightists in Christianity, 1957 (Xing Fuzeng 2017: 21-35)

In June 1957, the rapid anti-rightist campaign unfolded nationwide, gradually expanding its impact. In November, the Central Committee approved the "Opinions of the United Front Work Department on Conducting Socialist Education in the Han Nationality Religious Community." The government aimed to initiate socialist education directed at religious practitioners within the Han nationality religious community (especially Catholicism and Christianity). The document explicitly stated that during the learning process, "appropriate exposure and criticism of certain rightists" should take place, applying standards established by Mao Zedong and the Central Committee for identifying "rightists" to the religious community.

To initiate the anti-rightist struggle within Christianity, the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee held its tenth (expanded) meeting in Beijing from October 28 to December 4, 1957. The meeting focused on exposing and refuting a minority of bourgeois rightists within Christianity who "disguise themselves as religious figures, employing two-faced tactics, using the excuses of 'for faith' and 'for the church' to undermine the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, spread reactionary rhetoric, deceive and incite well-meaning believers, and aim to achieve anti-Party and anti-socialist objectives." After several days of group discussions, the meeting ultimately "uncovered" five rightists disguised under the guise of religion: Liu Lingjiu (president and editor-in-chief of "Tianjia"), Zhou Qingze (pastor of the Kulangsu Church of the Chinese Christian Church), Dong Hong'en (pastor of the Shanghai Christian Church), Fan Aishi (chairman of the Ningbo Three-Self Association and chairman of the Chinese Methodist Church's Ningbo Diocese), and Zhou Fuqing (elder of the Shanghai Lingliang Church). According to a report from the Central United Front Work Department, these five individuals were "notable for their reactionary words and deeds, with minimal religious influence, and were deemed manageable for a thorough struggle against."

In addition to the aforementioned five "major rightists" at the national level, Chen Chonggui was also severely criticized at this meeting. Wu Yaozong, along with eight other church leaders (including Chen Jianzhen, Jiang Chuan, Wu Yifang, Ding Yuzhang, Zhao Zichen, Tu Yuqing, Liu Liangmo, and Shi Ruzhang), jointly stated that Chen Chonggui's remarks in the Political Consultative Conference were "anti-Party slander," and were "using the pretext of speaking for the church to attack the Party and socialism, inciting Christians to oppose the Party, government, and the people of the nation, slandering the Soviet Union, promoting admiration for the US, and disparaging the Three-Self Patriotic Movement while inciting discord among patriotically united Christians from various sects." They also indicated that Chen's statements led to adverse consequences in churches across the country and became a banner for other rightists in Christianity (like Liu Lingjiu and Ma Xingge) to "attack the Party." (14) The Crackdown on the True Jesus Church, 1958-1959 (Inouye 2018: 208-210; Deng Zhaoming 1993: 2-19)

During the Great Leap Forward, the True Jesus Church became a primary target of repression and impact. Founded in 1917, the True Jesus Church is an indigenous Christian denomination in China. In March 1951, the church held its 12th General Assembly, where it developed a reform program expressing its commitment to strengthening political education and expanding support for the Korean War. In April, Wei Yisa, as president, attended a conference organized by the State Council on handling Christian groups receiving subsidies from the US and co-signed a joint declaration. In early 1952, Wei expressed his intent to participate in the Three-Self Movement during a self-criticism session. By March 1953, the headquarters sent a circular to various regions promoting the Three-Self reform declaration, with over 13,000 believers signing by September. In April, the True Jesus Church's national governing body established a preparatory committee for the Three-Self Reform Movement, relocating its headquarters from Beijing to Wuhan, with Jiang Yaohe and Liu Jun'an elected as chairman and vice-chairman, respectively. During this period, Wei Yisa faced criticism.

In May 1958, Zhang Zhiqi, Deputy Minister of the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee, mentioned the need to eliminate the True Jesus Church at a professional religious work meeting. This proposal was immediately disseminated to provinces. On June 5, at a religious work meeting in Sichuan Province, related issues were discussed, and localities were required to "gather information." In January 1959, Sichuan Province decided to begin the crackdown in the concentration areas of believers in Chongqing, with a unified leadership from the Party Committee. A joint task force comprising the United Front, public security, and religious affairs departments was formed to establish collaborative groups at the county level to conduct unified suppression operations. By the end of May, it was basically reported that the True Jesus Church had been "dealt a severe blow and discredited, achieving the intended objective," leading to "the struggle winding down." For example, in Chongqing, out of 135 key members from the regional church, 82 were dealt with (16 arrested, 34 under control, 2 subjected to labor education, 10 denounced, and 20 subjected to public reviews and debates).

The national Three-Self Movement also began synchronized criticism of the True Jesus Church in May 1958. "Tianfeng" published reports on the "illegal and criminal activities" of the True Jesus Church in Hubei, with special emphasis in "editorial notes" on the "counter-Party, anti-socialist, and anti-people's evil activities," asserting that "no leniency should be tolerated." In June, "Tianfeng" again targeted the True Jesus Church, following up the report about illegal activities in the Hanyang branch with a short commentary and reprinting an article from the committee of the Hubei Christian Socialist Education Learning Association denouncing the True Jesus Church. In July, "Tianfeng" published letters from readers of the Beijing True Jesus Church, calling for a denunciation of the reactionary group led by Jiang Yaohe.

During the Great Leap Forward, only the True Jesus Church was specifically named and subjected to unprecedented political liquidation, possibly due to its tightly organized national structure and strong "charismatic" tradition. Simultaneously, the True Jesus Church's "exclusive" view of the church saw itself as the only "true" church, and although it participated in the Three-Self reforms, it was viewed as a key obstacle to unity and thus faced comprehensive liquidation. Following combined worship services, denominational organizations ceased to operate, and congregations were required to merge with other sects. However, some scholars noted that the True Jesus Church continued to develop underground.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Criticism of the Spiritual Faction by the Accusation Movement and the Response of the Spiritual Faction 1952 (邢福增 2012: 139-140; 2005: 116-117, 196-201)

During the Christian Accusation Movement from 1951 to 1952, the Spiritual Faction (i.e., the Fundamentalists) held a “detached” or “neutral” stance on political issues, which was criticized as the toxin of imperialism’s “super politics.” Although the Central Committee pointed out the need to distinguish between “religion” and “politics,” mass movements often exhibited extremism and amplification, and under the politically “pan-politicized” circumstances, criticism of the Spiritual Faction’s “super politics” easily turned into attacks on the Spiritual Faction itself. One of the most representative examples was the publication of several articles targeting the Spiritual Faction in the July 1952 issue of Chengdu’s《新希望》, directly denouncing the “Spiritual Faction” as an “extremely reactionary sect” and labeling them as “disgraceful” within Christianity, as well as characterizing the Spiritual Faction as “superstitious.” However, this kind of broad struggle and wholesale denial of the “Spiritual Faction” was clearly inconsistent with the lines and strategies of the CCP and reformists, fearing it would provide an excuse for anti-Sanzi forces, criticizing the Sanzi reform as an eradication of faith. 李儲文 from the national Sanzi preparatory committee specifically wrote to correct this view, stating that when exposing imperialism’s use of the Spiritual Faction, one should not treat the “Spiritual Faction” as an object for exposure and attack; the primary obstacle to the Sanzi reform was imperialism, not the “Spiritual Faction,” and the Sanzi movement did not demand changes to the “Spiritual Faction’s” faith. The incident resulted in significant backlash from the Spiritual Faction, which was detrimental to unity. Ultimately,《新希望》was ordered to cease publication nationwide. During the second National Religious Work Conference held in Beijing from November 27 to December 16, 1953, the host, Vice Chairman of the Ministry of Culture and Education, 陸定一, reiterated that “the target of the entire struggle is reactionaries, not what is referred to as the Spiritual Faction.”

In 1953, the director of the East China Religious Affairs Office, 羅竹風, privately met with some individuals from the Spiritual Faction to encourage their participation in the Sanzi reform. 羅 emphasized that the Sanzi reform was an anti-imperialist patriotic political movement and an indispensable segment of the nationwide anti-imperialist patriotic movement. The government only requested church personnel to be patriotic and did not impose any other requirements on their faith. In the name of anti-imperialism and patriotism, the party-state urged the Spiritual Faction to participate in the Sanzi reform, leading to a few churches in Shanghai (南陽路聚會處, 靈糧堂, and 烏魯木齊北路禮拜堂) holding irregular communication meetings at the end of 1953 to discuss strategies.

During the communication process, the three churches gradually divided into two opinions regarding the Sanzi movement. The first opinion held that autonomy, self-support, and self-propagation were appropriate, and there was no issue with patriotism as long as faith could be maintained; the faith position was the baseline for participating in the Sanzi. Proponents of this view included: 任鍾祥, 周福慶, 陸傳芳, and 楊紹唐, while 唐守臨 leaned toward this but had a wavering stance. The second opinion was more aligned with 王明道’s position, believing that believers and non-believers should not be unequally yoked, opposing participation in the Sanzi. Those holding this position included: 張愚之, 藍志一, 田雅各, and 徐天民. The consensus between the two views was to oppose interference in faith, with the main divergence being whether to participate in the Sanzi. Ultimately, those holding the first opinion participated in the national Christian conference convened by the national Sanzi preparatory committee in 1954.

  1. Opinions on Religious Policy from the Christian Political Consultative Conference during the Mingfang Movement 1957 (吳耀宗1957: 2-5; 陳崇桂1957)

In March 1957, the second national committee’s third plenary session of the Political Consultative Conference was held, with the atmosphere of “great criticism and great debate” evident in the representatives’ speeches. The chairman and vice-chairman of the Christian Sanzi Patriotic Movement Committee, 吳耀宗 and 陳崇桂, also attended this meeting as national Political Consultative Conference members. 吳耀宗, in his speech, specifically expressed his views on current religious policy issues. He pointed out that there were both “tangible” and “intangible” problems in the implementation of religious policy. The so-called “tangible” problems referred to the suspension of gatherings ordered in some regions of the country during land reform, where worship has still not been allowed to resume, and in some areas, local officials forcibly occupied church properties and furniture, even threatening believers not to attend worship. Some officials also formulated a series of prohibitions against churches, forbidding them from collecting offerings, constructing worship halls, and developing believers, among other restrictions, and sometimes church leaders faced obstacles from officials when visiting congregations in different regions.Regarding the “intangible” issues, there are mainly two unhealthy phenomena: first, some agencies and schools adopt discriminatory attitudes toward Christians; regardless of how well they perform in work or study, their religious faith is seen as a flaw, and anyone who believes in Christianity is viewed as “problematic”; second, many books published across various regions that criticize religion are “one-sided, subjective, not factual, and dismissive,” exhibiting an unfair view of religion while ignoring the patriotic and peace-loving movements within the Chinese religious community.

To better implement the policy of religious freedom, 吳耀宗 urged relevant central departments and people’s committees at all levels to strengthen their attention to religious work and to provide more systematic educational propaganda on the policy of religious faith freedom. Particularly at the grassroots level, it is essential to enhance human resources and education. He also suggested that in addition to constitutional provisions, more supplementary articles related to implementing religious policies should be formulated. This is because those executing the policies can interpret the basic articles differently, and the establishment of supplementary articles can at least address existing problems. During the legislative process, it is crucial to consider the opinions of religious personnel.

陳崇桂’s remarks mainly included three points: (1) He pointed out that the issue of whether to believe in God or not, whether there is a God or not, reflects an internal ideological contradiction among the people rather than a confrontational contradiction. Therefore, during the “debate,” both sides must remain calm and cannot adopt a coarse or insulting attitude. However, he learned that some church buildings had been occupied by the government and turned into places for raising livestock. Furthermore, a senior official at the inauguration of an iron bridge stressed that this was “created by human effort, not the work of any God.” This official stated, “Those of you who believe in God should throw your God into the muck.” Regarding such near-insulting behaviors towards religion, he reported as a Political Consultative Conference member that he observed several provinces regarding church development, thoroughly understanding the situation of forcibly occupying worship places and insulting and slandering religion. He was convinced that this was not a manifestation of the “respect” that religious faith freedom should entail; (2) 陳崇桂 touched upon the evaluation of the Sanzi Patriotic Movement. He pointed out that the Sanzi movement was initiated under the principle of a united front to unite Christians nationwide. The entire movement has made certain achievements in calling on Christians to love their country, oppose imperialism, and strive for world peace. However, while advancing the Sanzi movement, the leaders “also made numerous mistakes.” He even self-criticized, saying, “I, an old man, have made the most mistakes”; (3) Concerning 吳耀宗’s mention of the numerous books criticizing religion published domestically as an “intangible problem,” 陳崇桂 indicated that these books criticizing Christianity, apart from some being translations from the Soviet Union, are materials critiquing Christianity from our own theological professors in Christian seminaries, who use “modernist” methods to “criticize the Bible.”陳 did not express agreement or disagreement with such modernist practices but pointed out that even “conservative” Christians are quite willing to accept Marx’s critiques. This is because Marx’s “initial belief and later disbelief” stem from honesty. “He does not oppose Christianity itself; rather, he opposes Christians who do not act according to the Bible.” Here, he reiterated that self-propagation does not mean “we no longer speak of the salvation of Jesus on the cross; we still talk about the doctrine of redemption through the precious blood.” Self-propagation simply further “enriches the content of our faith.”

  1. Reflection on the Discrimination Faced by Christians during the Mingfang Period 1956-57 (邢福增 1996: 104-107)

Before the rectification movement began, the atmosphere of discussion within Christianity was already influenced by a relaxed political environment. For example, at the fourth meeting of the first people’s congress of Shanghai in August 1956, seven Christian representatives (江長川, 劉良模, 竺規身, 羅冠宗, 陳善祥, 施如璋, and 謝永欽) made a joint statement on the implementation of religious policies. They pointed out that there still existed widespread discrimination against believers in society; some comrades still regarded Christianity as a reactionary sect and a counter-revolutionary group, and that those who believe in religion must be politically backward, often using faith as material for ridicule. Furthermore, Christians were also forced to abandon their faith when they joined the party or youth league, causing them significant distress. All of this reflects a lack of respect for religious faith in society.

During the Mingfang period, Christian representatives in local political consultative conferences and people’s congresses also highlighted the deviations in local religious policies at provincial-level conferences, and some Christians expressed their views through newspapers and symposiums. Thus, a movement of debate within the Christian community unfolded.In April 1957, Jiangsu Province Political Consultative Conference members 邵鏡三, 諸培恩, 雷智伯, 楊鏡秋, and attending representative 誠質怡 jointly spoke at a Jiangsu Provincial Political Consultative Conference meeting, pointing out that some local officials not only disrespected religious beliefs but also exhibited discriminatory behavior. For instance, in Dongshan Town, Zhenze County, Suzhou, the deputy town head criticized believers celebrating Christmas as superstitious and backward. Additionally, township organizations and cooperatives forcibly occupied churches.

In May 1957, Liaoning Province Political Consultative Conference members 孫鵬翕 and 段大經 made a joint statement at the Liaoning Provincial Political Consultative Conference meeting, noting that some grassroots officials lacked understanding of religious policy, discriminated against believers, and even used churches as theaters.

In Beijing, 李克 from the Chinese Christian Church reported that a school forced a Christian to abandon their faith to join a youth organization. Moreover, young believers were not trusted or utilized by organizations due to their faith.

河北省政協委員馬星格 (chairman of the Sanzi Association in Baoding) pointed out at the plenary meeting of the Hebei Provincial Political Consultative Conference that there was widespread discrimination against believers in society.

In June 1957, a symposium involving representatives from Christianity, Catholicism, Buddhism, and Islam was held in Beijing, where participants reflected on discrimination due to religious beliefs, noting that the religious affairs bureau was also unable to assist in resolving the issues.

  1. Response to Books Criticizing Religion 1957 (姜蒙光1957: 18-19)

In May 1957,《天風》published an article by 姜蒙光 titled “Reflections After Reading Some Books Criticizing Religion.” 姜蒙光, who is responsible for the Christian spiritual service group of the守真堂, expressed his opinions regarding the large number of books criticizing religion issued by Xinhua Bookstore in recent years. He pointed out three issues: (1) Dogmatism: Many of these books are translated works citing examples from the church during the Russian Tsarist period, neglecting the differences between China and Russia, leading to a blanket judgment that “all modern religions… require the populace to submit absolutely to oppression…” and that “the struggle against religious superstition is necessary.” 姜 noted that while critiquing religion based on historical facts is valid, it is unscientific to make generalized statements “without differentiation or explanation”; (2) Insufficient philosophical critique: Most of these books criticize religion from a political perspective, lacking a philosophical dimension, which prevents believers from using “persuasive means rather than dismissive methods that ignore facts,” the latter only increases Christians’ concerns; (3) Adverse effects on the execution and implementation of religious policy: The new China grants people freedom of thought, and the existence of different religious beliefs among the populace is an internal contradiction. However, criticism of religion should not insult or demean others’ beliefs. Finally, 姜 suggested that future publications criticizing religion should be reviewed by the national religious affairs department, fostering mutual respect in the context of “a hundred flowers blooming, a hundred schools contending.”

  1. Are Pastors an Exploitative Class? 1958 (天風1958a, 1958b, 1958c; 吳耀宗 et al. 1959)

In August 1958,《天風》specially introduced a discussion topic: “What Class Do Pastors Belong To?” This discussion was raised by a reader identified as “新恩,” referencing the discussions on “exploitative classes” and “working classes” from the work report of the Second Plenary Session of the Eighth National Congress of the Communist Party of China, directly questioning: “What class do pastors actually belong to?” He pointed out the existence of two differing positions: some opinions assert that pastors are an exploitative class, claiming the “exploitative nature” of pastors refers to the “ideological consciousness of pastors belonging to the category of exploitative class ideology,” suggesting that there is even “a similar non-labor income nature to the bourgeoisie.” Conversely, others argue that pastors belong to the working class, reasoned that their economic source is “the voluntary contributions of the working people,” indicating they do not exploit. Many pastors also come from “working-class families” and are not part of the “bourgeois intellectual class.”

Subsequent issues of《天風》featured related discussions, revealing that the support for the view of pastors as an exploitative class became nearly mainstream opinion. Colleagues from Zhejiang Province noted that the vast majority of pastors’ backgrounds, educational levels, and ideological sentiments belonged to the “nature of the bourgeoisie.” Even those from a working-class background were observed to “betray their original class, becoming dependent on imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism.” Similarly, 南京三自幹部徐如雷 reached the same conclusion.A worker from Shanghai’s St. Paul Church used the example of the church’s seven staff members (four pastors and missionaries, two workers, and one administrator) to illustrate that their combined monthly salary was 510 yuan, averaging 73 yuan per person. He retorted, “A pastor (or missionary) only preaches once or twice a month, spending no more than one or two hours, yet receives over 70 yuan in salary for a month. This calculation is astonishing.” His article was titled, “Aren’t they parasites in society?” which clearly indicated his strong position. He concluded: “Such churches and pastoral staff are filled with the toxins of imperialism and bourgeois thought, living a life of unearned enjoyment without labor. If they do not undergo socialist education, they will likely be abandoned by the masses and eliminated by society in the future. We must thoroughly change our exploitative class stance, actively participate in labor production, and gradually train ourselves through labor to become self-sufficient workers.”

Aside from one article expressing an opposing view, the discussions in《天風》had undoubtedly predetermined a stance aimed at preparing for the transformation of pastoral personnel. As 吳耀宗 and six other Christian figures pointed out at the 1959 National Political Consultative Conference: “We see that the Chinese Christian church is built on the basis of the exploitative class, serving the exploitative class, with many churches in various cities owning numerous properties, which in itself is part of the exploitative class.” The issue of whether pastors belong to the exploitative class was less about discussion and more about a predetermined fact under a left-leaning situation. As members of the exploitative class, pastors needed to undergo transformation, actively participate in agricultural labor production, or shift to factories. In fact, amid the Great Leap Forward, the comprehensive transformation of religion had become an irreversible trend. Targeting religious personnel (the exploitative class) for education and reform, and fully weakening the influence and development of religion, including fundamentally transforming and dismantling religious institutions, had already become a focus of the religious work “Great Leap Forward.”

  1. Critique of the Toxins in《荒漠甘泉》1959 (邢福增 2005: 201-202; 鄧肇明 1997: 107-116)

In May 1959,《天風》launched a critique of《荒漠甘泉》, focusing on revealing the imperialist toxins hidden within so-called “spiritual” works.《荒漠甘泉》is a daily devotional book, originally authored by Mrs. Charles E. Cowman, featuring reflections on daily Bible verses. Published in December 1939 by the Shanghai Gospel Bookstore, it included works from several coworkers at the Shanghai meeting place. From 1939 to 1950, it was reprinted almost every year and enjoyed great popularity among readers.

Initially,《天風》published a letter from 李興祥, stating that after undergoing socialist educational learning, he had engaged in deep self-transformation. He notably mentioned that《荒漠甘泉》had previously provided him with much spiritual comfort, but after his studies, he began to discover numerous problems, such as whether the book’s teachings on “suffering for the Lord” rationalized the actions of aggressors. Could this not be seen as “the toxin of imperialism”? In response, the editor of《天風》decided to initiate a discussion titled “Does《荒漠甘泉》Contain Toxins?”

Related individuals from the Shanghai Gospel Bookstore (which had since ceased operations), such as 任鍾祥, 唐守臨 (唐醒), and 左弗如, subsequently wrote critiques and reflections, acknowledging that《荒漠甘泉》had been exploited by counter-revolutionaries, spreading the notion of enduring suffering under the pretense of praising darkness, serving as a toxic ideological substance for the exploitative class. Churches in Shanghai and Wuhan even held seminars for discussion.

Notably, during this critique, a few believers submitted articles to《天風》expressing alternative viewpoints. For instance, 王奉義 stated that《荒漠甘泉》is a spiritual book that discusses spiritual matters, asserting that “its value cannot be measured by its suitability for a particular era.” More importantly, he noted that even within a socialist system, “wars, poverty, hunger, and other man-made sufferings may have diminished somewhat, yet people still inevitably face illnesses, death, and other misfortunes,” adding that “the spiritual anguish and darkness are often more difficult to endure.” In other words,《荒漠甘泉》provides comfort derived from God’s word and strengthens believers’ faith, thus holding significant meaning. 楊園 pointedly questioned the core of the issue, asking, “To critique a book written in the past using today’s standards, labeling it as ‘toxic,’ seems somewhat rigid. Is everything ‘incompatible with society’ considered toxins? This is difficult to convince people.”王 and 楊 both insisted that《荒漠甘泉》has “spiritual” value and should not be denied based on the standards of a particular era, even if it is dismissed as the “toxin” of imperialism. In total, this discussion saw《天風》publish 30 articles; aside from one article discussing differing opinions at a seminar in Shanghai, there were 23 articles supporting the “toxin” argument and 6 opposing it. Overall, the critical voices remained mainstream.

In December 1959, the《天風》editorial team decided to conclude this critique with an article by “新堅”: “The ideological shadows that imperialism has long spread among our believers cannot be removed in a day. To thoroughly oppose imperialism, we must uproot the old roots of imperialism from an ideological standpoint. Therefore, eradication of the residual imperialist ideological toxins has become an urgent issue. This discussion was launched to analyze specifically what constitutes imperialist toxins through the lens of《荒漠甘泉》, thereby allowing for a better critique of these toxins.”

The critique of《荒漠甘泉》was clearly orchestrated, aiming to launch an offensive in the realm of religious thought, demonstrating that the toxins of imperialist ideology are pervasive and must be accounted for beneath the spiritual facade. Even in the extremely leftist context, six believers still dared to publicly express dissenting opinions, reflecting that a minority of believers remained dissatisfied with the “pan-politicization” of the faith domain.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. Sino-Soviet Relations (Nakajima 1987: 262-270, 280-282; Whiting 1987: 478-538; 陳永發2021: 543-551)

In the 1950s, Sino-Soviet relations underwent dramatic changes from cooperation to intensified contradictions, which had a profound impact on the international communist movement and the political landscape in Asia.

In July 1949, 毛澤東 proposed the policy of “leaning towards the Soviet Union.” In December, 毛澤東 led a delegation to Moscow, returning to Beijing in early March. During this time, China and the Soviet Union signed the Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Alliance and Mutual Assistance on February 14, 1950. This treaty established a close military and economic cooperation relationship between the two countries, with the Soviet Union promising to provide China with technical and economic assistance to help China in its industrialization efforts. Although 毛 was not completely satisfied with the results of this trip, the Soviet Union did provide economic aid to the new China. Meanwhile, Soviet experts came to China in large numbers, while China followed the Soviet line in diplomacy and ideology. The Korean War also enhanced the strategic cooperation between China and the Soviet Union.

However, with the death of 斯大林 (Joseph Stalin) in 1953 and the rise of 赫魯曉夫 (Nikita S. Khrushchev), cracks gradually appeared in Sino-Soviet relations. 赫魯曉夫 implemented a de-Stalinization policy, emphasizing peaceful coexistence and easing relations with the West, which conflicted with the revolutionary approach upheld by the Chinese leader 毛澤東. In 1956, at the 20th Congress of the Soviet Communist Party, 赫魯曉夫 criticized the cult of personality surrounding 斯大林, which 毛澤東 believed weakened the leadership core of the communist movement. The ideological divide between the two countries gradually widened.

Starting in 1958, Sino-Soviet contradictions further escalated. 毛澤東 expressed dissatisfaction with 赫魯曉夫’s “three peaceful policies” (peaceful coexistence, peaceful competition, peaceful transition), criticizing his overly compromising attitude toward the United States and Western countries; at the same time, he viewed 赫魯曉夫’s public criticism of the Great Leap Forward as interference in China’s internal affairs. The Chinese Communist Party’s claim that the Great Leap Forward provided a shortcut to communism undoubtedly challenged the Soviet Union’s leadership position within the socialist camp. Additionally, China expressed dissatisfaction with the unequal conditions in economic cooperation and the Soviet attitude towards China. In the same year, the Soviet Union withdrew its aid plan for China’s nuclear submarine construction, symbolizing the gradual breakdown of cooperation between the two sides.

In 1960, the Soviet Union unilaterally withdrew all its experts from China and terminated economic and technical assistance, further deteriorating relations between the two countries. In 1962, during the Sino-Indian border conflict, the Soviet Union leaned towards supporting India, further intensifying the Sino-Soviet contradictions. By the end of the 1950s, Sino-Soviet relations had transitioned from initial cooperation to complete opposition, officially splitting in the early 1960s.

The changes in Sino-Soviet relations had far-reaching implications for the international political landscape. Firstly, it led to a split in the international communist movement, forming a “revisionist” camp led by the Soviet Union and a “revolutionary left” camp led by China. This resulted in a loss of unified action for global communist forces during the Cold War. Secondly, the political landscape in Asia also changed as a result. The Sino-Soviet rift forced China to seek cooperation with other countries, such as China’s gradual engagement with the United States in the late 1960s. Finally, the Sino-Soviet antagonism also intensified military tensions in border areas and prompted both sides to invest substantial resources in an arms race.

In summary, the changes in Sino-Soviet relations during the 1950s not only altered the interaction patterns between the two countries but also profoundly affected the global political landscape during the Cold War and the future development of the international communist movement.

  1. The Korean War and the Cold War (Nakajima 1987: 270-280; Jun 2010: 221-243; Stueck 2010: 266-287; 陳永發2021: 551-578)

In the 1950s, China’s involvement in the Korean War (朝鮮戰爭) was a crucial turning point that led to the comprehensive deterioration of Sino-American relations and the formation of the Cold War landscape in Asia, having a profound impact on regional and even global politics.

The Korean War broke out in June 1950, with North Korea attacking South Korea with the support of the Soviet Union, swiftly occupying most of South Korean territory. The United Nations Security Council passed a resolution organizing a multinational force led by the United States to intervene. In the early stages of the war, North Korea was steadily defeated under the counterattack from the allied forces, with the conflict gradually approaching the Chinese border, threatening the security of northeast China. The newly established Chinese Communist regime, while consolidating its domestic rule, had to confront military threats from abroad. The Korean War not only increased the danger of a “U.S. imperialist” attack but also signified the potential for the Kuomintang to take this opportunity to return to the mainland.At the one-year anniversary of the founding of the People’s Republic of China, the Chinese Communist Party (中共) had no choice but to decide to send the “Chinese People’s Volunteer Army” to Korea to stop the advance of the American-led United Nations forces. A strong sense of survival crisis prompted the new government to launch a nationwide “Resist America and Aid Korea” movement, and China thus found itself back in a state of emergency war.

In October 1950, Chinese troops entered Korea to fight and successfully halted the offensive of the allied forces, stabilizing the front line near the 38th parallel by early 1951. The war ultimately ended in 1953 with an armistice agreement, returning the Korean Peninsula to its pre-war divided state.

The Korean War marked the starting point of a comprehensive opposition between new China and the United States. Prior to this, the United States had maintained a wait-and-see attitude towards the governance of the Chinese Communist Party; however, China’s military intervention in Korea and direct confrontation with American forces led the U.S. to view new China as one of its main enemies. The U.S. quickly formulated a comprehensive blockade policy against China, imposing economic, technical, and trade embargoes and rallying Asian countries to collectively contain China.

After the Korean War, the United States established an “anti-communism” policy in Asia, strengthening military support for Taiwan, even deploying the Seventh Fleet to the Taiwan Strait, which provided breathing room for the Kuomintang regime in Taiwan. The U.S. also further enhanced military alliances with Japan, South Korea, and Southeast Asian countries, creating an “anti-communist encirclement” around China. The Korean War laid the foundation for the adversarial relationship between China and the U.S. during the Cold War, which did not begin to ease until the Sino-Soviet rift in the early 1970s.

China’s intervention in the Korean War played a significant role in the formation of the Cold War structure in Asia. Firstly, the Korean War turned East Asia into one of the main battlegrounds of the Cold War. The U.S. significantly reinforced its military deployment in Asia, supporting South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan as frontline positions, while the Soviet Union bolstered its support for China and North Korea. East Asia quickly became a confrontation zone for the two major powers, the U.S. and the Soviet Union.

Secondly, the Korean War strengthened the Sino-Soviet alliance. Faced with America’s blockade and threats, China adopted a “one-sided” foreign policy, learning comprehensively from the Soviet Union. During the war, the Soviet Union provided China with a large amount of weapons and technical support, and sent experts to assist in rebuilding China’s defense system. This cooperation consolidated the strategic alliance between China and the Soviet Union, allowing China to gradually become an important member of the international communist camp.

Moreover, the Korean War changed the political dynamics of Asian countries. Japan rapidly rebuilt its economy with American support, becoming a stronghold of capitalism in East Asia; South Korea strengthened its reliance on the U.S. post-war, solidifying a pro-American and anti-communist regime; while the establishment of the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO) in 1954 marked an expansion of U.S. influence in Southeast Asia, further curbing the development of communism.

At the same time, the Central Committee of the Communist Party mobilized the masses to support the war in Korea, calling for the launch of propaganda movements across the country, especially to “instill a universal attitude of hatred, contempt, and disdain towards American imperialism.” It established the Committee for the Defense of World Peace Against American Aggression (later renamed the All-China Anti-American and Aid Korea Federation), directing the establishment of local chapters to initiate a large-scale anti-American and aid-Korea movement. In mid-February 1951, the Central Political Bureau decided to “continue to promote this movement nationwide.” The Anti-American and Aid-Korea Federation issued a notice in March to ensure that every place and every person in the country could actively participate in the movement to defend the homeland against America. The nationwide anti-American and aid-Korea movement officially commenced.

The focus of the anti-American and aid-Korea movement was to eliminate the residual forces and influences of American imperialism in China. In light of the anti-American and aid-Korea situation, the counter-reaction movement also developed with intensity. According to Minister of Public Security 羅瑞卿, it was precisely because American imperialism “had lit the flames of war at our door and had some internal supporters within us” that it was even more necessary to suppress counter-revolutionaries, “to clean up our houses for a better fight against imperialism.” Some scholars point out that the CCP’s nationwide participation in the Korean War was undoubtedly to prevent the possibility of North Korea’s defeat posing a threat to China’s northeastern border, but it also capitalized on the common enemy sentiment to further consolidate its power. The external actions created favorable conditions for internal stability, and internal stability also improved conditions for external action.

In summary, the Chinese Communist Party’s intervention in the Korean War in the early 1950s fundamentally changed Sino-American relations, turning the two countries toward comprehensive opposition and deepening the confrontational nature of the Cold War in the Asian region. The Korean War prompted the formation of the Asian Cold War structure, with the antagonism and cooperative relations among China, the United States, the Soviet Union, and their allies becoming significant characteristics of the international situation in the future.China’s participation in the war showcased the international influence of the new China while simultaneously consolidating domestic power through mobilization campaigns; however, it also incurred the long-term cost of isolation, with its subsequent effects continuing to impact the geopolitical landscape during the Cold War.

  1. Mao Zedong and Nationalism: (汪宏倫 2014: 208-216, 238-240; 盛慕真 2021)

On October 1, 1949, at the founding ceremony, 毛澤東 announced that “the Chinese people have stood up,” marking the establishment of the People’s Republic of China and a new milestone in Chinese history. The Communist Party won power by defeating the Kuomintang, declaring the overthrow of the old society and beginning the construction of a new China. However, as a revolutionary regime, the CCP continued the struggle model from before the founding of the state, launching a series of political movements nationwide to eliminate class enemies. The CCP claimed that the construction of the socialist new state required continuous struggle and revolutionary means. Mao Zedong transformed the sense of crisis regarding survival, constructed through foreign threats (American imperialism and Soviet revisionism) and various domestic latent enemies, into a powerful cohesive force of Chinese nationalism. Simultaneously, Mao called for the realization of a communist utopia and, through the spirit of “revolutionary optimism,” promoted violence and struggle, successfully combining class struggle with nationalism to forge the “people” of the new China.

The various political movements in the 1950s reflected how the CCP integrated nationalism with communism. Initially, Mao Zedong seized the opportunity of the Korean War to promote “defending the homeland” and “resisting American imperialist aggression,” igniting patriotic sentiments nationwide. This war not only elevated China’s international status but also consolidated the regime through mass mobilization, laying the foundation for subsequent political and economic movements. It can be said that the Resist America and Aid Korea movement (1950-1953) brought nationalism to its peak.

In terms of land reform, Mao Zedong called upon peasants to participate in land distribution, successfully establishing an image of liberation by overthrowing the “feudal landlord class” and enabling peasants to “stand up.” In the ideological domain, Mao initiated an ideological reform movement that combined nationalism with communist education. Intellectuals were required to undergo learning and transformation to achieve the transition from the old society to the new China, establishing the identity of “new Chinese people.”

During the Great Leap Forward movement in 1958, Mao Zedong further adopted the slogan of “surpassing the British and catching up with the Americans” to inspire a sense of national pride and collective glory among the people to facilitate the transition toward a communist society. The Great Leap Forward, centered on “self-reliance,” transformed nationalist sentiments into motivation to realize a communist society.

Overall, in the 1950s, Mao Zedong shaped the communist path of new China through the formation, consolidation, and expansion of his “charismatic authority,” utilizing the sentiment of nationalism and mass mobilization.
