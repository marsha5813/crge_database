China: Agency (1950-1960)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Policies on the Withdrawal of Missionaries from Enemy Nations (Yang Weihua 2020: 91-93; Zhang Le 2020: 114-120)

On July 2, 1941, China formally severed diplomatic relations with Germany and Italy. Both governments planned to evacuate their citizens from China, but the majority of German and Italian missionaries insisted on remaining in the country. In August, Foreign Minister Guo Taichi stated that German and Italian missionaries could continue to reside in China as long as they focused on their missionary activities and complied with Chinese laws. However, local military and political authorities were dissatisfied with the Foreign Ministry’s stance and demanded the removal of all such foreigners to prevent them from acting as spies.

On December 9, China declared war on Germany and Italy, categorizing the remaining German and Italian missionaries as enemy aliens. The Foreign Ministry issued orders to keep these foreigners under temporary protection and surveillance, allowing them to stay in their original locations while monitoring their communications. Nonetheless, calls for more stringent measures began to emerge. To establish norms and unify perspectives, the Foreign Ministry consulted with various departments and initially determined that missionaries should not receive any shelter but be concentrated in designated churches under local supervision to continue their religious work. The Military Commission emphasized that several espionage cases disguised as missionary work had already been uncovered, making the justification for continued missionary activities questionable. Nevertheless, the Foreign Ministry insisted on preserving the status quo, emphasizing that the Vatican and foreign missionary organizations had greatly assisted in wartime efforts and propaganda, and due to the relatively lenient treatment by Germany and Italy toward their nationals, concentrated oversight would be sufficient to mitigate espionage concerns.

In January 1942, the Foreign Ministry published the “Regulations on the Treatment of Enemy Nationals” and the “Regulations on the Concentration and Surveillance of Enemy National Clergy.” Article 13 stipulated that enemy clergy should be concentrated in designated Catholic or locally led Jesus churches while continuing their missionary work under the supervision of local authorities. The guidelines emphasized that clergy would be stationed in designated churches for evangelistic endeavors, in keeping with the established directives.

In June 1942, after establishing diplomatic relations with the Vatican, Chiang Kai-shek proposed three principles for handling enemy national clergy: (1) Those suspected of espionage would be treated as enemy aliens; (2) Those without suspicion could freely preach within designated areas but were prohibited from entering military zones; (3) Church properties that needed to be returned would be handled accordingly unless proven to be hostile. The Executive Yuan issued a directive to implement these principles on June 25.

  1. Missionaries Detained in Internment Camps During the Occupation (Leck 2006; Zhang Qinying 2020: 63-109)

Following the outbreak of the Pacific War, American and British missionaries in China’s occupied regions were classified as enemy aliens and confined to various internment camps (Civilian Internment Camps).

The main detention centers included: Shenyang, Beiping, Shandong (Weixian, Qingdao), Shanghai (Pudong, Longhua, Zhabei, Lincoln Road, Daxilu, the Cathedral, Shanghai West, Xujiahui, Franciscan Church, Yuyuan Road, and more than ten other locations), and Guangzhou.

Statistics reveal that during World War II, the number of Western civilians interned by the Japanese military in Asia was roughly comparable to that of prisoners of war, totaling approximately 130,000 individuals. Among these, around 9,350 Western civilians were detained in China. Scholars have pointed out that because expatriates were not military POWs, internment camps should not be equated with POW camps or compared to Nazi Germany’s concentration camps. If American and British missionaries did not evacuate from China, they too would be interned. It is generally believed that the number of missionaries detained in Chinese internment camps was approximately 1,200.

Some of these detention centers were located within areas associated with Christian and Catholic organizations. For instance, nearly a thousand individuals were held at the Weixian internment camp, associated with the Courtyard of the Happy Way operated by the Presbyterian Church in North America, which served as a grouping of church buildings that included a church, hospital, and school. The Stanley internment camp in Hong Kong was situated within the premises of the Anglican St. Stephen’s College. Detention centers such as the Cathedral in Xujiahui, the Jesuit Theological Institute, and the Franciscan Church in Shanghai were also locations for imprisoning missionaries and clergy.

Notable missionaries detained at the Weixian internment camp included Watson M. Hayes, President of North China Theological Seminary; Eric Liddell, the British Olympic champion from the 1924 games and a teacher at Tianjin New Learning School, who died in the camp on February 21, 1945; Father Raymond J. de Jaegher; and L. Y. Davies, academic dean at Shandong University. Ralph A. Ward, Bishop of the American Methodist Church, was also interned in Shanghai and Beiping. John L. Stuart, President of Yenching University, was imprisoned in Beiping.

  1. The Imprisonment of Chinese Theologian Zhao Zichen (Xing Fuzeng 2005: 286-289)

On December 8, 1941, the Japanese military attacked Pearl Harbor, marking the beginning of the Pacific War. Due to hostilities between Japan and the United States, Christian schools, hospitals, and organizations throughout North China were blockaded by Japanese forces. All British and American-run primary and secondary schools in Beiping were taken over by the Japanese military. The circumstances surrounding Yenching University were dire as well; just hours after the Pearl Harbor incident, Japanese troops surrounded the campus, capturing 11 professors, including Zhao Zichen, Zhang Dongxun, Lu Zhiwei, and Hong Weilian.

While imprisoned, Zhao resolved that upon his release he would accomplish three goals: first, to recuperate and restore his health; second, to write books; and third, to “help the church achieve self-sufficiency and independence, transforming it into a church that belongs to China and does not rely on outsiders.” Despite enduring great suffering during his imprisonment, his faith became a source of strength, with prayer serving as his greatest source of empowerment. Simultaneously, he provided comfort to fellow prisoners through his faith.

Zhao was imprisoned until June 18, 1942, totaling 193 days in confinement. Following his release, he was sheltered by Ling Xianyang, president of the North China Anglican Church, residing within the Anglican compound in Beiping. During this time, Zhao completed manuscripts for “Christian Progress” and “The Life of St. Paul.” Later, he documented his experiences in prison in “Memoirs of Imprisonment,” published in 1948.

  1. The Communist Party’s Religious Policies During the War of Resistance (Wang Jianlang, Huang Kewu 2021: 941-942; Central Committee 1992a: 316; Chen Jinlong 2006: 74)

In May 1941, Mao Zedong drafted the “Governance Guidelines for the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region,” which stated: “Protect the religious freedom of all anti-Japanese citizens (landlords, capitalists, farmers, workers, etc.) and respect the religious beliefs and customs of the Mongolian and Hui ethnic groups.” When explaining this article, the border region government clarified that “religion refers to Islam, Lamaism, Catholicism, etc.” “Weakened ethnic groups and those believing in religion all have rights, and no one can restrict anyone else.”

The “Temporary Regulations on Land Use in the Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu Border Region,” published in 1941, stipulated: “Land contracts obtained lawfully by Catholic and Protestant churches shall be recognized as owned by the church; foreign missionaries who purchased or donated land in a personal capacity shall arrange for the ownership of that land to be transferred to the relevant church or school.” In January 1942, the Communist Party issued attachments to the “Central Committee’s Decision on Land Policy in Anti-Japanese Base Areas,” stating: “Religious land (including land owned by Christian, Buddhist, Islamic, Taoist, and other sects) shall remain unchanged.”In the winter of 1934, Liu Liangmo organized a mass singing event in Shanghai under the YMCA and initiated the movement for songs of national salvation, composing a song titled “Save China.” In 1935, he collaborated with Lu Gancheng, the general secretary of the Shanghai YMCA, to establish a singing group within the organization. In May, he recorded “The March of the Volunteers” on vinyl, which became an important piece performed by the singing group. When the “Seven Gentlemen” like Shen Junru were arrested by the government, Liu also participated in rescue efforts. In January 1936, the “Shanghai United National Salvation Federation” was established, with Shanghai Christians issuing a declaration opposing passive diplomacy and demanding the use of force to uphold national integrity and dignity, of which Liu was one of the 28 initiators. In May, the “National United Salvation Federation” was formed in Shanghai, and Liu served as a board member. During various activities organized by the Federation, Liu Liangmo led the singing of “The March of the Volunteers,” earning him the title of “cheerleader for the Salvation Federation” from Shen Junru and others. In June 1936, during the first anniversary of the establishment of the National United Salvation Federation, the mass singing group held a large singing event at the Shanghai Public Gymnasium, where Liu conducted from the stage.

In the summer of 1936, Liu traveled to the United States to attend the “World Student Christian Federation” conference held in San Francisco. After returning to China, he joined the YMCA’s “Military Services Department,” working in various places such as Suiyuan, Suzhou, Zhengzhou, and Changsha to promote services for soldiers. During this time, he authored “The ABCs of Mass Singing,” which detailed how to teach the masses to sing and organize community singing groups.

In 1939, while in Jinhua, Zhejiang, he met with Zhou Enlai. Later, Liu was briefly detained by the Nationalist Party in Ningbo under suspicions of being a Communist but was released and subsequently applied to the YMCA to continue his studies in the United States.

From the summer of 1940 until August 1949, Liu Liangmo was primarily active in the United States, studying at Crozer Theological Seminary and the University of Pennsylvania. During this time, he also campaigned for China’s anti-Japanese efforts and organized singing groups among the expatriate community. After the victory in the anti-Japanese war, he continued to stay in the U.S., preparing to establish the American branch of the “China Democratic League.” In September 1946, when Feng Yuxiang spoke in the U.S., Liu served as his translator.

In August 1949, Liu Liangmo received a telegram from Zhou Enlai via Gong Peng, inviting him to Beijing to participate in the new Political Consultative Conference. He promptly returned to China with his family. Along with Wu Yaozong, Deng Yuzhi, Zhang Xueyan, and Zhao Zichen, Liu became a Christian representative in the Political Consultative Conference. Subsequently, Liu offered his position to an Islamic representative, changing to an alternate representative. During the conference, Liu and several representatives jointly proposed that “The March of the Volunteers” be adopted as the national anthem, which was approved.

After returning to China, he became the director of the national council’s work group within the YMCA and participated in a Christian visitation team in North China, meeting with Zhou Enlai in May 1950. Within the Communist Party, Wu Yaozong’s initiated Three-Self Reform Declaration was referred to as the “Wu-Liu Movement,” highlighting Liu’s involvement.

Liu Liangmo played a significant role in the Three-Self Reform Movement: he was an initiator of the reform declaration and a member of the presidium for the “meeting of Christian groups accepting U.S. subsidies.” As an executive committee member, he was also the leader of the propaganda group. In his role as head of propaganda, he became a key figure in advocating for the Christian accusation movement. In May 1951, he published an article in the “People’s Daily” titled “How to Hold a Church Complaint Meeting?” calling for local churches to understand that the purpose of the complaints was to eliminate imperialist influences within Christianity. The targets of the complaints were “imperialist elements hidden within the church, their accomplices, and the failures of other churches.” He further provided guidance on organizing complaint meetings: first, establish a “Complaint Committee” to determine whom to accuse and who should be invited to testify; then hold mobilization meetings to instill the rationale and importance of why and what to accuse. Following this, accusation group meetings should be held in various churches and communities to identify the most effective accusers, inviting them to participate in the larger accusation meeting. He emphasized that during the preparation of complaints, guidance from the Religious Affairs Office was essential. In conjunction with the complaints, effective accusation statements could be published in local newspapers, and exhibitions could be held. Liu Liangmo’s guidance indeed became a reference model for various localities.

Moreover, he was an important theorist behind the “complaint” movement. In the early phase of the reform movement, he published several articles in “Tianfeng,” focusing on points such as: (1) American imperialism’s use of Christianity to invade China; (2) Opposition to imperialism is not contradictory to patriotism; (3) Critiques of “super-political” stances and “exclusive love” ideologies.In January 1948, the Executive Yuan amended Article 7 to state: “Nationals, companies, or churches from countries that have entered into equitable treaties with our country may apply for registration and reissuance of land ownership certificates for land acquired in the name of Chinese nationals prior to the treaty, provided they possess valid documentation proving their rights as genuine purchasers at the time of acquisition and are endorsed by their country’s consulate.”

  1. The Religious Policies of the Communist Party During the Chinese Civil War (Zhonggong Zhongyang 1991: 264; 1992b: 35-36, 38; 1992c: 44-48)

In September 1945, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China indicated in its “Guidance on the Work in Newly Liberated Cities” that “the rescue of allied prisoners and the protection of foreign nationals… should not infringe upon religious organizations and charitable institutions, and we should strive not to commandeer schools…”

In February 1948, the Central Committee issued directives related to the treatment of foreigners, stating that “in the newly occupied areas and cities, we will encounter more foreign expatriates and the churches, schools, hospitals, orphanages, and nursing homes established by them… Generally, we will not adopt exclusionary or confiscatory policies toward these economic, cultural, and religious institutions established by foreign nationals, regardless of whether they belong to an imperialist nature.” It continued, “As long as they acknowledge and comply with the laws and regulations of our democratic government and the People’s Liberation Army, they may be allowed to continue residing in our liberated areas, conduct business or other legitimate activities, and will be protected by our democratic government.”

Article 1 stated, “Any churches established by foreigners and the schools, hospitals, orphanages, and nursing homes they operate shall not be confiscated or destroyed after our army arrives. They are allowed to continue their activities as long as they comply with the laws of our armed forces and democratic government and do not engage in espionage or sabotage. Should any foreigners or agents entrusted by them flee, their properties will be put under government custody. However, the original organizers can return to take over. If these organizations have acquired land in rural areas or have occupied land, that land should be handled in accordance with land laws. However, any small gardens within the scope of these organizations should remain under their ownership, and property not attached to land should not be confiscated.”

Article 6 mentioned the need to “identify any imperialist elements among foreign nationals and pay attention to any conspiratorial sabotage actions… When certain foreign missionaries engage in espionage or sabotage activities, we should not only detain and punish them but also gather sufficient evidence to publicize their misdeeds domestically and internationally, making a clear distinction between their criminal acts and legitimate religious activities. Those missionaries who are guilty should be prosecuted or expelled, but churches need not be closed. It is permissible for them to send other representatives to lead their churches to avoid misunderstandings by foreigners regarding our government’s stance on religion.”

In January 1949, the “Guidance on Foreign Affairs” issued by the Central Committee stated that “in principle, the privileges of imperialism in China must be abolished and the independence of the Chinese nation must be realized, a position that is unwavering. However, in the execution steps, issues should be handled according to their nature and circumstances. Problems that are beneficial and resolvable for the Chinese people should be addressed. Those that remain unresolved should be deferred. Issues that are not significantly harmful or not considerably harmful to the Chinese people should not be rushed to resolve, even if they present easy solutions. Problems that have not been thoroughly studied or have not matured for resolution should also not be hastily approached.” Specifically regarding foreign missionaries, point 5 stated: “Those already in our liberated areas are allowed to continue residing and conducting their business, while newcomers are not permitted for the time being.” Point 6 concerning schools operated by foreigners specified: “Existing private foreign schools can maintain their status; their curricula must comply with the regulations applicable to other schools. New applications for establishment are denied.” Point 7 concerning hospitals operated by foreigners indicated: “Existing institutions may continue but will be subject to supervision and inspection. New establishments are not allowed without approval.” Point 10 regarding foreign charitable organizations specified: “Those belonging to imperialist governments… should not be recognized, and their assistance and relief should be refused. Those belonging to international cooperative organizations should be evaluated based on whether they undermine our national sovereignty and the independence and autonomy of our populace before a decision is made to accept their aid.”In January 1948, the Executive Yuan amended Article 7 to state: “Nationals, companies, or churches from countries that have entered into equitable treaties with our country may apply for registration and reissuance of land ownership certificates for land acquired in the name of Chinese nationals before the treaty, provided they possess valid documentation proving their rights as genuine purchasers at the time of acquisition and are endorsed by their country’s consulate.”

After the Chinese Communist Party intervened in the Korean War, on December 16, 1950, the U.S. government announced the control of properties in territories it governed in China and North Korea, as well as prohibiting vessels from the U.S. from sailing to Chinese ports. In light of the U.S. freezing of Chinese public and private properties in the U.S., the Central People’s Government State Council also issued an order on December 28 to control American properties in China and freeze U.S. public and private deposits in China. This action severely impacted missionary work in China, which still relied on American funds for sustenance. On December 29, the State Council announced decisions on handling cultural, educational, and relief institutions, as well as religious groups receiving U.S. subsidies, which stated that “Chinese religious groups accepting American subsidies should be transformed into organizations completely independent and self-managed by Chinese Christians. The government encourages their self-sustaining movements.”

From April 16 to 21, 1951, the Religious Affairs Bureau of the Ministry of Culture and Education of the Central People’s Government convened a “Conference on Handling Christian Groups Accepting American Subsidies” in Beijing. The purpose of this government-convened meeting was to seek “to finally, thoroughly, permanently, and completely end more than a century of cultural aggression by American imperialism against the Chinese people under the leadership of the Central People’s Government.”The conference was convened under the name of “Conference on Handling Christian Groups Accepting American Subsidies,” but in essence, it was not merely addressing these “technical” issues. A comprehensive view of the meeting’s content reveals that the core topic was how to accelerate severing the ties between Christianity and imperialism and to initiate a “struggle” between the church and American imperialism. As noted by 陸定一 (Lu Dingyi), deputy director of the Ministry of Culture and Education of the State Council, at the meeting: “Handling Christian groups that accept American subsidies is not a technical issue; it is a struggle. The goal of this struggle is to thoroughly eliminate the influence of over a hundred years of American imperialism’s cultural aggression against our country.” Moreover, American cultural aggression is not just a historical issue; it is a current reality that continues to manifest through Christianity.

As a result, over 150 church representatives from across the country, including many self-governing churches unrelated to Western denominations, such as 敬奠瀛 (Jesus Family), 倪柝聲 (Christian Meeting Place), and 魏以撒 (True Jesus Church), passed the “Procedures for Handling Christian Groups Accepting American Subsidies” and the “Joint Declaration of Representatives from Various Chinese Christian Churches and Organizations.” At the same time, to more comprehensively promote the Christian reform movement and lead Chinese churches to undertake the task of building a new China, the Beijing conference decided to establish a new organization—the “Preparatory Committee for the Chinese Christian Anti-American Aid to Korea Three-Self Reform Movement,” specifically responsible for coordinating Christian reform efforts, with 吳耀宗 as the chairman of the preparatory committee. All Christian groups and denominations, including the council, were expected to undertake learning and reform under the guidance of the reform preparatory committee.

The establishment of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee not only targeted reforms within Christianity but also reflected the CCP’s attempt to establish patriotic religious groups across major religions, thereby achieving unified leadership in the religious sphere. In the 1950s, various religions subsequently established patriotic religious groups supported by the party state: the 中国佛教协会 (Chinese Buddhist Association, 1953), the 中国伊斯兰教协会 (Chinese Islamic Association, 1953), the 中国道教协会 (Chinese Taoist Association, 1957), and the 中国天主教教友爱国会 (Patriotic Catholic Association, 1957).

  1. 金陵協和神學院 (Jinling Union Theological Seminary) 1953 (徐如雷 2022: 244-248; 汪維藩 2013: 1-2)

By the end of 1950, after China intervened in the Korean War, Sino-U.S. relations were completely broken, and the Chinese government announced a freeze on all U.S. assets in China. The economic aid that Christian missionary work had traditionally relied upon was thus interrupted, leading various seminaries to the brink of closure. In 1951, to reduce expenses, Jinling Theological Seminary merged with Jinling Women’s Theological Seminary, setting a precedent for institutional consolidation.

In August 1952, a theological education symposium was held in Shanghai in the East China region, where it was decided to establish a preparatory committee for the East China United Theological Seminary. In November, Jinling Union Theological Seminary was officially established in Nanjing, formed by the union of 11 seminaries in the East China region: Shanghai Anglican Central Theological Seminary, Shanghai Baptist Theological Seminary, Hangzhou Chinese Theological Seminary, Nanjing Jinling Theological Seminary (which included Jinling Women’s Theological Seminary), Wuxi North China Theological Seminary, Jinan Qilu Theological Seminary, Zhangzhou Minnan Theological Seminary, Fuzhou Union Theological Seminary, Ningbo Trinity Bible Institute, Zhenjiang Baptist Bible Institute, and Jinan Mingdao Bible Institute.

The 11 seminaries included both seminaries under church universities and those jointly run by denominations, as well as independent seminaries. In terms of theological positions, they encompassed both fundamentalist traditions and those leaning towards modernist theologians. At that time, in order to prevent faith from being influenced, representatives of the fundamentalist faction suggested merging into two seminaries—modernists uniting at Nanjing Jinling Theological Seminary and fundamentalists at Wuxi North China Theological Seminary, leading to a stalemate in opinions at the conference. 罗竹风 (Luo Zhufeng), head of the East China Religious Affairs Bureau, stated: “Under the premise of anti-imperialism and patriotism, any theological thought has the freedom to exist, and the government will not interfere. Various theological thoughts in China cannot be considered to have a singular authority. In other words, since religious belief is free, our theological thoughts must also be free.” The principle of mutual respect at Jinling Union Theological Seminary emerged from this line of thinking. The school’s guiding principles were four “cornerstones of faith”: “1. The entire Bible is God’s revelation, including all essential doctrines concerning salvation, serving as the foundation for believers’ faith and moral conduct. 2. The one God is the source of all things and the Father of humanity, full of justice and love. 3. Jesus Christ is the Holy Son of God, who became flesh to redeem mankind, was crucified, resurrected from the dead, and is the head of the church, the Savior of all ages. 4. The Holy Spirit is one of the Trinity, working in regeneration and sanctification, bestowing various gifts to believers in the church.” Note that all four points are listed as “1,” without any order of precedence.

To accommodate different theological traditions, Jinling Union Theological Seminary adopted the principle of “mutual respect” in its early years.Among the more than 30 faculty members at Jinling Union Theological Seminary, one-third came from a fundamentalist background, while over 60% of the more than 100 students adhered to fundamentalist traditions. Balancing the debates between “modernists” and “fundamentalists” became a crucial issue that the seminary had to address. At that time, the Jinling Education Office stipulated that certain courses within theology and biblical studies be divided into classes based on either “modernist” or “fundamentalist” viewpoints, with no directives for a “unified faith.”

Between 1957 and 1959, the China Spiritual Seminary, Chongqing Theological Seminary, and Guangzhou Union Theological Seminary merged into Jinling Union Theological Seminary. The Chongqing Theological Seminary and China Spiritual Seminary were respectively administered by 陈崇桂 (Chen Chonggui) and 贾玉铭 (Jia Yuming), who had spiritual backgrounds. Their participation in the Three-Self Movement was believed to be based on considerations of unifying the spiritual faction, allowing them to operate independently. However, following the Anti-Rightist Movement, the spiritual faction was largely purged from the Three-Self organizations. Chen Chonggui was labeled a rightist, and Jia Yuming was also marked as part of the inner party, resulting in the merging of both spiritually inclined seminaries into Jinling Union Theological Seminary.

In 1961, Yenching Union Theological Seminary was further merged into Jinling, making Jinling the only theological seminary in the country, which resumed classes in April 1961. However, due to the severe impact of the Great Leap Forward on religious activities, Jinling could only offer continuing education programs for pastoral coworkers. By 1962, undergraduate courses were reinstated. Nevertheless, starting in 1962, the seminary was influenced by the “Four Clean-Ups” Movement (which aimed to clean politics, the economy, organizations, and thoughts), leading to a suspension of new student admissions. In 1966, with the outbreak of the Cultural Revolution, the last remaining seminary established a command center for the Red Guards, and the seminary was forced to close, plunging Chinese theological education into a dark period lasting over a decade.

  1. Yenching Union Theological Seminary 1953 (徐以驊 1999: 68-151)

In December 1950, the United States announced the freezing of all Chinese assets in America, and numerous educational, charitable, cultural, and religious organizations that relied heavily on American subsidies faced an immediate financial crisis. On January 15, 1951, the Ministry of Education held a meeting for higher education institutions receiving foreign subsidies, where Minister 马叙伦 (Ma Xulun) pointed out that church schools were significant activities in American imperialism’s cultural aggression. In response to the U.S. freeze on Chinese assets, the State Council decided to take over educational sovereignty, converting the previously subsidized higher education institutions into schools run by the Chinese people themselves. After the meeting, the Ministry of Education of the Central People’s Government and the Education Department of the East China Military and Political Committee announced the takeover of Union Medical College and Yenching University. On February 12, Yenching University was officially taken over by the Ministry of Education.

With the freezing of U.S. funds, Yenching University suddenly lost all subsidies. As Yenching University was taken over by the government, the future of the religious school came under scrutiny. President 陆志韦 (Lu Zhiwei) suggested reorganizing the religious school to become an academic research institution for various Chinese religions, thus allowing the government to permit it to function as a department within the university. However,院长赵紫宸 (Zhao Zichen) preferred to separate the university from the religious school, stating that the only way forward after separation would be “to let the church support itself.” To this end, Zhao convened a meeting of all faculty and students and invited some alumni in Beijing to participate in the discussion. A few days later, Zhao drafted a document titled “The Future Direction of Yenching Religious Academy,” proposing to rename the religious academy as Yenching Religious Academy, register it with the Beijing municipal government, and request approval from the Ministry of Education to become a private religious academy.

After the government took over Yenching University, it immediately launched a patriotic education campaign, organizing seminars and denunciation meetings aimed at purging the harmful effects of American cultural aggression. Starting in July 1951, the religious academy at Yenching University also began learning research and engaged in criticism and self-criticism. After several months of study, the Beijing Religious Affairs Bureau planned to hold a meeting at the religious academy to denounce American imperialism’s use of religion as a means of aggression. The denunciation meeting was held on November 24, lasting the entire day, during which Zhao Zichen made accusations.

During the Three-Anti and Five-Anti Movements, the Beijing Municipal Committee of the CCP dispatched a “work team” to Yenching University in December 1951 to lead the movement. The president was sidelined, and classes were suspended to carry out the movement. Although the religious academy had already separated from the university, the discipline inspection committee of Yenching University still centrally directed the religious academy’s activities. In early March 1952, Zhao Zichen, as the head of the religious academy, was subjected to two rounds of accusations from the entire school, requiring him to conduct a thorough self-examination. Ultimately, Zhao was deemed to have a “stubborn” attitude and was removed from his position as president in March due to accusations from the “Economy Inspection Committee,” including “using religion to organize and systematically spread toxins that undermine the new China; serving as one of the heads of reactionary religious groups worldwide; and, as a representative in the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference, appearing to comply with government policies while actually opposing them.” As a result, he “accepted the demands of the masses” and had his position terminated, with 蒋翼振 (Jiang Yizhen) serving as the acting head.In May 1952, the movement was declared concluded. In November, the religious academy moved away from Yanyuan. As early as May 1952, Yenching Religious Academy, Beijing Theological Seminary, and Union Women’s Theological College began preliminary discussions about merging the three institutions and negotiated unification in August. In April 1953, the three seminaries officially merged to form “Yenching Union Theological Seminary,” with 王仲梓 (Wang Zhongzi) and 蒋翼振 (Jiang Yizhen) serving as the chair and vice-chair of the administrative committee, respectively. In September, the Far East Missionary Alliance Holiness Bible College, Shenyang Northeast Theological Seminary, and Henan Kaifeng Huanei Baptist Theological Seminary also merged into Yenching. Between 1954 and 1956, Hubei Lankou Lutheran Theological Seminary, Hunan Bible College, Beijing Assembly of God Truth College, Central China Union Theological Seminary, and Beijing Xiangshan Spiritual Institute joined in succession.

In 1961, Yenching Union Theological Seminary was merged into Jinling Union Theological Seminary.

  1. China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee 1954 (邢福增 2010: 111-116)

After the establishment of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee in April 1951, nearly three years of efforts were still met with rejection from certain spiritual faction churches citing “different beliefs” and “reforming faith.” The impact of churches such as Christian Meeting Place and the church of 王明道 (Wang Mingdao) was particularly significant. Strengthening and expanding the unity among Chinese Christians under the overarching principle of “Three-Self Reform” became an important issue that needed to be addressed.

From November 27 to December 16, 1953, the Second National Religious Work Conference was held in Beijing. The conference was presided over by 习仲勋 (Xi Zhongxun), Minister of the Central Propaganda Department and Deputy Director of the Ministry of Culture and Education, and 陆定一 (Lu Dingyi), Deputy Director of the Ministry of Culture and Education, summarizing the Christian work from 1950 to 1953 and formulating future work guidelines. According to the meeting documents, to “implement the patriotic reform movement,” the future work’s focus would be “to gain the broad support of devout believers while also winning over the majority of pastoral staff.” This way, it would be possible to “expand the China Christian Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee as a leading organization by more comprehensively including representative upper leaders from the churches, making it a genuine patriotic united front organization for all Christian denominations in the country.” To achieve this goal, it was emphasized that the slogan should focus on “patriotism” and not emphasize the slogans of “Three-Self” or “reform,” and that a formal organization should be established in the first half of 1954, possibly using the name “Christian Patriotic Association.” To unite more believers, patriotic reform organizations and active patriots must genuinely “care for the religious lives of believers,” as a fundamental means of connecting with the laity and a major condition for religious mass work.

From May 17 to 22, 1954, the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee held its second plenary meeting, attended by the heads of the Central Religious Affairs Bureau, 何成湘 (He Chengxiang), 罗竹风 (Luo Zhufeng), and 上海宗教事务处 (Shanghai Religious Affairs Bureau) director 陈一鸣 (Chen Yiming). The meeting decided to convene a national Christian conference in Beijing in the second half of the year and to establish a “formal leading organization.” Clearly, the national Christian conference was convened primarily under the directives of the Central Committee’s Christian work, with the goals of “great unity” and the name of the new organization set in accordance with the CCP’s evaluation of the Christian reform movement. The party state was not satisfied with only individual denominational churches participating in the Three-Self Reform, and resolving the resistance from influential spiritual factions to the movement became a focal point of Christian work.

Prior to the 1954 National Christian Conference, the preparatory office extended invitations to representatives of the spiritual factions. The conference secretary general 沈德溶 (Shen Derong) pointed out that to “further expand the anti-imperialist patriotic unity of Chinese Christianity,” it was decided to invite “representatives from certain denominations and groups that have not participated or actively oppose the China Christian Three-Self Reform Movement.” In the end, 27 representatives from spiritual factions participated in the Three-Self Conference for the first time, with 15 from Shanghai alone. However, due to the boycott by 王明道 and other spiritual factions, five invited representatives from Beijing declined to attend.

From July 22 to August 6, 1954, the China Christian National Conference was held in Beijing. 吳耀宗 (Wu Yaozong) suggested that for the sake of unity, the name of the formally established leading organization be changed from the previously used “Christian Three-Self Reform Movement” to “Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement.” This was because the term “reform” easily evoked associations with historical religious reform movements in the church. “Some comrades mistakenly believe that ‘reform’ means ‘to abolish the church’s system’; others fear that ‘reform’ may interfere with their own beliefs, thus adopting a passive observation stance towards the Three-Self Reform Movement.” Therefore, the renaming of the “Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement” would help eliminate factors that hinder church unity. He even emphasized that the Three-Self Patriotic Movement would become “a movement for all Chinese believers, a great unity of all Chinese believers.”“Any denomination, any church and believer with differing beliefs should work together on the basis of patriotism and love for their faith to contribute the greatest strength to the Three-Self Patriotic Movement.” During the National Christian Conference, 何成湘 (He Chengxiang), Director of the Central Religious Affairs Bureau, criticized those who refused to participate in the patriotic movement citing “different beliefs,” but he still hoped that the spiritual factions could return to a common foundation of patriotism.

The chairman of the China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was 吳耀宗 (Wu Yaozong), with 李儲文 (Li Chuwen) serving as the secretary-general. The conference passed the “Letter to All National Colleagues,” reaffirming that “we understand that the purpose of self-governance is not to unify or alter the church’s systems, but to completely sever ties with imperialism, allowing Chinese Christians to unite and manage their own churches; self-supporting does not interfere with each church’s economy but aims for each church to entirely cut off its economic connections with imperialism and be fully responsible to Chinese believers; and self-propagating is not about unifying beliefs or altering faith, but about thoroughly purging the remnants of imperialist ideological influence so that each church preaches the pure gospel consistent with the Bible. As for the differences in faith, systems, and rituals among churches, we should mutually respect one another.” This can be seen as a response to the spiritual factions.

  1. Christian Joint Publishing Bureau 1956 (邢福增 2012b: 228-232, 239-260)

According to a survey by the China Christian Council in 1950, the national Christian publishing institutions included: 中華信義書報部 (Chinese Lutheran Press), 中華浸信會書局 (Chinese Baptist Publishing House), 中華基督教出版協會 (Chinese Christian Publishing Association), 中國基督教聖教書會 (Chinese Christian Sacred Book Society), 天風社 (Heavenly Wind Society), 田家半月刊 (Tianjia Biweekly), 青年協會書局 (Youth Association Publishing House), 宣道書局 (Evangelical Publishing House), 時兆報館 (Shizhao Press), 華英書局 (Huaying Publishing House), 廣協書局 (Guangxie Publishing House), 廣學會 (Guangxue Society), 靈修日程社 (Spiritual Calendar Society), 閩北基督教會書局 (Northern Min Christian Church Publishing House), 中華聖公會中央辦事處書室 (Central Office of the Chinese Anglican Church Publishing House), 上海福音廣播電台 (Shanghai Gospel Radio Station), and others totaling 17 institutions.

In January 1951, the East China Military and Political Committee required all Christian publishing establishments receiving foreign subsidies to register with relevant authorities. In the new context of the Korean War, the Christian publishing industry faced severe challenges. On March 16, a Christian publishing conference was held in Beijing to assist the Christian publishing sector in overcoming the difficulties arising from the cessation of U.S. funds after the outbreak of the Korean War, and to discuss how to sever ties with imperialism and eliminate its influence. The conference decided that the Christian Publishing Association would be responsible for book reviews, providing feedback to various publishing units to remove the influence of imperialism. Additionally, it proposed a unified operation of the Christian publishing sector, aiming to establish a joint distribution entity.

After the publishing conference, the association pushed for the establishment of Christian joint bookstores. Between March and June 1951, the association convened two discussions with Christian publishing institutions in Shanghai and consulted various organizations across the nation. It is noteworthy that the responses were “principally supportive, though there were some considerations regarding actual participation conditions,” reflecting some resistance from existing publishing institutions toward the unification proposal. The earliest supporters were only Guangxue Society, Youth Association Publishing House, and Baptist Bookstore, which sent representatives (吴耀宗, 张仕章, 胡祖蔭, 张仁镜, 金炎青, and 张伯怀) to form a preparatory committee, deciding to first establish a central joint entity in Shanghai. However, further achieving the merger of the various Christian publishing institutions involved more complex issues, with the willingness of independent publishing entities to yield and submit to the leadership of the Christian Publishing Association being a primary concern.

Between 1952 and 1953, fewer than 10 publishing entities continued to operate, collectively publishing 44 Christian works, reflecting serious setbacks in the Christian publishing industry under political pressure. In 1953, the Christian Publishing Association ended itself due to a lack of necessity for continuation. Four entities, including the Guangxue Society, Chinese Baptist Publishing House, Youth Association National Council Publishing Department, and China Sunday School Alliance, formed a united editorial committee in 1953 according to the spirit of the Christian publishing conference as the first step towards unification. The united editorial committee consisted of the main editorial staff from the four entities, with 沈德溶 (Shen Derong) serving as executive secretary to coordinate the publication plans, while the actual publishing responsibilities remained with the respective entities. This indicates that the Three-Self Reform organization had begun to manage and coordinate the reform plans for Christian publishing. In 1954 and 1955, the Guangxue Society, Chinese Baptist Publishing House, Youth Association Publishing House, and China Sunday School Alliance collaborated on editorial work and published three titles under the Guangxue Society, including two volumes of “New Sermon Collections” and “The Life and Teachings of Prophet Jeremiah” (by 李榮芳). Meanwhile, various publishing houses still published over 30 works under their independent names.In December 1956, the Christianity Joint Publishing Bureau was formed through the alliance of the Guangxue Society, Chinese Baptist Publishing House, China Sunday School Alliance, and Youth Association Publishing House, with 江文漢 (Jiang Wenhan) serving as the general secretary. The establishment of the Christianity Joint Publishing Bureau marked the formal entry of the Christian publishing industry into an era of “unification.” After its establishment, the Joint Publishing Bureau published a total of 13 books in 1957. However, following the Anti-Rightist Movement, the work of the Christianity Joint Publishing Bureau faced unprecedented challenges. By the 1960s, there were no more advertisements for new book publications in the magazine 天風 (Tianfeng), reflecting a halt in the publishing activities of the Joint Publishing Bureau. During the Cultural Revolution, with the cessation of the Three-Self organizations, the Joint Publishing Bureau also faced a similar fate of closure.

  1. China Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Seminary (天風 1956a: 28, 1956b: 3; 王德龍 2017: 146-164)

After the establishment of the People’s Republic of China, the central government began to address the system of theological education in China, primarily through unification efforts. Utilizing the funding disruption post-Korean War, several seminaries were merged into three “unified” seminaries: Jinling Union Theological Seminary (Nanjing), Yenching Union Theological Seminary (Beijing), and Guangzhou Union Theological Seminary (Guangzhou). By 1956, these three unifying seminaries had merged over 30 seminaries from various regions of the country.

However, outside of these three “unified” seminaries, there were still some individual seminaries that managed to persist. The first category was seminaries run by spiritual factions that joined the Three-Self Movement: besides the influential 重庆神学院 (Chongqing Theological Seminary) led by 陈崇桂 (Chen Chonggui) and 贾玉铭 (Jia Yuming), there was also the Huai’en Amateur Theological Seminary established by 戚庆才 (Qi Qingcai) at the Baptist Church. The second category consisted of independent seminaries run by individuals from the spiritual faction. Due to reservations regarding the Three-Self Movement, these seminaries continued to operate independently. Though smaller in scale and unconnected to larger organizations, they relied mainly on faith-based contributions, enabling them to survive even after the “liberation.” Examples include the 北京香山灵修院 (Beijing Xiangshan Spiritual Institute) led by 毕咏琴 (Bi Yongqin), the 中国传道人修养院 (China Christian Worker’s Home, led by 焦维真), and the 圣经学社 (Bible Society) in Shanghai.

During the 1954 National Christian Conference, the China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was established, aimed at encouraging the participation of the spiritual faction in the Three-Self Movement. 焦维真 attended the conference, while 毕咏琴 refused to do so. However, after the arrest of the anti-Three-Self activist 王明道 in 1955, many within the spiritual faction, including 毕咏琴, decided to join the Three-Self Movement, likely with the intention of fostering unity among spiritual factions. The China Christian Worker’s Home, Huai’en Amateur Theological Seminary, and Bible Society did not merge into Jinling or Yenching Union Theological Seminaries but instead cooperated with 贾玉铭’s China Christian Spiritual Theological Seminary to form the 中国基督教上海灵修神学院 (China Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Seminary), with 贾玉铭 as its president, and 焦维真, 戚庆才, and 杨绍唐 (Yang Shaotang) as vice presidents, and 竺规身 (Zhu Guishen) as the chairman of the board. The seminary aimed to emphasize “this collaboration is centered on Christ, pooling the strength of each body to cultivate holy workers who love their country and church in the new China.” Its educational policy prioritized “spiritual life and consistent biblical knowledge, aiming for students to achieve a correct balance in faith and life, knowledge and experience, so they may be useful to the Lord in building the church in the new China.”

In October 1956, the seminary held a founding ceremony where 贾玉铭 stated: “‘China Christianity’ is our target audience, ‘Shanghai’ is the location of the seminary, ‘Spirituality’ indicates our focus on experiential faith and abundant life, while ‘Theological Seminary’ signifies our dedication to the study of biblical truth and theological knowledge.” The seminary hoped to concentrate its efforts on cultivating “holy workers” who met the needs of the times.

However, following the onset of the Anti-Rightist Movement, 贾玉铭, 焦维真, 戚庆才, and 杨绍唐 were all required to go to Beijing for re-education. Both 贾 and 杨 were classified as designated rightists, and all classes were suspended, compelling students and faculty to participate in educational activities in Shanghai and engage in labor to combat drought in Jiading County. In 1958, the Religious Affairs Bureau required that Three-Self churches were not to hire students from the Spiritual Academy as ministers, rescinding previously issued appointment letters due to perceived ideological issues, mandating further participation in educational activities. This re-education process continued until March 1959, at which point students from other provinces were sent back to their hometowns. The China Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Seminary was ordered closed, marking the end of the last seminary managed through spiritual faction cooperation, ultimately succumbing to the tides of revolution.

  1. National Christian Conference and Promotion Association 14th Annual Meeting 1950 (Song Jun 2017: 117-159, 213-248; Xing Fuzeng 2012b: 25-29, 43-47)

At the end of January 1950, the Executive Committee of the China National Christian Council decided to hold a national Christian conference in Beijing at Yenching University from August 19 to 27 under the theme “Christianity and the New Era.” A special preparatory committee was formed to follow up on related work. The committee was chaired by Zhu Youyu (Bishop of the China Anglican Church), with Tu Yuqing (Secretary-General of the National Fellowship), Bao Zheqing (Secretary-General of the Zhejiang Shanghai Baptist Association), Cui Xianxiang (Secretary-General of the National Council of the China Christian Church), and Jiang Changchuan (Bishop of the North China Conference of the China Methodist Church) as vice-chairpersons. Wu Gaozi (Secretary-General of the Promotion Association) was appointed as Secretary-General. Each member was a leader of major denominations and organizations nationwide. The preparatory committee decided to form four groups: the conference declaration, the ministry program, the reform plan, and cooperative organizations. On March 28, the national conference preparatory committee decided to postpone the full meeting. In July, Secretary-General Wu Gaozi proposed his resignation during an executive meeting, which was accepted by the Standing Committee of the Promotion Association. In August, since the national conference was too close to the 14th annual meeting of the Association, to streamline and save resources, it was decided to merge the conference into the annual meeting.

The postponement and eventual cancellation of the national conference reflected the difficulties it faced. On one hand, there were obviously differing opinions within the Chinese church regarding how to reform. The Promotion Association intended to play the role of representing Christianity to push for reform (the Promotion faction), which clearly diverged from the external progressive forces represented by “Tianfeng” (the Tianfeng faction). On the other hand, more importantly, the Chinese Communist Party was unwilling to see the national conference proceed smoothly. It was clear that the party-state believed the reform work for Christianity should be led by its trusted ally—Wu Yaozong. If the national conference reached any consensus on church reform, it would undoubtedly become a major obstacle to Wu Yaozong’s reform efforts. While the Promotion Association was preparing for the national conference, Wu Yaozong, with the support of the party-state, launched a signature campaign for the reform declaration. This grand signature campaign and the Promotion Association seemingly became two different paths for the direction of reform in Chinese Christianity.

The competition for leadership in church reform between Wu Yaozong and the Promotion Association finally concluded with the convening of the 14th annual meeting of the Promotion Association in October 1950. To counter Wu Yaozong’s reform declaration, Vice Secretary-General Hayward (Victor E. W. Hayward) requested that the annual meeting issue a “Letter to National Believers,” discussing the Three-Self reform from a religious standpoint. Wu Yaozong was also excluded from the list of the declaration committee. To ensure that the direction of the annual meeting aligned with the vision of the Promotion Association, the Association hoped to retain the attendance rights of missionaries according to the original charter. Additionally, Hayward served as the executive secretary of the procedural committee for the annual meeting and discussed materials on the “Church Reform Issue” of the national conference within the procedures to replace Wu Yaozong’s reform declaration.

On October 9, the East China Military and Political Committee held a symposium, with Christian representatives invited, including: Wu Yaozong, Miao Qiusheng, Jiang Changchuan, Wu Gaozi, Tu Yuqing, Jiang Wenhan, Ai Nian San, Liu Liangmo, Chen Jianzhen, Shao Jingsan, Zheng Jianye, and others. Government representatives included Shu Tong (Minister of the Propaganda Department of the East China Military and Political Committee), Pan Hanyan (Deputy Mayor of Shanghai, Minister of the United Front Department of East China Bureau), Zhou Erfu (Secretary-General of the United Front Department of East China Bureau), Mei Dajun (Cadre of the United Front Department), and Zhou Lixing (Director of the East China Religious Affairs Office). The party and government representative lineup was not light, indicating their authoritative presence. The symposium ultimately made two regulations for the annual meeting: first, foreign members of the Promotion Association’s Executive Committee should not attend the meeting; second, the annual meeting should uphold the objectives of the Christian reform declaration.

Under the active intervention of the CCP, the 14th annual meeting of the Promotion Association, held from October 18 to 25, ultimately passed a resolution supporting the declaration “The Path for Chinese Christianity to Strive in the Construction of the New China” and called on believers nationwide to actively participate in the signature campaign. Additionally, the annual meeting decided to “with utmost determination, complete the Three-Self Movement within five years.” Wu Yaozong was also elected as Vice-President of the National Promotion Association amid the opposing forces of support and opposition. After the meeting, major newspapers in Shanghai reported the annual meeting’s news under titles such as “Severing the Ties Between Christianity and Imperialism.”(2) Three-Self Reform Declaration Signature Campaign 1950 (Xing Fuzeng 2012b: 25-52; Song Jun 2017: 161-211; Wickeri 1990: 127-133; Bush 1970: 177-187; MacInnis 1972: 158-160)

The brewing of the Three-Self Reform Declaration began with the results of Christian leaders’ discussions with Zhou Enlai during their visit to Beijing. The formation of the Christian delegation was related to the preparations for the national Christian conference. It can be said that both were responses from the Chinese church reflecting on their future and reforms after the establishment of the New China, and they also mirrored the twists and turns of the two factions within the church (the Promotion faction and the Tianfeng faction) moving from unity to division.

In October 1949, the Promotion Association held its first Executive Committee meeting after the establishment of New China. It decided to convene a national representative conference of Christianity as soon as possible, to connect various denominations across the country, and to establish a national administrative body. It also decided to organize a delegation to connect with churches nationwide to facilitate the organization of the national conference. At the meeting, Wu Yaozong hoped to convey the significance and resolutions of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference to various regions. Thus, the Promotion Association, the National Association of Young Men and Women Christians of China, and the Christian representatives of the People’s Political Consultative Conference jointly initiated the organization of the delegation.

In April 1950, the North China delegation was announced, consisting of Wu Yaozong, Liu Liangmo (Director of the Youth Association National Committee), Wu Gaozi, Tu Yuqing, Cui Xianxiang, Wang Zizhong (Secretary-General of the North China Christian United Association), Deng Yuzhi (Secretary-General of the National Women’s Youth Association), and others. After visiting Jinan from April 15 to 18, the delegation moved to Beijing. On the 30th, Wu, Liu, Deng, Tu, Cui, Wang, along with Zhao Zichan (Yenching School of Religion) and Zhao Fusang (Deputy Secretary-General of the Beijing Youth Association) held discussions with Deputy Minister Xu Bing of the Central United Front Department on religious issues, during which Wu Yaozong hoped to meet with Zhou Enlai.

On May 2, Zhou Enlai met with Wu Yaozong, Liu Liangmo, Deng Yuzhi, Cui Xianxiang, Wang Zizhong, Tu Yuqing, and Jiang Changchuan among others at the State Council. Chen Qiyuan, Deputy Minister of the Ministry of the Interior of the Central People’s Government, was also present. On May 6, Wu Yaozong and others returned to the State Council for a second discussion. Present were additional attendees including Ai Nian San (Shanghai Chinese Lutheran Church), Yang Xiaopeng (Secretary-General of the Tianjin Christian Youth Association), Ling Yuxiu (Chairperson of the Beijing Women’s Youth Association), Ling Xianyang (Beijing Diocese of the China Anglican Church), Zhao Fusang, and more. Chen Qiyuan and over ten officials from related departments were also in attendance, with discussions lasting more than three hours. At 11 PM on the 13th, Zhou held a third meeting with Wu Yaozong and 19 others, increasing the attendees to include Zhao Zichan, Lu Zhiwei (President of Yenching University), Jiang Changchuan, Gao Fengshan (Principal of Beijing Huiwen Middle School), Pang Huiting (also known as Pang Zhikun, Beijing Congregational Church), Chen Wenrun (Secretary-General of the Beijing Women’s Youth Association), Liu Weicheng (Shanghai Christian Mutual Association), Huo Peixiu (Tianjin Congregational Church), and Zheng Xisan (Chairman of the Tianjin Wesleyan Church and North China Conference). This discussion lasted four and a half hours and concluded at 3:30 AM the following day.

Christian leaders harbored great expectations for meeting with Zhou Enlai. At that time, the most pressing issues for them were the implementation of policies regarding religious freedom and corresponding measures for managing religion. They hoped the central government would issue directives for local people’s governments to strictly enforce the provisions on religious freedom outlined in the common program, to help resolve the current issues facing Christianity. However, Zhou pointed out that the main obstacle facing Christianity in China at present was its relationship with imperialism. The government required religious groups to clear and sever ties with imperialism and to establish self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating churches.

At Zhou Enlai’s request, Wu Yaozong later discussed revising the originally prepared “Preliminary Opinions on Handling Christian Issues,” which was subsequently renamed “The Path for Chinese Christianity’s Future Efforts,” and submitted this revised version to Zhou Enlai after printing. Notably, Wu Gaozi, the official representative of the North China delegation, was not included on the list of Christian leaders Zhou met with three times. Wu later expressed that this was Zhou’s deliberate exclusion of him from the list. At that time, Wu held the positions of Secretary-General of the Promotion Association and Secretary-General of the National Conference Preparatory Committee; Zhou’s disregard for Wu Gaozi clearly indicated the CCP’s favoritism towards Wu Yaozong and a belittlement of the Promotion Association, aiming to undermine the preparations for the national conference.

On May 15, Wu, Liu, Tu, and Ai left Beijing and continued to visit Xi’an. On the train, Wu Yaozong revised “The Path for Chinese Christianity’s Future Efforts” and sent the revised draft back to Beijing. On the 20th, the relevant departments of the Central Committee and the State Council held a discussion to review the fifth revised draft of the declaration. While in Xi’an, Wu Yaozong received Zhou Enlai’s response to the fifth revision and began to invite dozens of fellow believers within the country to co-sign it.Wu Yaozong, in order to garner more church leaders as co-signers and act as initiator, made concessions on certain opinions. For instance, he accepted the suggestions from Shanghai Christian leaders and modified the statement “Christian organizations should adhere to the principle of not using foreign personnel” to “Chinese Christian churches and organizations that still rely on foreign talent and economic assistance should formulate concrete plans to achieve the goal of self-sufficiency in the shortest possible time.” Later, he removed the word “immediately” to avoid misunderstanding that foreign economic relations needed to be severed at once. Furthermore, he deleted the references in the preface of the fifth revision regarding the discussions with Premier Zhou, replacing them with an introduction that emphasized the significance of the declaration itself. Additionally, Beijing church leaders changed the wording in the introduction from “the countries that brought Christianity to China are mainly these imperialist nations” to “the people who brought Christianity to China are mainly from these imperialist countries.” Wu had expressed to the Shanghai church leaders that all modifications would need to be approved by Zhou Enlai. However, regarding the fundamental viewpoint on the relationship between Christianity and imperialism, Wu Yaozong remained uncompromising. The basic stance against imperialism provoked significant resentment among church members. On June 6, the Youth Association held a discussion on the declaration, which met with intense opposition from participants. On July 26, over 20 administrative staff from the Promotion Association requested Wu to remove the clauses in the declaration that criticized imperialism.

The “Reform Declaration” includes four sections: the preface, general tasks, basic policies, and specific measures. The preface primarily acknowledges that Christianity, over its hundred-plus years of history in China, has “established relationships with imperialism, both intentionally and unintentionally, in both tangible and intangible ways.” Even after the establishment of the New China, imperialism still attempted to use Christianity for subversion. The total mission of the Chinese church moving forward is to thoroughly uphold the common program, oppose imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism under government leadership, and strive for the construction of the new China. The basic policies include two aspects: first, the Chinese church must acknowledge the fact of being used by imperialism in the past and eliminate the “imperialistic influence within Christianity”; second, it aims to achieve self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation as its reform goals in the short term. As for specific measures, concrete plans must be developed to explore how to achieve self-renewal in a short time and to improve the church in terms of religious and general operations.

On July 28, Wu Yaozong finalized the last (eighth revision) draft titled “The Path for Chinese Christianity to Strive in the Construction of the New China,” along with the support of 40 Christian initiators, and submitted it for publication, also writing to over a thousand Christian leaders nationwide to solicit their individual signatures in support. Initially, Wu only planned to publish the declaration without intending to turn it into a wide-ranging signature campaign. In June, Zhou Enlai instructed that the declaration be transformed into a signature movement, aiming to leverage mass mobilization to pressure church leadership to accept the reform direction desired by the CCP and to establish Wu’s leadership position within the Christian reform movement.

The first round of signatures was concluded at the end of August 1950, with a total of 1,527 names received. On September 23, the People’s Daily published the full text of the declaration on the front page, including the complete list of the first batch of signatures. An editorial titled “The Patriotic Movement of Christian Leaders” was also published to generate momentum for the declaration. The editorial affirmed the patriotic movement of Christian leaders and highly praised the signature campaign.

By the end of September 1950, “Tianfeng” announced a second batch of names, increasing the total number of signatories to 3,268, adding 1,741 more than the first batch. Following the People’s Daily’s high-profile support for the declaration, various gatherings and symposiums were held nationwide in support of the Three-Self Movement and the Reform Declaration. By early December, the number of signatories had risen to 26,727, and by the end of December, it reached 78,596. By the end of January 1951, nearly 90,000 individuals had signed the Reform Declaration. The involvement and support of political forces played a significant role in the development of the signature campaign for the Reform Declaration. By April 1951, the number of signatures had increased to 200,000. By September 1952, the total number of signatures reached 338,000.

  1. Christian Publishing Review 1951 (Xing Fuzeng 2012b: 229-235)

On March 16, 1951, a Christian publishing conference was held in Beijing, aimed at assisting the Christian publishing industry in overcoming difficulties arising from the cessation of American funding after the outbreak of the Korean War, and to discuss how the publishing industry could sever ties with imperialism and eliminate its influences. The timing of the conference coincided with the nationwide anti-American and anti-imperialist movement, making the elimination of imperialist toxins a key focus of the party-state’s propaganda efforts.

In order to purge the “toxic” elements from Christian publications, the review of Christian books and periodicals was comprehensively initiated after the conference.At that time, the China Christian Publishing Association listed ten standards for the review of publications: (1) authors recognized as traitors to Christianity; (2) direct or indirect, explicitly or implicitly anti-communist, anti-Soviet, or disparaging remarks about the People’s Government and its leaders; (3) containing imperialist, capitalist, or feudal thoughts; (4) emphasizing unrealistic “super-political” ideas that are harmful to patriots; (5) propaganda for imperialism or agents of imperialism; (6) distorting or misunderstanding facts, insulting Chinese culture, and damaging the self-esteem and confidence of the Chinese people; (7) containing inflammatory or divisive ideas that hinder unity between the Chinese people and the government; (8) obstructing the anti-American aid to Korea efforts; (9) indiscriminately endorsing the “universal church” manipulated by imperialism; (10) opposing or undermining the Three-Self Movement.

Under these standards, a large-scale political review of Christian publications was initiated. In May 1951, the publishing association required various publishing institutions to notify the association of their review results by the end of July. At the same time, it called on over 200 church organizations in Shanghai to review their collections and report back to the association. In July, the publishing association received review reports from 17 publishing institutions: Guangxuehui, Youth Association Bookstore, Baptist Bookstore, Tianfeng Society, Shizhao Press, China Bible Society, Chinese Sunday School Association, China Anglican Church Publications Committee, Christian Missionary Society, Guangxie Bookstore, Christian Gospel Bookstore, Salvation Bookstore, Gospel Publications, and the Christian Student Union of various universities (14 of these were in Shanghai), along with the China Christian Bible Society (Hankou) and the Literature Department of the Sichuan Conference of the China Christian Church (formerly the British Baptist Bookstore) and the Monthly on Youth Issues (both located in Chengdu). Among the 649 titles submitted for review by Guangxuehui, Youth Association Bookstore, and the Chinese Baptist Bookstore, 352 were deemed problematic, accounting for 54.2% of the total. These works containing “toxic” elements were either removed from sale or edited.

In June 1951, at a mass protest meeting of the Christian community held in Shanghai, Wu Yaozong, Chairman of the Christian Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee, pointed out that American imperialism used Christian publishing as “an important tool for cultural invasion.” In addition to Guangxuehui and other publishing bodies producing numerous reactionary publications that spread toxins, the Bible also became a tool to narcotize the people and implant enslaving thoughts. At the same protest meeting, Hu Zuyin, the volunteer general secretary of Guangxuehui, also made accusations, claiming that “the imperialists’ motive for founding Guangxuehui was not to promote the gospel of Jesus but to narcotize the thoughts of the Chinese people and eliminate their resistance to imperialism.” He critiqued from the perspective of “cultural invasion,” admitting that among nearly 2,000 books published by Guangxuehui, at least 60% of the materials were translations from American publications, becoming a channel for spreading pro-American, admiration of America, and fear of America ideologies.

In August 1951, the China Christian Publishing Association, Guangxuehui, and the China Bible Society jointly held a conference in Shanghai to expose the crimes of American imperialism exploiting the Christian publishing industry to invade China. At the meeting, Zhang Bohuai, the general secretary of the Publishing Association, pointed out that “many Christian publishing organizations are almost entirely controlled by imperialist agents,” and their publications disseminate imperialist toxins. “Whether they are conservatives or reformists, spiritualists or social gospel advocates,” they are all being utilized by imperialists. This protest meeting targeted not only the various toxins found in printed materials but also pointed the finger at the Bible itself, accusing it of being a tool of imperialist cultural invasion. Miao Qiusheng from the China Bible Society indicated that spiritualist missionaries misinterpret the Bible, spreading toxic ideas of super-politics, super-nationalism, super-globalism, and the notion of “loving one’s enemies,” thus facilitating imperialist invasion. At the meeting, Xie Zonggao and Liu Meili from Guangxuehui also conducted a comprehensive self-critique.

In September 1951, “Tianfeng” published an editorial titled “Purging American Imperialist Influences in the Christian Publishing Industry,” stating that for many years, American imperialists, under the guise of “literary preaching,” published a large number of books and periodicals containing various ideological toxins. Both the review of publications and the accusations against publishing institutions were an integral part of the overall CCP’s efforts regarding Christian reform work. This scrutiny of Christian publications and the campaign of accusations undoubtedly represented a significant event in the history of Christian publishing in China, leading to a heavy blow to the literary publishing industry due to increasing politicization.

  1. First Cadre Training Class for the Christian Three-Self Reform Movement (Xing Fuzeng 1996: 77-80; 2012: 144-145)

In November 1951, the Preparatory Committee established the “First Cadre Training Class for the Christian Three-Self Reform Movement” in Shanghai, with the aim of training a group of cadre, especially active young individuals, to serve as the backbone for deeply promoting the Three-Self reforms.Similar training classes were also established in various locations. The Three-Self Reform Training Class lasted for three months, with 99 participants from different denominational churches and organizations in Shanghai. In addition to the members of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee, the East China and Shanghai Religious Affairs Offices also sent nine colleagues, including Section Chief Yao Piaoting and Director Luo Zhufeng, to assist in the training.

The training class preparatory committee was chaired by Tu Yuqing, with Deng Yuzhi as vice-chair and Huang Peiyong as the secretary. Other committee members included Liu Liangmo, Li Chuwen, Situ Tong, Shi Ruzhang, and Shen Derong. The leading roles in the training class were clearly dominated by the Youth Association members, further demonstrating the significant role of the Youth Association faction.

The training program consisted of five main procedures: (1) clarifying learning objectives and establishing a correct learning attitude; (2) recognizing the greatness and love of the motherland: comparing old and new China, understanding the great achievements and future of the New China, and recognizing how the New China came to be; (3) understanding the nature of imperialism and its exploitation of Christianity for invasion: comprehending the essence of imperialism, understanding American imperialism’s aggression against China (including cultural invasion), and recognizing how imperialism uses Christianity for invasion; (4) understanding the significance of the Three-Self Reform Movement and the government’s religious policies; (5) learning summary: writing personal learning summaries, group exchanges, and group summaries. From the training class procedures, it was evident that participants were required to learn to support the New China in an anti-imperialist and patriotic manner; as believers or church workers, they were expected to uphold the Three-Self Reform Movement as a foundation.

One participant, Shen Yifan, son of Bishop Shen Zigao of the China Anglican Church, criticized his own thinking in his personal learning summary: “I had long believed that my benefits came from imperialism. In church schools, from elementary to university to seminary, I seemed to sail smoothly; thus, I hoped to study abroad in the UK and the US to obtain a degree and seek a high position within the church after returning. The liberation of China shattered all my personal plans, hence I did not love the New China. At the beginning of the liberation, I held a strong desire for a regime change. When the Korean War broke out, I even felt pleased, believing that the ‘change of regime’ was imminent. Through a few years of realistic education, especially during this training, I understood the essence of our motherland, recognized the great achievements and bright future of the country; comparing my happy life today with the past, I emotionally affirmed that my fate is inseparable from that of the New China. I must stand from the people’s perspective to benefit myself.”

On December 11, the training class held a graduation ceremony attended by over 400 people. In his summary report, Li Chuwen pointed out that after the training, participants recognized the necessity of firmly standing on the “people’s position” and clearing the harmful influence of the “Christian position.” Each participant also recognized the importance of self-criticism and ideological learning, while further solidifying their understanding of the significance of the Three-Self Movement.

  1. Adjustment of Colleges and Nationalization of Church Schools 1952 (Xing Fuzeng 2012b: 189-210)

In the early years of the CCP’s establishment, the approach towards the old education system (particularly private education) was one of “protection and maintenance, strengthening leadership, and gradually transforming.” In August 1950, the Central Committee of the Communist Party issued “Guidelines on Catholic and Protestant Issues,” reaffirming that church schools were “private enterprises” and that the government would treat them equally under the principle of “balancing public and private interests.” The government set forth requirements: (1) church schools must include political courses as mandatory; (2) church-run higher education institutions could offer religious electives; (3) no exhibitions or mass meetings promoting or opposing religion should be held at schools; (4) student believers and non-believers should not attack each other over faith issues but should unite to oppose imperialist agents.

By the end of 1950, as the anti-American aid to Korea movement deepened, waves of accusations against imperialism for leveraging church schools for cultural invasion swept across the country, leading to vigorous mass movements within church schools. The central government’s policy on church schools also entered a new stage, with local campaigns against cultural invasion by American imperialism focusing on church schools. By the end of December, both China and the U.S. had frozen each other’s assets and public and private deposits, prompting the CCP to use the new context of the anti-American aid to Korea movement to accuse American imperialism of cultural invasion in China. Beginning in January 1951, all levels of church schools were fully involved in the wave of the anti-American patriotic movement.

From January 16 to 22, 1951, the Ministry of Education held a conference on “Handling Foreign Subsidized Higher Schools.” The attendees included leaders from the Education Departments or Cultural and Educational Committees of East China, Central South, and Southwest administrative divisions, as well as 85 representatives, including chairpersons, principals, teachers, and students from 19 higher education institutions receiving foreign subsidies.At the meeting, Minister of Education Ma Xulun pointed out that church schools were significant activities in the cultural invasion of American imperialism. In response to the United States freezing Chinese assets, the State Council decided to reclaim national educational sovereignty, transforming universities that previously received American subsidies into schools managed by the Chinese people themselves. He criticized church schools as an important avenue for America’s “cultural invasion,” asserting that they disseminated toxic ideas that promoted admiration for America, fear of America, and glorified the decaying “American way of life,” seeking to undermine Chinese national culture and harm the souls of Chinese youth. Lu Dingyi, Deputy Director of the State Council’s Cultural Education Committee, characterized the nature of the issues being addressed as a serious “political struggle against American imperialism.”

Following the conference on managing foreign-subsidized higher education institutions, the Military and Political Committees of East China, Central South, and Southwest convened meetings in February 1951 to address issues concerning secondary schools. By the end of 1951, the Ministry of Education completed a summary report on the handling of schools that received American subsidies. The overall evaluation of church schools in the report was that they were “a cultural invasion base set up by imperialists under the guise of religion in China.”

In the autumn of 1952, the Ministry of Education began large-scale adjustments to higher education institutions, resulting in the dissolution of all church universities, which merged with other institutions. From then on, church universities became a historical relic in China. Simultaneously, the Ministry of Education issued directives to take over all private primary and secondary schools nationwide by 1954. The “nationalization” of private schools marked the formal conclusion of the transitional phase of the old educational system in China. However, the national takeover of private schools was in reality a monumental undertaking involving vast resources. As a result, the plan, originally intended to conclude in 1954, was extended until 1956 before it was essentially completed.

  1. Movement to Accuse Christian Historiography (Xu Yihua 2004: 14-18; Xing Fuzeng 1996: 80-81; 2005: 212-219)

As early as the end of 1951, the renowned Chinese church historian Wang Zhixin conducted self-criticism regarding his book “Outline of the History of Christianity in China,” published in 1940. He indicated that his understanding of Anglo-American imperialism was “very vague” and that there were many imperialist ideological toxins within the text, even falling into reactionary views. Therefore, he “requested all those who possess this book to ruthlessly destroy it, to prevent the continued spread of this ideological toxin.”

From September to November 1953, Wang Zhixin published his research on “Materials on the History of Chinese Christianity (Church History)” in “Xie Jin,” addressing topics such as Christianity and unequal treaties, church incidents, and the Taiping Rebellion. In his writings, he criticized imperialism for using Christianity to invade China, asserting that the activities of missionaries in China “are directly proportional to imperialist aggression against large nations.” Thus, he argued that “the history of Christianity’s missionary endeavors in China over the past 140 years is essentially the history of imperialist aggression against China.”

Despite Wang Zhixin’s attempt to apply the perspective of imperialist aggression to explain Chinese church history, the response was again critical. In February and March 1954, “Xie Jin” published several articles criticizing Wang’s “position” in historiography as seriously problematic. Critics believed that while Wang acknowledged in his writings the fact that Western missionaries utilized Christianity to conduct aggression, he “did not articulate it adequately.” Furthermore, Wang appeared to view the courageous anti-imperialist actions of the Chinese people—the church incidents—as mere “church difficulties,” suggesting that they were due to “the ignorance of the common people and exclusivism among local officials.” This reflected Wang Zhixin’s insufficient awareness of the iron-clad reality that imperialism used religion as a tool for invasion, as well as his failure to clarify the intertwined nature of Christianity and unequal treaties. In an atmosphere of “anti-imperialist” learning within the church, the publication of these articles inevitably produced negative effects and created considerable confusion among believers.

Due to the discussions concerning the issue of imperialism using Christianity for its aggression, the matter was not merely an academic discussion but rather a statement of political stance from the outset. Consequently, Wang Zhixin quickly responded, acknowledging his insufficient understanding and inappropriate wording. He reiterated, “If it is said that these two articles of mine intentionally employed oblique language to criticize while covertly supporting, or that I aimed to secretly insert a leg into the conversation, I can firmly deny any such intention or malicious intent. After three years of study, with the help of my peers, I can now admit my understanding of anti-imperialism and patriotism and the Three-Self Reform has continuously improved.” In addition to Wang Zhixin, Lin Yongyu, the editor of “Xie Jin,” also conducted a review, admitting that when addressing the history of imperialism using Christianity to invade China, if one does not genuinely stand firm on the people’s position and truly grasp scientific historical perspectives and methods, then even with a “mountain of materials,” one cannot produce a true “historical narrative.”From 1953 to 1955, “Xie Jin” published several critical articles concerning the history of Chinese Christianity, primarily assessing the conduct of missionaries (such as the Morrison family, Bishop Burge, Li Lezhi, Li Timoteo, and Pi Zhuwen). Additionally, Tang En compiled a series of historical materials under the theme of “long-term control by American imperialism” covering institutions like China Christian University, China Christian Theological Seminary, and biblical initiatives.

In 1957, Wang Zhixin, using the pseudonym “Er Wang,” discussed the early independence movements of Chinese Christianity. He distinguished between the “old Three-Self” and the “new Three-Self,” evaluating the independence and contextualization movements of the church before and after 1949, along with the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, which sparked debate on how to assess the independence movement. Critics argued that he acknowledged the “old Three-Self” while forgetting the fundamental people’s perspective, suggesting that the evaluation should differentiate between “false Three-Self” and “true Three-Self,” as the independence and contextualization movements prior to 1949 were merely tools for imperialism to carry out “insidious and malicious aggression against China.” In March 1958, the editorial office of “Tianfeng” officially concluded this debate. They ultimately published a response by Wang Yi to close the discussion and publicly released a document titled “Reference Materials on the Issue of Self-Governance,” which had been compiled by the Secretariat during the 1954 National Christian Conference in China. Although the editors stated they “did not intend to conclude this discussion” as historical research can continue to be explored and debated, Wang Yi’s and the document’s content fully reflected the official stance and tone on the matter.

Wang Yi aimed to differentiate between imperialist missionaries and Chinese believers who led and participated in the independence movement. While he condemned the imperialist crimes of missionaries who invaded China and manipulated the Chinese church, he also recognized that the independence movement reflected the demands of Chinese Christians for autonomy in the early 20th century. He even suggested that the issue of “changing labels” should not be generalized. He superficially evaded whether the “old Three-Self” necessarily equated to “false Three-Self,” only asserting that there were elements of “false independence” that had been utilized by missionaries within independent churches. Another distinction Wang sought to make was regarding elements in independent churches that had been labeled as counter-revolutionary. Thus, counter-revolutionaries like Ni Tuosheng and Jing Danying were considered false independent traitors; however, within their churches, there remained a large mass of misled believers who should not be indiscriminately dismissed. Old China, still under the dual pressures of imperialism and feudalism and lacking the leadership and support of the Chinese Communist Party, saw that the achievements of the “old Three-Self” could not be compared with those of the “new Three-Self.”

The evaluations of historical materials and several issues related to the history of Chinese Christianity in the 1950s can be viewed as the outcome of the official attempts to establish a politically correct historical orthodoxy after 1949, described by Xu Yihua as a “historical accusation movement.” The issues involved—how to assess missionaries, missionary movements, independence movements, and contextualization movements—remain contentious topics within the discourse on the history of Chinese Christianity on the mainland.

  1. The Three Major Testimonies and Ten Major Tasks 1956 (Luo Weihong 2014: 712-716)

In January 1956, Mao Zedong convened the highest state council meeting, directing the reinforcement and expansion of the people’s democratic united front, to unite all possible forces. Following Mao’s suggestion, the Central Committee of the CCP held a special meeting regarding intellectuals, and Zhou Enlai’s speech during the meeting fully demonstrated the party’s attitude toward winning over intellectuals. Subsequently, Mao delivered two important speeches in April and May (on “Ten Major Relationships” and “Let a Hundred Flowers Bloom, Let a Hundred Schools of Thought Contend”), reaffirming the CCP’s concern for economic development and its determination to grant intellectuals greater freedom.

In this relatively relaxed political climate, the China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee convened its second plenary (expanded) meeting in March 1956, with a total of 249 attendees and representatives, exceeding the number present at the 1954 National Christian Conference. The meeting added 13 new committee members and 4 standing committee members, including newly participating spiritual faction individuals. Several “spiritual faction” members spoke sequentially, including Jia Yuming, Yang Shaotang, Sun Hanshu, Zuo Furu, and Yan Jiale.

Wu Yaozong delivered a report on the “China Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement,” proposing the well-known “Three Major Testimonies and Ten Major Tasks.” The so-called “Three Major Testimonies” included: the testimony of achieving the Three-Self of the Chinese Church, the testimony of participating in socialism, and the testimony of striving to defend world peace. In explaining the “testimony of the Three-Self,” he stated, “Christianity in China is derived from foreign sources. While spreading the gospel is indeed a good thing, many pious and devoted believers from abroad have offered numerous prayers and contributed significant financial resources to the work of evangelism.”However, unfortunately, imperialism exploited missionary activities to conduct insidious maneuvers, tightly linking the church system with colonialism… After years of effort, the Chinese church was able to break free from the ties of the missionary system and colonialism, and Chinese Christians could unite to establish and thrive within their own churches, embarking on the paths of self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation. He pointed out that the fundamental significance of the Three-Self Patriotic Movement is for Chinese believers to manage their own churches, which “does not equate to the Chinese church severing its spiritual fellowship with Christians from other countries.” Opposing colonialism and the invasions of colonial states is not against “believers and people from other countries.” Therefore, believers from various national churches are members of one body, with Christ as our common head. The fully autonomous, self-supporting, and self-propagating Chinese church enables Chinese believers to bear better witness to the gospel of Christ. He reiterated, “We place great importance on the rich heritage of Christianity over the past two thousand years; this valuable legacy is proof of the work done by the Holy Spirit among believers throughout different eras and regions.” A fully realized Three-Self Chinese church can also contribute to the spiritual fellowship of Christians worldwide.

What Wu articulated regarding the “Three-Self” was no longer centered on struggle and accusation but restored more religious meaning. The relationship between Christianity and imperialism did not possess the intrinsic linkage that characterized the accusation movement, but rather returned to a level of being “exploited.” More importantly, in explaining the mission of the “Three-Self” at that time, there was no previous tendency towards exclusion; he recognized the evangelistic efforts of foreign Christians driven by religious sentiments throughout history and affirmed the spiritual exchanges between the Chinese church and universal Christians, as well as the Chinese church’s inheritance and development of the two-thousand-year church tradition and heritage, thereby imparting new content to the “Three-Self.”

The report on the “Ten Major Tasks” also demonstrated the vision and direction for church development. Among the ten tasks, three were of a political and social nature (actively participating in socialist construction, striving for world peace, and promoting patriotic education), while the other seven were church-centered: consolidating and expanding unity, mutual respect, improving church organization, establishing self-support promotion committees, advancing self-propagation studies, promoting publications, meeting the spiritual needs of believers, strengthening theological research, and cultivating talents. Wu’s propositions regarding “building the church” indeed brought a fresh and relaxed atmosphere to the Chinese church, which had experienced years of political movements and had been raised under the banner of “struggle.”

  1. Overseas Connections of Chinese Christianity 1956-1957 (Matsutani 2016: 175-193; Zhao Tianen, Zhuang Wanfang 1997: 94-95)

After the establishment of the CCP, missionaries from Europe and America withdrew from China, marking the end of the missionary era. Simultaneously, under the political climate of anti-imperialist patriotism, the Three-Self Movement not only aimed to sever ties between the Chinese church and imperialism but also fully implemented the accusation movement against Western missionary agencies, labeling them as tools of imperialism in the invasion of China. In this context, the overseas connections of Chinese Christianity were essentially at a standstill. In 1952 and 1955, the British Archbishop of Canterbury, Hewlett Johnson, and a delegation from the British Quakers visited China at the invitation of the Committee for the Defense of World Peace.

As for external visits, they were limited to Eastern European communist countries; for example, in 1952 and 1954, Liu Liangmo and Bishop Zheng Jianye were invited to attend representative conferences by the Christian Democratic Union in East Germany.

Starting in 1956, the Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee made significant breakthroughs in international connections. In terms of visits, in March 1956, the national Three-Self Movement invited Gustav Nystrom from the Evangelical Alliance of Switzerland, Josef L. Hromadka from Czechoslovakia, Bishop Jano Peter of the Reformed Church of Hungary, and Bishop Rajah Manikam from the India Lutheran Church to visit China. In May 1956, Bishop Hall of the Hong Kong Anglican Church visited China. Between May and October, visits were received from officials of the Japan Christian Women’s Association, the principal of the St. Guildford Children’s Home of the Hong Kong Anglican Church, the secretary-general of the United Churches of India, and representatives from the Anglican Church of Australia. From February to September 1958, delegations from the Theological Seminaries of Hamburg University in West Germany, the Japan Christian Visiting Group, and the Hungarian Christian delegation visited China.As for overseas visits, in the summer of 1956, Bishop Ding Guangxun attended the preparatory meeting for the Lambeth Conference of the worldwide Anglican Communion in the UK and later participated as an observer in the meeting of the Central Committee of the World Council of Churches (WCC) held in Hungary. During this period, the general secretary of the World Council of Churches suggested that Ding change his status from observer to representative, which Ding did not agree to. The WCC had been accused of being a tool of imperialism during the accusation movement. After the meeting, Ding traveled to West Germany to attend the World Christian Student Federation meeting (with Zhao Fusang and Huang Peiyong also participating). In September, the Chinese Christian delegation went to East Germany to participate in the eighth representative conference of the Christian Democratic Union. In 1957, a representative of the China Christian Church visited India, and later a delegation from the national Three-Self Movement (including Ding Guangxun, Huang Peiyong, Shen Derong, and Chen Jianxun) made a reciprocal visit to Hungary, where the Reformed Church of Hungary conferred an honorary doctorate on Ding Guangxun at the Berenyi Seminary. In November, the Chinese Christian delegation visited Hungary at the invitation of the Hungarian Christian Association.

It is evident that from 1956 to 1957, prior to the Anti-Rightist Campaign, the national Three-Self organization made efforts to restore some connections with the universal church, both in terms of hosting visits and making outbound trips. In alignment with Wu Yaozong’s “Three Major Testimonies and Ten Major Tasks” regarding “promoting fellowship and friendship among Christians and peoples of all nations,” the national Three-Self organization established the China Christian International Liaison Committee in May 1956. The countries and organizations they engaged with were no longer limited to Eastern European countries; they even attempted to connect with overseas denominations (such as the Anglican Church) and the very groups they had accused during the Korean War (the World Council of Churches). This indicates that under the relatively relaxed political atmosphere of 1956-57, Chinese Christianity was exhibiting breakthroughs.

However, following the Anti-Rightist Movement, the political landscape in China tilted left again, and the aforementioned space for engagement dissipated, leading to the inability of the China Christian International Liaison Committee to continue its operations. In 1958, Bishop Ding Guangxun canceled his plans to attend the Lambeth Conference of the Anglican Communion in London.

  1. Publication of “Holy Light” 1957 (Wang Weifan 2013: 2)

According to a resolution from the second national conference in 1954: “Under the common goal of opposing imperialism, patriotism, and love for religion, we aim to promote the unity of all believers within the Christian church, further advance the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, and adopt the principle of mutual respect regarding differences in faith, systems, and rites among various churches.” At the end of 1956, Bishop Ding Guangxun suggested that “Nanjing Three-Self” publish a devotional periodical. The publication invited Jia Yuming to name it “Holy Light,” with plans for it to be a bimonthly. The editorial team included Rev. Li Chenzhong (from the spiritual faction, a graduate of Hunan Bible School and former pastor of the Christian Church in Sucheng, Anhui Province) and Wang Weifan, a graduate of Nanjing Union Theological Seminary.

The purpose of “Holy Light” was to “expound the truth of the gospel of Christ; nurture the spiritual lives of brothers and sisters in various places; and provide materials for church workers for Bible study, devotions, sermons, and pastoral experiences.” The inaugural issue was published on March 1, 1957, with contributions from authors including Jia Yuming, Jiang Peifen, Yang Shaotang, Sun Hanshu, Han Peter, Cai Wenhao, and Li Chenzhong, most of whom had backgrounds in the spiritual faction. By 1957, there were about 5,000 subscribers.

However, starting in early summer 1958, the religious community in Jiangsu Province launched a campaign against rightists. The bimonthly “Holy Light” ceased publication after its seventh issue (February 1958). Nonetheless, the “Editor’s Note” in that issue did not express an intention to cease publication, continuing to call for subscriptions and submissions.

  1. Joint Worship 1958 (Luo Weihong 2014: 729-740)

The year 1958 marked a watershed in the development of the Chinese church. As Mao Zedong initiated the Great Leap Forward, signaling a transition towards a communist society, it also marked the advent of an era characterized by “adventurism” and an extreme leftist approach. In an atmosphere of nationwide adventurous sentiment, the communist utopia seemed poised to be realized within Chinese society.

The existence of the Chinese Christian church, especially the multidenominational structure, was viewed as an impediment to the socialist path during the Great Leap Forward and faced severe impacts. In the latter half of 1958, “Tianfeng” began characterizing the organizational structures of Chinese churches and church groups as “essentially maintaining a semi-colonial appearance from the era of imperialist domination,” with “the legacy of imperialism being preserved intact.” This so-called semi-colonial appearance included: (1) the distorted distribution of churches; (2) sectarian opposition and schisms within the church; (3) the issue of agencies from the era of imperialist missions; (4) the reactionary control of churches.The article further states: “We must thoroughly change the semi-colonial appearance of Chinese Christianity in the continuing and deepening process of socialist education movements.” Under this situation, the Three-Self organization implemented joint worship across the country under the guise of changing this “semi-colonial” appearance.

Zhejiang Province was one of the first regions to respond. Between April and May 1958, the Ningbo church proposed four rectification plans, advocating: (1) economic coordination and unified support; (2) unified arrangement of pastoral personnel; (3) unified management of church properties; and (4) the establishment of productive organizations where pastoral personnel could engage in production. The three major church institutions in Hangzhou (the Zhejiang Conference of the China Christian Church, the Zhejiang Diocese of the China Anglican Church, and the Zhejiang-Shanghai Baptist Council) also decided to conduct joint operations in August. In Wenzhou, a citywide expanded meeting of the Three-Self Movement was held from May 26 to 27, 1958, where it was decided to abolish the original denominational names, unify personnel, eliminate denominational distinctions, and unify economic management. In terms of faith traditions, baptism, immersion, anointing, and Sabbath observance were free, but communion was to be held monthly, advocating the use of multiple cups during communion. The meeting also resolved to begin joint worship on the first Sunday of July. During the Great Leap Forward, only six churches remained active in the city, including Chengxi Church (Methodist), Canghe Lane Church (China Christian Self-Governing Church), Huayuan Lane Church (China Inland Mission), Yongguang Church (China Jesus Church), Toushou Naolou Church (Seventh-day Adventist), and Xichengxia Church (Christian Gathering Place). All other churches ceased activities. After July 1958, as church unification progressed, only Chengxi Church served as the venue for Christian activities in the entire city.

The model of joint worship quickly spread from Wenzhou to various parts of the country. Grassroots congregations from different denominations adopted methods of uniting with other congregations in the same region, significantly reducing the number of grassroots congregations. For instance, in Shanghai, the number of churches decreased from 208 in 1950 to only 22 after the implementation of joint worship, and later further reduced to 8. In Beijing, the number shrank from 65 in 1949 to just 4 by 1958. In Jiangsu Province, by the end of 1957, there were 321 Christian churches open in the province, but by early 1959, this number dwindled to 83, with Nanjing’s larger churches consolidated from 23 to 7. In Sichuan Province, the total number of churches before 1958 was 134, which was reduced to 67 after the implementation of joint worship. In Qingdao, the number was cut down from 50 to 10, and then further down to 7. In Hangzhou, 28 churches were merged into 5, which later reduced to 1 as pastoral staff participated in training, and after the training, a total of 3 churches opened (which was later reduced to 2).

The implementation of joint worship effectively dismantled the denominational system and reinforced the Three-Self organization’s control over Christianity. Since the founding of the CCP, the national and regional frameworks of various denominations had remained unchanged, and the development of the Three-Self organization was aimed at replacing the leadership of these denominations with reformist figures. However, with the existing denominational structures still in place, the organizational autonomy of the denominations was maintained to a certain degree, and the national Three-Self organization found it difficult to completely interfere with the economic and personnel authority of each national institution. After the implementation of joint worship, denominational organizations ceased to operate, and all levels of the Three-Self organizations became the superior leading bodies of urban and rural churches. This comprehensive leadership of the “Three-Self” over the “church” fundamentally transformed the denominational system of Chinese Christianity and reflected the party-state’s proactive use of the political situation to implement a more radical religious policy. This was also an inevitable outcome of the highly centralized and unitary leadership system of the state.

During the Great Leap Forward, Wenzhou became a pilot “no-religion zone,” and policies aimed at eliminating religion were officially put on the agenda. The joint worship promoted across the country significantly reduced the number of grassroots churches, with many pastors being reassigned to work in factories. All of these actions undoubtedly formed part of the “elimination of religion” policy.

  1. Political Movements and Christianity in the 1950s (Ying 2014)

Since the founding of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, the CCP has successfully consolidated its one-party dictatorship through a series of political and mass movements (suppressing counter-revolutionaries, resisting U.S. aggression and aiding Korea, ideological remolding, the Three Anti and Five Anti movements, purging counter-revolutionaries, and the Anti-Rightist Campaign). Simultaneously, economic transformations (such as land reform and collectivization) realized the state’s control over land and other agricultural resources. The state gained control and allocation rights over the vast majority of resources within society. For a social member to obtain the most basic conditions for survival, it was necessary to acquire the corresponding resources from the state, thus forming a highly unified total society. Under this model of integration, the party-state penetrated deeply and widely into grassroots social life at an unprecedented level and scope, causing local social integration to be subordinate to the political integration at the state level, achieving a high degree of consistency between the two.The effectiveness of this integration model, however, came at the cost of destroying social independence and autonomy. In other words, the “totalism” that Zou Dong referred to, where the power of political institutions can intrude into and control various levels and sectors of society without limits, reached its peak during the Cultural Revolution but developed through successive political movements starting from the early years after the founding of the country in the 1950s.

In the 1950s, as China moved towards totalitarian politics, every political movement, regardless of its purpose or the target of its actions, inevitably affected the Chinese church because Christianity was viewed as a heterogeneous force associated with imperialist aggression. For example, during the early political movements of the CCP’s regime, land reform not only mobilized urban churches to participate in rural land reforms, expressing their support through direct involvement but also led to rural churches being occupied and church properties being confiscated under the accusation of being “landlords.” The anti-American aid to Korea movement targeted the fight against “American imperialism” and made Christian churches and their associated endeavors a focal point for liquidating and denouncing American “cultural invasion.” Although the ideological reform was primarily aimed at intellectuals transitioning from the old society, Christian individuals were also required to learn to transform their thoughts through criticism and self-criticism, thus purging the “religious toxins” of imperialism. The Three Anti Campaign focused on “anti-corruption, anti-waste, and anti-bureaucracy,” targeting cadres in government agencies, while the Five Anti Campaign referred to “anti-bribery, anti-tax evasion, anti-theft of state assets, anti-quality deterioration, and anti-stealing national economic secrets,” primarily aimed at capitalists. However, large figures in Christian endeavors were also accused of violating the “Five Anti” campaign. Moreover, the Three Anti and Five Anti campaigns became intertwined with ideological reform, evolving into various forms of “ideological big tigers.” These movements were interconnected and conducted through intense mass mobilization methods (such as accusation meetings), aiming to expose and denounce class enemies (American imperialist lackeys, bad elements, and big tigers).

The mobilization methods mentioned continued to be applied in political movements after the mid-1950s, including the purge of counter-revolutionaries in 1955 and the Anti-Rightist Campaign (or Socialist Education) in 1957-58. Within these nationwide political movements, Christianity was inevitably required to purge counter-revolutionaries and rightists hiding within the church.

During the Great Leap Forward in 1958, Christianity had to fully cooperate with the transition to a communist society and undergo comprehensive changes. During this period, churches across various regions actively engaged with the party, and pastors were characterized as members of the exploiting class; even religious thoughts were temporarily classified as “toxins.” In this context, joint worship further dismantled the denominational system of churches and, under the guise of merging, closed numerous churches nationwide.

Key Theological Issues

  1. Cleansing the Poison of Imperialism (邢福增2012b: 127-130, 136-140; Ying 2022; Wickeri 1990: 133-146)

In January 1951, 吳耀宗 wrote in 《天風》 that the issue of “self-propagation” is not about “who propagates,” but rather about “what is being propagated.” If having Chinese people propagate the Gospel signifies the completion of “self-propagation,” then the problem is clearly solved. Most Western missionaries have already retreated, and the task of spreading the Gospel has naturally been passed on to Chinese believers. However, 吳 firmly believed that the issue of self-propagation is not that simple. His concern was: What should Chinese believers “propagate”? If what is being propagated by Chinese individuals is still the same set of beliefs originating from the West, then the Chinese church remains deeply influenced by the poison of Western imperialism. Therefore, he said: “If we truly want to achieve ‘self-propagation,’ Chinese believers must discover the treasures of Jesus’s Gospel for themselves, break free from the shackles of Western theology, reassess escapist thinking, and create a theological system of their own; only then can we express the true spirit of Jesus’s Gospel within the context of the new China.”

吳’s interpretation of self-propagation indicates that the precondition for reasserting the “true spirit” of the Christian Gospel in the new China is to cleanse the influence of imperialist poison on Christianity. The “poison” mainly involves two aspects: (1) Reformism; (2) Supersessionism.

Regarding reformism, it mainly targets the social gospel movement related to the reform of Chinese society. The Christian Youth Association can be said to represent this aspect. On July 27, 1951, the National Council of the China Christian Youth Association and the National Council of the Young Women’s Christian Association held an accusation conference, where 吳耀宗 accused American imperialism of using “reformism” to invade China through the youth association. What is reformism? 吳 stated, “Any approach that prettifies the surface, evades class struggle, and opposes the use of revolutionary means to overthrow the old social system is reformism.” He pointed out that the greatest task of the Chinese revolution is to overthrow the three major enemies: imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism. However, the youth association not only did not oppose these three enemies but also attempted to “deceive the masses” through public education, rural services, and labor welfare, thus “blur the Chinese people’s perception of the dominance of these three major enemies.” The young women’s association representative 鄧裕志 also indicated during the accusations that the youth association advocated a “third way,” attempting to propose “Christian social democracy” outside of capitalism and communism. This assertion aims to “divide the revolutionary forces of the colonized people” and foster accomplices for imperialism.

Another figure, 劉良模, who became a backbone of the national Three-Self Movement, stated in a January 1952 seminar on the Christian ideological transformation that reformism seeks to “transform society through gradual legal means, without revolution,” which is essentially “reactionary.” Because it “dissipates revolutionary力量, numbs the people’s resistance to reactionary rule, and prolongs the lifespan of reactionary domination.”

If the target of the critique against this “toxin” of reformism is the “social gospelists” of Chinese Christianity, then for the “spiritualists,” the toxin they spread is “supersessionism.” According to Christian Political Consultative Conference representative 劉良模 in March 1951, “supersessionism” is the attitude that “Christians should not concern themselves with politics.” He believed, “This is an excuse used by a minority of bad elements within Christianity to deceive believers and to incite discord between the believers and our people’s government.” The term “supersessionism” itself represents “a form of political attitude.” Later, 吳耀宗 also pointed out, “American imperialism has exploited the ‘spiritualists’ within Christianity, donning a façade of extreme mysticism, distorting the Bible, spreading poisonous thoughts of ‘supersessionism,’ anti-communism, anti-Soviet, and anti-people, attempting to incite conflict between countless devout Christians and our people’s government.”

As a member of the spiritualist faction, 郑证光, an elder from the 福州 meeting place, acknowledged that the spiritualist faction appears “transcendent,” “deceiving socially passive Christians with the lie that Christians are heavenly and should not ‘concern’ themselves with earthly matters, propagating the toxic idea that Christians should be ‘supersessionist’!” As a result, Christians are unwilling to resist the oppressive ruling class and the aggression of imperialism. After the establishment of the new China, “supersessionism” also diluted the will of the people’s revolution and weakened the strength of the people’s revolution.

By the end of 1952, the Jinling Union Theological Seminary, formed by the merger of 11 theological colleges and Bible schools in East China, was officially established, and published the 《金陵协和神学志》 in September 1963, undertaking the task of constructing a new era of Christian theology.副院长誠質怡在《发刊词》中说：“过去基督教被人利用作为侵略中国的工具，以致荒谬的、叛道离经的思想竟作为‘神学’传播，纯正的信仰被扭曲了。今后我们不但要肃清毒素，而且还要积极地弘扬耶稣基督拯救的真理，与爱国爱教的广大信徒们联合起来，推进三自革新运动。”

  1. 吳耀宗的神学革新与再思（邢福增2014；2016）

作为基督教三自运动的发起人，吳耀宗认为，在摆脱西方神学的羁绊，清算逃避现实的思想后，中国基督徒必须创造自己的神学系统。他对中国神学的构建及革新工作抱有极大的期望，认为此乃中国基督教能否在新中国立足的关键。这涉及两条并行的神学战线：（1）是对他心目中“逃避现实”的神学思想作清算，从而革新基督教神学；（2）是建立基督教与唯物主义之间的调和关系。这都可以追溯到吳在“解放”前的神学根源。

吳耀宗的革新基督教思想中，昂扬政治上反帝爱国的同时，实际上也结合了宗教或神学的变革因素。他在1952年指出，在研究帝国主义对传教运动的影响后，让他清楚看到教会在解释基督教方面所犯的错误，已经偏离了耶稣的教导，其中最大的问题，就是过分强调了上帝的“超在”而忽略了上帝的“内在”。前者突显了人性的罪恶，因此有意无意地否定了上帝如何通过人来工作。同时，对罪恶的关注，最终使神学偏向末世论——末后的审判及永生。吳耀宗主张持平地结合两者，即所谓“辩证底结合”，从而纠正传统神学与封建主义及资本主义打成一片的流弊，以崭新态度面对当前的社会变革，并在革命运动中发现上帝的旨意。

针对这套新神学体系的内容，吳耀宗提出五方面的建议：（1）重建既超在又内在的基督教上帝观，并有效地应用在当前的世界局势；（2）承认上帝启示的普遍性，即使不认识上帝的人，也是实现上帝旨意的途径。必须打破“基督徒”与“非基督徒”，或“有神”与“无神”的不同阵营，基督徒及有神论者并非垄断对真理的认识；（3）把自然及社会视作上帝工作的场景，并将自然及社会科学以及支配自然及社会的规律视为上帝工作的方式；（4）对人应有新的信心，上帝通过人工作，对人性既乐观又不盲目乐观；（5）不应将上帝的国视作遥远及将来的境界，只要对现实有清醒的认识，上帝国就在当下开始。

在吳耀宗的思想中，与基督教神学革新同时推进的另一条战线，就是基督教与共产主义的调和问题。其实，他早在30年代就已开始探索两者的关系，到40年代出版的《没有人看见过上帝》，便详细地从理论层面阐释两者并不矛盾，甚至各有所长，可以相互补充。关于调和，又涉及基督徒与唯物论者两个不同进路。

就基督徒而言，他希望向基督徒解释上帝与真理的关系，从而化解基督教与共产主义、唯物论之间的矛盾与对立。同时，在肯定共产革命所追求的无阶级、无剥削社会理想，其实也是基督教所认同的。因此，基督徒首要及迫切地作的，并非否定这些“教会外”的美善与真理，因为“所有的善都来自上帝”。他以中国的解放为例，指这是“一個惊天动地的故事”，而解放后的建设及抗美援朝斗争的胜利，也是“世界历史的一个奇迹”。他批评解放后仍有基督徒否认非基督徒所作的好事，这根本违背了耶稣基督的教导。他以马太福音二十五章为例，指在审判的日子，分别“义人”和“不义的人”，主要是看他们是否遵行上帝的旨意。

针对唯物论者，吳主要从三方面向他们说明：（1）基督教并不违反唯物论：基督教虽不是唯物论，但可以接受唯物论的真理，而基督教里面必须具备的唯心论成分，也不一定与唯物论冲突。(2) Christianity and Materialism Can Complement Each Other: Theoretically, 吳一直相信上帝真理的绝对与独特性可以补充或纠正唯物论的弱点与偏差，然而，由于现实基督教的腐败，信仰所具备的批判性无法发挥。无论如何，中共在短短三十年间能够“领导中国人民，完成这个古老国家的革命事业”，在行动实践上，已经证明比基督教更具优势。如何将理论结合实践，重新思考两者的关系，正是他所关注的；（3）基督教会否像唯物论所判定的那样，最终自然灭亡？一方面，他认为步向消亡的宗教只会是迷信的、下等的宗教，如同马克思所形容的“人民的鸦片”式、逃避现实的宗教；另一方面，吳耀宗关心的不是宗教的形式是否存在，而是宗教的本质。他深信，如果宗教的本质是对“真理”的追求、景仰及服从，那么这种“高级宗教”表现出的宗教情操是“不随宗教形式的改变而改变的”。

可以说，唯有经过革新的基督教神学才能与唯物论调和。然而，革新神学的努力却成为属灵派不参加，甚至反对三自运动的争论基础（革新等于革掉属灵派信仰）。中共出于团结属灵派的需要，必须约束吳在这方面的思考，以免妨碍三自运动的进展。同时，调和基督教与唯物论的主张，又触碰到中共意识形态的禁区，唯物与唯心论者可以在政治上合作，而非强行尝试在理论层面构建两者的调和与互补。因此，吳在建国初期关于新神学构建的两条战线，均不见容于现实政治，受党国制约而无法展开。

  1. Christianity’s “Débâcle” in China? 1951 (邢福增2012b: 285-286, 303-306; Hood 1991: 185-222)

In 1951, the renowned missionary journal 《国际宣教评论》(International Review of Missions) published an article authored by “中国传教士” (A China Missionary), which sparked significant controversy and reaction within the Western missionary camp. The article was titled 《基督教在华传教潰败芻议》 (First Thoughts on the Débâcle of Christian Missions in China), summarizing the author’s reflections on the failures of Western missionary efforts in China after his withdrawal from the mission field. At the time, many missionaries were fleeing China, marking the official “loss” of the largest missionary field in the world and drawing a conclusion to over a century of Western missionary work in China. “中国传教士” believed that the mission agencies and missionaries held some responsibility for the end of the missionary movement in China, and its “défaite” (Débâcle) was not without the “judgment” (judgment) of God.

The true identity of “中国传教士” is David M. Paton, an English Anglican who had served in Chongqing and Fujian, and returned to his home country in January 1951. Shortly after his return, he participated in a Far Eastern consultation convened by the International Missionary Council, where he presented seven lessons learned. The article 《基督教在华传教潰败芻议》 is based on this foundation. In 1953, he published the book 《基督教传教运动和上帝的审判》(Christian Missions and the Judgment of God) in London, further elaborating his views.

He noted that the end of the missionary era can be understood in terms of God’s will, the work of Satan, political factors, tragedy, or a combination of these various interpretations. However, he preferred to use the term “débâcle” because the conclusion of the missionary work represented a judgment from God to highlight its inherent weaknesses. 裴氏进一步阐释其观点背后的神学基础，他认为，圣经的教导曾把“历史中的邪恶”视作上帝的惩罚，而历史的动荡过程也是显露上帝目的的场景。上帝的作为不仅彰显于宗教的世界，也同样可见于共产中国。同时，教会不仅是上帝施恩的对象，也是审判的目标。因此，他确信上帝正通过政治和社会革命来讲话。虽然中共不承认甚至否认上帝的存在，但它以不可抗力（force majeure）促成了在华传教时代的结束，这应被理解为上帝旨意的执行。

裴氏深知其观点的争议性，因此他强调中共是上帝审判的执行者，并不代表中共是站在上帝一边（on God’s side）。正如上帝曾通过亚述人来审判以色列，但最终亚述人也消失于历史之中，而上帝的选民却仍存活。此外，他还指出，上帝的审判与恩慈其实是不可分割的，基督徒同时是义人和罪人，因此上帝审判的目的是要让传教士认识到自己的失败与骄傲，并寻求忏悔。

裴大卫的观点，引发了传教士圈子广泛的讨论。不少传教士承认传教运动在中国的确有失败之处，但却反对将这些失败与上帝的审判相提并论。例如，魏士德（Charles C. West，美北长老会）反对“审判”的定位，因为即使差会政策有可供检讨之处，但他们在中国建立的机构的消失、中国教会领袖在压力下的屈服、普世合一团契及基督教社会见证的崩解，根本可以视为一场“悲剧”（tragedies）。不少“好的东西”在反帝国主义的名义下受到攻击，这又如何能够理解为上帝的审判？丁克生（S. H. Dixon，英国循道会）认为，传教运动的结束完全是政治权力因素的结果，因此不应视作对传教团体及其工作的直接审判。虽然华传教经验肯定有不足之处，但即使他们奉行更简朴的生活、完全的委身、更加适应中国人的生活，甚至对社会问题有更全面的政策，最终也难逃共产主义的指控。因为中共根本不允许国家以外存在独立的敌对团体。

海维德（Victor E. W. Hayward，英国浸信会，曾任中华全国基督教协进会副总干事）也坚决反对上帝藉中共审判传教士失败的这种说法。他主张从社会和政治的角度来理解中国发生的一切，而不是问上帝为何将传教士带离中国，为什么上帝使用中共这个“无意识的仆人”（unwitting servants）来完成这件重要的事情？他将上帝的审判理解为危机中上帝给予人们发现自身失败、赎罪、更新信心与委身、校正方向、学习如何更好地事奉上主的机会。海维德承认传教运动有许多值得检讨之处，实际上，他对在华传教工作的经验，与裴氏并没有明显的分歧。问题在于，究竟传教运动是否全盘失败？中共又是否只是上帝审判传教士的工具。

  1. 在华传教运动的反思与教训 （邢福增2012b: 287-303, 309-311；Ling 1999: 181-218; Bush 1970: 61-68）

1951至52年间传教士的全面撤离，正式标志着近代基督新教来华传教运动的结束。在这140多年的历史中，1900年义和团运动、1925至27年的北伐，以及1936至45年的抗日战争，是传教运动面临的三次最重大冲击。然而，即使在27年中大量传教士从内地撤至上海，甚至离开中国，但在局势稳定后，他们随即回到中国。在抗战后期，差会也积极规划战后的重建工作。 然而，传教士对1950年代初的撤离，并没有丝毫重返中国的希望。正如毕範宇所说：“差会永远不可能以旧有的形式回到中国。不论共产朝代的长短，外国传教士的地位已永久改变。他们不能再回去‘控制’学校、医院或教会。反对基督教的中国人甚至不会欢迎西方的宗派重返，或差会借人事、财务及物业来控制教会。”这不仅是“传教时代的结束”（End of Missionary Era），也是一个时代的终结。许多从中国撤离的传教士及差会组织，对在华传教运动进行了全面的检讨，形成了一次大规模的“宣教再思”（Re-thinking Mission）。

有关反省涉及两个重点：（1）如何解释中国共产党运动的成功？基督教是否要为无法阻止其成功而负责？中国基督教神学究竟出了什么问题？对此，有传教士将共产主义成功的原因归咎于基督教忽略了中国社会的需要，但也有相反的人指控社会福音淡化了福音的真义。这种相互批评的立场，基本上仍是延续了20世纪自由主义与基要主义的争论。传教士不约而同地藉此机会检讨神学的成效。不过，神学本色化的策略是否有失误是一回事，基督教神学能否成功阻止共产革命又是另一回事。实际上，共产党的成功关键，不论是填补了文化真空，还是领导了民族主义革命，在传教士眼中，都假设了基督教是共产主义的竞争对手。基督教的责任，就是要防止共产主义的传播。然而也有传教士指出，基督教真的可以阻挡中国革命的洪流吗？但如果抱着基督教有能力阻挡共产主义的想法，显然是不切实际的。

（2）传教士如何面对中共关于帝国主义及文化侵略的指控？他们在文化、社会及政治方面的过失，是否代表他们是帝国主义的工具？对此，有传教士坚信基督的福音是美善的。传教运动与帝国主义的偶然关系确实是不幸，许多针对基督教的批评，例如传教士受帝国主义政府雇用协助侵华，或传教事业的目的是为了奴化国人，都是毫无依据的。也有人承认，整段历史清楚揭示，不平等条约及保护传教条款使基督教无法摆脱所谓帝国主义分子的指控。在不平等条约的背景下，传教士根本无法逃避帝国主义指控。传教士应切实检讨过去在中国及其他传教工场里，教会对政府的依赖关系。不过，他们也承认，绝大部分传教士都受到西方文化优越感的影响，将福音与英美生活方式等量齐观。他们即使流露出对中国文化的欣赏，往往也是肤浅、高傲及策略性多于客观认知。中国教会领袖愈“西化”，其地位也愈稳妥，结果使他们完全断裂于传统文化的根源。传教士的西方优越感，也反映在他们在华生活水平的问题上。传教士不约而同地承认，他们在中国的待遇与福利，远高于中国同工。许多传教士承认，他们并不能实践在生活上接近中国人，且与本地同工保持一致的承诺。在教会事工方面，不少传教士对未能帮助中国教会实现自治、自养而感到遗憾。传教士也为其家长式传统感到懊悔。然而，即使部分传教士在反思时提及“帝国主义”一词，但实际上他们对这个概念的理解，却并不完全等同于中共的定义。对于对在华传教运动持较严厉批判的传教士所关注的，所针对的不外乎是个别传教士与政府的关系，以及整体传教运动所处的殖民扩张时代，以及不平等条约中的保护传教问题；对于共产主义就传教运动与殖民运动及帝国主义之间的同质性指控，他们也有所保留。

此外，1951年6月，美国基督教协进会海外布道部（Division of Foreign Missions, The National Council of the Churches of Christ in the USA）于纽约召开为期三天的会议，并向参加的回国传教士派发问卷，问卷针对回国传教士提出了三个问题：（1）您从中国传教经验中学到了什么教训，避免在其他宣教工场重蹈覆辙？（2）您学习了哪些教训，可以引入其他宣教工场？（3）如果您能够回到中国，您在生活及工作上会有哪些改变？共有152位传教士填写问卷，最后整理成“Lessons to be Learned from the Experiences of Christian Missions in China”报告。

不少传教士离开中国后，转到世界各地传教。有传教士指出，数以千计的传教士将带着特别的经验到各地，这不仅是地理上的转移，否则在中国发生的事情难免会在其他地区重现。他们要记着在中国学习到的教训：传教士必须紧记普世宣教的见证必须实现本色教会，教会的自养也是传教工作不可或缺的目标。同时，传教士要具备应变能力，面对各种变局，不要将工作与自己的名字等同，并借此建立自己的安全感。

根据80年代中国基督教人士（丁光训、沈以藩、汪维藩）的表述，在1953至1956年间，中国教会曾开展了一场“神学群众运动”。沈以藩指出，这场“神学再思”主要围绕着四个问题：（1）世界究竟是由撒但掌管，还是由上帝统治和管理？（2）人的本性是否全然败坏，还是仍在某方面体现了“上帝的形象”？（3）信与不信的关系；（4）信仰与道德的关系。实际上，“神学群众运动”一词并未见于1950年代教会文献，相信是出于80年代的整理与诠释。不过，其中所讨论的问题，确实是在当时由三自组织所提出的。有关于讨论，与其说是主动的神学构建，不如说是被动地对反三自属灵派神学的一种回应或抗衡。其针对的对象，主要是倪柝声及王明道。

先是1953年8月，《天风》刊载署名“一群读者”的文章，批判上海福音书房在1953年4月出版的《圣洁没有瑕疵》（该书原为倪氏在1939年的讲稿），指其内容对新社会多有诋毁，假借属灵的虔诚来反对新中国及三自革新。这群读者驳斥书中强调的“源头问题”，指有人暗示“新中国的源头不好”，是对国家人民的诽谤。他们又批评书中指“教会从来没有罪”，是否认帝国主义利用基督教。还指其中提及“撒但有一个工作，就是控告”，是针对三自革新中的控诉运动。文章最后指“教会中竟有人却妄用了信仰自由和出版自由，直接地或暗示地来散布毒素，对祖国不利、对教会不利、对三自革新不利。我们不明白，今天的贤明的政府当局和三自革新当局为何不以负责任的态度，对这种情况给予适当的处理？”

此外，金陵协和神学院院长丁光训也在1953年9月的《金陵协和神学志》上撰文，批评某些人将“因信称义”片面扩大，不仅形成信与不信的对立，更会产生信徒与人民对立的情绪。丁提出，神学应首先与人民和好，才能为主作见证及传播福音。因此，基督徒必须首先爱自己可爱的祖国，与祖国的人民接近、结合，深入到人民中去，“去思想他们所思想的，去关心他们所关心的，去爱他们所爱，去恨他们所恨的”。唯有与人民建立共同点，才能向他们传播福音。

1954年1月，吴耀宗也不点名地驳斥《圣洁没有瑕疵》的观点，指书中谓：“神所注意的问题，不是好坏的问题，而是源头的问题”“人是问有爱心没有爱心，但神是问爱心从哪里来的”，是“荒谬绝伦的说法”。吴举好撒玛利亚人的故事，指出耶稣的标准是，“看一件事情，只要看它的好坏，不要问它的源头”。上帝的旨意就是“行善”，所以“行善的人，不管他有没有基督教的信仰，都是上帝所悦纳的”。吴进而指出，“一切的善都是从上帝而来”，所以他反问：“那些自以为是不信上帝，而实际上是遵行了上帝旨意的人，他们是不是也可以体认上帝的真理，是不是也可以表彰上帝的美善呢？”

为何1953年底三自组织会对《圣洁没有瑕疵》作出批判？相信是与当时基督徒聚会处以“信仰”为理由反对三自运动有关。该书原是倪柝声在1939年在沪主讲的信息，一直没有整理出版。直至1953年才在李渊如及唐守临主持下，把尘封14年的稿件抄清整理出版。虽然书中内容并非针对新中国及三自革新，但全国三自却认定其神学观点已成为反三自的依据（教会是圣洁的，但魔鬼却以教会中的罪恶来控告教会，世界是由魔鬼掌权，充满罪恶，信徒要远离世界），让信徒在不自觉中敌视新中国及三自革新。倪柝声已于1952年4月被捕（消息未公开），他被捕前曾反对三自革新干涉属灵派信仰，后来聚会处进一步落实。因此，全国三自对倪这部著作的批判，正是要反驳基督徒聚会处反三自的神学论述。

针对另一个反三自的重要人物王明道，由于他以“信与不信，不能同负一轭”作为拒绝参加三自的理由，并在属灵派中产生重要影响力，全国三自决定作出反驳。1955年2月，《天风》发表署名“秦牧”（青年会全国协会校会组干事黄培永）的〈“你们和不信的原不相配，不要同负一轭”的正意与曲解〉，从释经角度反驳王明道“曲解”经文，指“拒绝参加反帝爱国运动，并没有《圣经》的根基，而是出于帝国主义的思想毒素”。未几，广学会更出版单行本，取名《信的见证》。文章引用了13段经文，对“轭”的意思作出考证，指保罗的原意是基督徒不要与任何人同负“罪恶的轭”，也不要与其他宗教的信仰者或没有宗教信仰的人同负“信仰的轭”。但如果将之解释为基督徒与不信主的人不应有任何交往，甚至形成信与不信的对立，便是“曲解”经文的“毒素”。

1956年《天风》组织了两次大规模讨论，主题分别是“基督徒怎样看世界”（2月至5月），“基督徒和世人的关系”（7月至11月），刊登了神学院老师、牧者及信徒对上述问题的看法。据统计，《天风》共发表32篇，就基督徒如何看世界（属魔鬼？属上帝？），人是否完全败坏或仍有上帝形象，信与不信的关系是否对立，以及信仰与道德行为的关系，作出讨论。上述讨论，并不纯粹是神学观点的不同，更重要是应用在基督徒如何看待新中国，以及在中国社会与非信徒的关系。此外，广学会出版部主任张伯怀又出版《“不要爱世界”是什么意思？》，以及《讲章集之一》（1954）、《讲章集之二》（1955）、《从天上来的异象》（韩彼得，1955）等著作，也涉及有关课题。

1955年5月底，王明道再阅读吴耀宗的《黑暗与光明》后，得到“新引导”，决定“从信仰不同一点着手”撰文反击。该篇文章从6月3日开始撰写，至6月9日完稿，全文长2万多字，题为〈我们是为了信仰〉，刊于6月号的《灵食季刊》。王十分重视此文，先后数次在讲习班讲解，并且出版独立的单行本，印数达5,000册。

在文中，他首先阐释基要主义与现代主义神学的矛盾，然后再以吴耀宗著作为例，说明现代主义对“基要要道”的“否定”（不信）。据此，指出“这就是现代派的信仰！这就是现代派的真相！我称他们为‘不信派’，是不是冤枉他们？是不是对不住他们？”针对“不信派”，王明道进一步指出“彼此尊重”的原则并不适用，因为“他们不信耶稣，他们不是基督徒；但他们伪装基督徒，混在教会里面，讲一些似是而非的虚构的道理，去迷惑信徒，败坏信徒的信心。”这些人是教会中的窃贼，是混入羊群中的披着羊皮的豺狼。为了“使他们不能伤害神的羊群”，必须起来反对他们。“对这些人谈不上尊重，更谈不上团结”。

接着，他又驳斥崔宪详，指两派的分别，绝非“大同小异”。而针对丁光训呼吁“团结”，王的回应是，只应“与真实有信仰、热诚爱主的人合一”，而要“与不信派和恶势力分离”。他明确宣布，“我是‘信仰上有着某种了不得的不同而不能团结’”。不仅如此，他更要“奉我主耶稣基督的名同他们战斗”。他又批评身为属灵派却参加三自的汪维藩，重申现代派与基要派之间绝非“大同小异”。

最后，王明道重申，“不和这般‘不信派’有任何联合，或参加他们的任何组织”，因为这是《圣经》的真理。“我们不惜付任何代价，作任何牺牲，歪曲和诬陷是吓不倒我们的。”

《我们是为了信仰》一文，总结了王明道的立场和心志，后来成为反三自的经典文献。

  1. Zhou Enlai (Xing Fuzeng 2023: 106, 112; 2012: 24, 29-34, 39-40; MacInnis 1972: 24)

Zhou Enlai (1883-1976), originally from Shaoxing, Zhejiang, was born in Huai’an, Jiangsu. He studied at Nankai Middle School in Tianjin in 1913 and later attended Nankai University. He had short-term studies in Japan, France, and England, and joined a communist group during his time in France. During the first cooperation between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, he served as the secretary of the Central Bureau of the Communist Party’s Soviet Area. During the War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression, he was the director of the Eighth Route Army’s office in Chongqing. After the establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, he served as the Premier of the State Council (also Secretary of the Party Group), Chairman of the National Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference, and Chairman of the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party. He passed away in January 1976.

Zhou Enlai was a key pioneer in religious work in the early years of the Communist Party’s founding. In June 1950, at the second Party Group meeting of the National Committee, he categorized religions in China into “ethnic religions” and “politically connected” religions, with the former being Islam and Lamaism, and the latter referring to Christianity and Catholicism, which he stated were “connected to imperialism.” He emphasized that one must not underestimate the use of Catholicism and Christianity as tools of aggression by imperialists. Chinese religious practitioners and activities must “sever ties with imperialism to block the plots of imperialists.” However, this struggle was primarily aimed at activities involving reactionary and counter-revolutionary elements, while the vast majority of believers remained a target for unity. When dealing with reactionary elements in the religious community, it was necessary to not “involve general religious issues,” and he reiterated the importance of “respecting everyone’s freedom of belief,” stating that the government should protect churches and temples from occupation and destruction.

In March 1950, the First National United Front Work Conference was held in Beijing, establishing the basic principles for work on Catholicism and Christianity after the founding of the nation. However, the government did not require missionaries currently in China to leave immediately. On the issue of overseas donations, he stated that in principle, “fundraising from abroad should not be continued,” but also advised not to “blindly exclude” donations as long as there were no strings attached. He simultaneously pointed out that the main obstacle faced by Christianity in China was its ties to imperialism. He made it clear that the reason for Chinese people’s opposition to Christianity is inseparable from Christianity’s ties to imperialist aggression over the past hundred years.   1. Zhang Zhiqi (Shanghai Local Chronicles; Zhang Zhiqi 1957: 52-77; 1958a; 1958b; 1958c: 2102-2103; MacInnis 1972: 23)

Zhang Zhiqi (1911-1983) was from Hanyang, Hubei. He joined the Communist Party Youth League in 1927 and became a member of the Communist Party in 1929. After 1935, he worked in Shanghai and participated in various patriotic organizations. In 1946, he organized and led crowds in Shanghai for an anti-civil war peace march. After the founding of the People’s Republic of China, he held various positions including member of the Standing Committee of the Central South Bureau of the Central Committee, Secretary-General of the Central South Military and Political Committee, and Deputy Director of the Central National Affairs Commission.

From August 1954 to January 1965, Zhang Zhiqi served as the Deputy Minister of the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee. After the anti-rightist campaign, Zhang aligned his understanding of united front work with the Party’s line. In his 1957 article “Class Contradictions and Internal Contradictions Among the People,” he stated that while China was mainly still in a situation of “internal contradictions among the people,” “class contradictions had not yet been completely resolved, and class struggle had not ended.” There remained “a considerable number of people” opposing the Communist Party of China and even attacking it. In the ideological realm, the “contradiction between proletariat and non-proletariat thoughts” was still “a relatively prominent contradiction.” If not handled properly, it could further escalate into confrontational struggles. Therefore, it was necessary to encourage moderates to “lean left” or even “make a remarkable leftward shift.”

Shortly thereafter, in 1958, he attempted to lead the united front with class struggle in his essay “The People’s Democratic United Front is a Special Form of Class Struggle,” stating that “socialist revolution must use struggle to eliminate the bourgeoisie,” and that the “People’s Democratic United Front” was “a special method for engaging in class struggle” and “a special weapon for advancing the socialist revolution and consolidating the dictatorship of the proletariat.” The united front was a combination of unity and struggle, aimed at “constantly achieving transformation.” Essentially, it emphasized “peaceful transformation,” specifically through a “redemption policy” (that is, allocating benefits economically and politically) and educational methods to encourage self-transformation, characterized by “both tension and relaxation,” which required “a long-term struggle process.” He was confident that a “new favorable situation” had been created, “accelerating the pace of socialist transformation” to achieve “a socialist society without class exploitation.”

If class struggle had not yet concluded, the united front became a “special form” of class struggle. How would the Communist Party deal with religious issues? In 1958, he published an article titled “Atheists and Theists Can Cooperate Politically and Walk the Socialist Path” in “Philosophical Research.” On the surface, it seemed that atheists could collaborate politically with theists, but in reality, the article expressed how he understood the relationship between religion and “class contradictions.” It is evident that after the anti-rightist struggle, the Communist Party was grappling with the complexity of religious issues in the context of the struggle between “two roads”: socialism and capitalism. (1) Under the internal contradictions among the people, it did not mean that “the religious issue had no class contradictions among the people currently; or that antagonistic contradictions no longer existed.” In navigating the “socialist revolution,” specifically during the struggle between the two roads, the situation of the religious community remained very complex; (2) this was reflected in believers’ insufficient political awareness of socialism. Meanwhile, the upper-level figures “who genuinely uphold anti-imperialism, love for the country, support for the socialist path, and the leadership of the Communist Party were still a minority.” There were many in the religious community who, although they “might accept the leadership of the Communist Party and walk the socialist path,” were still in a neutral political position, “harboring varying degrees of skepticism toward the Communist Party and socialism or having conflicting sentiments.”However, the serious issue is that within the religious community, there are still individuals who not only “have not clearly drawn the line between themselves and imperialism and counter-revolutionaries, but some have even openly expressed a firm unwillingness to sever ties with imperialism (especially the Vatican), opposing the socialist new China and the leadership of the Communist Party.” In this regard, the contradictions manifested are “antagonistic contradictions, that is, enemy-versus-us contradictions.” These “bourgeois reactionary rightists in the religious community” often disguise themselves in religious garb and are utilized by “imperialists, remnant counter-revolutionaries, landlord class members, and rich peasant elements” to struggle against the Chinese people through the issue of religion. Furthermore, Catholicism and Christianity have originally been tools of imperialist invasion, and imperialism is still unwilling to accept its defeat in China. The threat of imperialism to China still exists, so their struggle against the Chinese people is long-term. “We must continue to work hard to completely rid religion of the influence and control of imperialist forces and hostile classes, transforming the religious issue entirely into internal contradictions among the people.”

At that time, the socialist education movement in the religious community (i.e., the anti-rightist struggle) was in full swing. Zhang Zhiqi raised the issue of “breaking” and “establishing,” indicating that this movement, while “aimed at breaking the political stance of capitalism among religious individuals and establishing a political stance of socialism,” did not “require religious believers to abandon their faith.” He acknowledged that Marxist-Leninists could “firmly stand on a socialist political stance” without needing “every supporter of the socialist political system to be a Marxist-Leninist,” and that they could be allowed to retain different worldviews in terms of ideology and to keep their respective religious beliefs. In this publicly published article, he addressed criticisms that the Communist Party’s “religious policy is a deception” and that “Communists want to eradicate religion.” Thus, despite the strong flavor of “class struggle” expressed in the text, he still affirmed the importance of the policy of religious freedom.

However, Zhang’s remarks at internal meetings conveyed a different message. In May 1958, the Communist Party held a professional meeting on religious work, where Zhang Zhiqi delivered a speech that communicated the central government’s basic stance on religious work to the religious cadres. He pointed out that religion, as “a superstructure leftover from the old society,” although it had lost its foundation after the elimination of the exploiting classes, would not be “immediately eliminated” and could still “prolong its ideology for a considerable period.” However, “generally speaking, religion is unlikely to develop and will gradually weaken, and we should consciously facilitate its gradual weakening.” The so-called “consciously facilitate” means to implement reforms of the religious system.

Then, would it contradict the policy of religious freedom for the government to lead the reform of religion? In the current political situation, how should the policy of religious freedom be understood? Zhang Zhiqi emphasized that religious freedom, in addition to promoting freedom of religion, also includes “the freedom to promote non-belief” and “the freedom to promote anti-religion.” His understanding of “protecting religious freedom” is that it does “not help religion develop, but is aimed at politically uniting the masses of believers to work with the working people to develop productivity and advance politically.” Zhang candidly pointed out that to achieve the “elimination” of religion, one must first promote its “weakening.” “Today’s protection is for tomorrow’s elimination. Protecting religious freedom is to create conditions that prompt differentiation within the religious community, allowing them to use their own hands to transform themselves in a manner of self-reformation, ultimately achieving the elimination of religion.”

  1. Chinese Communist Party (Ying 2014: 884-901; MacInnis 1972; Yang 2012: 65-72)

The Chinese Communist Party is a revolutionary party with a strong ideological orientation. Both before and after the establishment of the nation, it insisted on transforming Chinese society according to revolutionary ideals and goals (communism), while also making adjustments to meet the needs of realpolitik. As a party that believes in materialism and atheism, how to handle religious issues has been an unavoidable topic in its revolutionary nation-building process. From a worldview perspective, the CCP adheres to the opposition between materialism and idealism, viewing religion as the “opiate of the people” and its ultimate demise, but it also establishes unity and struggle against religion based on real political needs, as well as the means to achieve the end of religion. Thus, religious work has always been an important aspect of the CCP’s united front work, with judgments regarding unity and struggle made according to the political line and needs of different eras.

Reviewing the history of the CCP since its founding in 1921, religious struggle has become one of the fronts of the youth movement, student movement, and mass movement. The newly formed Chinese Communist Party actively intervened in the non-Christian movement in 1922 and infiltrated the Young Men’s Christian Association. It can be said that Christianity was the first religion the CCP made arrangements for and engaged with, using “cultural invasion” as an important narrative and weapon in the anti-imperialist struggle. From the 1930s to the 40s, during the anti-Japanese resistance to the Nationalist-Communist civil war, the CCP emphasized uniting patriotic religious figures while fighting, expanding the united front according to the needs of realpolitik.

After the founding of New China, with the complex development of both internal and external situations, the CCP’s judgments on the situation of social revolution, the balance of power between friend and foe, the internal contradictions of the people, and changes in internal power struggles all influenced its religious policies. From the “New People’s Democracy” that was emphasized in the early years to the comprehensive launch of the anti-American and aid Korea movement after intervening in the Korean War, the party engaged in a fierce struggle against imperialism (especially American imperialism) and various mass political movements closely related to it (land reform, ideological transformation, Three Anti and Five Anti campaigns). It can be said that between 1949 and 1953, although Christianity became one of the five recognized religions, it still needed to undergo innovation in the anti-imperialist struggle. Although the CCP proposed a distinction between religion and politics (imperialism), how to define the “running dogs” and “toxins” of “imperialism” during the process of “politicization” and radicalization of mass movements easily led to the problem of excessive targeting. However, in 1953, the CCP also began to realize the issues at hand and thus reaffirmed the goal of “unity.”

In 1953, the CCP announced the end of the new democratic phase and gradually transitioned towards constructing a socialist society. However, concerning the question of how to eliminate the exploitative classes while walking the socialist path, it opposed using “direct elimination” methods, advocating instead for a “gradual transformation” approach. Minister Li Weihan of the United Front Work Department emphasized that one should not attempt to overturn religion through atheistic propaganda, asserting that “the elimination of religion can only be possible when humanity has eliminated classes and greatly enhanced its ability to control nature.” In this context, the CCP understood “eliminating religion” as a natural outcome of achieving a socialist society (i.e., natural demise). During the transitional period, religious work still focused on unity and transformation, with struggle as a supplementary aspect. The national Christian conference in 1954 proposed “expanding unity,” which can be seen as a concrete expression of this direction.

However, when “expanding unity” faced resistance from the spiritual faction, the CCP viewed it as an object of struggle and, through the anti-revolutionary campaign of 1955, regarded the anti-Three Self religious issue as a counter-revolutionary political issue. As a result, in 1955 and 1956, it successively dismantled the counter-revolutionary groups of Wang Mingdao and Ni Tuosheng in Christianity, and the counter-revolutionary group of Gong Pinmei in Catholicism. After the anti-revolution struggles, it successfully forced the spiritual faction opposing the Three Self Movement to join the patriotic camp.

In 1956, the political line of the CCP experienced a more relaxed adjustment, and Mao Zedong declared that “large-scale, storm-like mass class struggles were basically over,” yet “class struggle had not completely ended,” thus proposing the direction of “correctly handling contradictions among the people.” Against this backdrop, religion was seen as a contradiction among the people rather than that of hostile classes. At the same time, the CCP put forward the slogan “Let a hundred flowers blossom, let a hundred schools of thought contend,” leading to a relatively open environment for Chinese Christianity between 1956 and 1957.However, following May and June of 1957, the CCP’s judgment of the situation reversed to the anti-Rightist struggle, and the line of class struggle again dominated the CCP’s religious policy. The expansion of the anti-Rightist campaign became a large-scale political liquidation of Chinese Christianity, with most of the originally united subjects classified as “Rightists.”

In China in 1958, the contradictions of friend and foe from the anti-Rightist struggle continued, while the atmosphere of “leap forward” towards a communist society further radicalized religious work, leading to a “promoting weakening” approach aimed at the “disappearance of religion.” Under the extremely “left” line, the Christian system received unprecedented shocks, with many church venues closed under the pretext of “joint worship,” and religious clergy were labeled as the “exploiting class.” The “no-religion zone” experiment indicated that religion had become the final target the CCP intended to “eliminate.”

In March 1952, the chairman of the Family Management Committee wrote a letter to 吳耀宗, the chairman of the Preparatory Committee for the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, reporting the internal problems of the Jesus Family and requesting that someone be sent to lead the reforms. In April, the national Three-Self organizations sent a working group led by 沈德溶, along with 朱大衛 and 金啟昌, to Tai’an, establishing their presence at 馬莊 with the assistance of Christian and government departments in Shandong Province. After the work team settled in, they held seminars and individual discussions, established a Learning Committee, and organized believers for study. 沈德溶 and officials from the Tai’an Municipal Committee’s United Front Work Department gave reports on topics such as “How American Imperialism Utilizes Christianity to Invade China,” “The Direction of the Three-Self Patriotic Movement,” “The Religious Policy of the People’s Government,” and “New Democratic Society and its Future.” In May, an exhibition was held to expose the issues of leaders like 敬奠瀛 and 左順真. From May 27 to 29, a three-day accusation conference was held, during which 敬 returned to 馬莊 with 崔憲祥 from the Preparatory Committee to accept criticism from the public. More than 120 people signed up to make accusations, and finally, 60 took the stage to accuse.

After the accusation conference, it was decided to “split the family” of the Jesus Family, restoring 138 households based on blood relations, which were categorized into agricultural, industrial, medical, educational, and production households, among others, and redistribute properties. By October 1952, the “family splitting” was completed, leading to the declaration of the Jesus Family’s “disintegration.” The “馬莊耶穌家庭” was also renamed “北新莊基督教堂.” The working group announced the “end of imperialism and reactionary feudal forces’ rule and exploitation of the 馬莊耶穌家庭.” Ultimately, 敬奠瀛 was arrested by the government and imprisoned in Tai’an Prison.

With the disintegration of the “old family,” various “small families” also began their Three-Self Patriotic Movement studies, splitting their families and dissolving existing organizations. The Jesus Family became the first Christian sect to be comprehensively “banned” after the establishment of the People’s Republic of China.

  1. Christian Counter-Revolutionary Group Case 1955-1956 (邢福增 2010: 97-147; 2005: 121-140; Wickeri 1990: 162-170)

In 1955, leftist literary theorist 胡風 faced criticism for his independent views and was soon deemed by the Central Committee as part of a “struggle against the 胡風 counter-revolutionary group.” This nationwide anti-胡風 movement further evolved into a nationwide campaign to purge counter-revolutionaries. On July 1 and August 25, the Central Committee issued directives regarding the initiation of struggles to eradicate hidden counter-revolutionaries.

In light of the anti-counter-revolutionary situation, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Office convened over 500 Christian representatives on June 14 for a meeting to expose the “胡風 counter-revolutionary group,” demanding that the religious community disclose counter-revolutionaries. Subsequently, several Christian figures spoke, indicating the need to remain vigilant against hidden counter-revolutionaries within Christianity. On June 20, the national and Shanghai Three-Self Movement committees held a seminar to condemn the 胡風 counter-revolutionary group.

On July 11, the publication 《天風》 issued an editorial entitled “Strengthen Unity, Distinguish Right from Wrong,” which for the first time named and criticized 王明道, a Beijing Christian Church leader who publicly opposed the Three-Self Movement. His errors included: (1) fundamentally denying that Chinese Christianity was exploited by imperialism, instead claiming the toxins of imperialistic ideology were “truths” of the Bible; (2) attempting to incite conflict between “fundamentalists” and “modernists” within Christianity over beliefs, thereby undermining and eliminating the foundation of the Three-Self patriotic movement; (3) self-aggrandizing as a servant of God, judging participating Christians in the Three-Self Movement as “spiritually degenerate” and “non-believers.” Therefore, in response to 王明道’s “disruption of anti-imperialism and patriotism under the guise of faith,” the editorial called on patriotic Christians to “clear the boundaries of right and wrong with Mr. Wang Mingdao from the perspective of anti-imperialism and patriotism” and actively engage in the struggle.

On July 4, 1955, the anti-counter-revolutionary struggle movement began in Beijing, requiring all students attending the Christian church to report and denounce issues related to Wang’s activities.On August 5, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Office convened religious leaders to study materials related to the “胡風 counter-revolutionary group.” Director 羅竹風 reiterated that religious individuals must “eliminate counter-revolutionaries from within the church.” He described the counter-revolutionaries in the religious community as primarily “appearing with pious and enthusiastic facades,” while in reality, they were merely “disguised in religious garb” to deceive the faithful. 罗’s report marked the commencement of the counter-revolutionary struggle in the religious community.

In the early morning of August 8, 王明道 and his wife were arrested at home, along with approximately 20 others in Beijing, 香山, 長春, and 廣州. On August 15, 《天風》 published a short commentary titled “Exposing the Reactionary Words and Actions of Wang Mingdao,” calling for the “decisive exposure of Wang Mingdao’s reactionary actions and an immediate distancing from him.” After Wang’s arrest, from August to November, Three-Self organizations and churches across the country launched a campaign against the “Wang Mingdao counter-revolutionary group,” with 《天風》 printing numerous articles critiquing Wang. Shortly thereafter, 林獻羔, a “Wang Mingdao element” in Guangzhou, was arrested, and a campaign against the “Shanghai version of Wang Mingdao’s reactionary rhetoric” —《聖膏》— was initiated in Shanghai. In October and November, the资料室 of 《天風》 compiled collections titled “Exposing the Reactionary Words of Counter-Revolutionary Wang Mingdao” and “Accusations Against the Wang Mingdao Counter-Revolutionary Group,” thoroughly critiquing Wang and his associates.

After his arrest, Wang succumbed to threats and pressure, confessing to the government. On September 29, 1956, he was granted “educational release” for his willingness to repent and agree to participate in the Three-Self Movement. However, having not fulfilled his commitment, both Wang and his wife were arrested again on April 29, 1958. In April 1961, the Beijing People’s Procuratorate formally charged them under the “Counter-Revolutionary Punishment Regulations.” In July 1963, the Beijing Intermediate People’s Court sentenced Wang Mingdao and 劉景文 to life imprisonment and a 15-year fixed-term sentence for counter-revolutionary crimes. Wang Mingdao’s appeal was rejected in September by the Beijing Higher People’s Court Criminal Appeals Division, which upheld the original verdict.

The case of the “Wang Mingdao counter-revolutionary group” became a focal point of the counter-revolutionary struggle within the Christian community, marking the government’s shift in focus toward those church hardliners who refused to participate in the Three-Self Movement based on their differing faith. At that time, apart from Wang Mingdao, the Christian Assembly was also one of the most organized and populous elements of the anti-Three-Self camp, undoubtedly becoming the next target of the government.

Starting in mid-January 1956, leaders of Christian assemblies in places such as Hunan (长沙, 衡阳, 株洲, 常德, 慈利, 湘潭, etc.), Guangdong (广州), Zhejiang (杭州, 宁波, 温州, 平阳, 萧山, 宁海, 瑞安, 黄岩, 金华, 磐安, 绍兴, 嘉兴, 嘉善, etc.), and Fujian (福州, 厦门, 泉州, 晋江, 莆田, 福清, 闽侯, 福鼎, 古田, 连城, 漳浦, etc.) were successively arrested by public security authorities on counter-revolutionary charges. This was followed by a comprehensive accusation meeting calling on the Christian community to denounce the counter-revolutionaries hidden within the assemblies, with participants from local assemblies coming forward to join the accusations. A nationwide campaign to rectify the assemblies thus began.

As the center of the national assembly, the Shanghai Assembly also became a target for rectification on January 29, 1956. That evening, the Shanghai Public Security Bureau arrested 李淵如, 汪佩真, 張愚之, 藍志一, among others, officially announcing the dismantling of the “倪柝聲 counter-revolutionary group” within the Shanghai Assembly. On January 30, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Office held a meeting to declare the crimes of the 倪柝聲 counter-revolutionary group, attended by over 2,500 Christians. From February 8 to 19, a crime exhibit was held concerning the 倪柝聲 counter-revolutionary group, with nearly 10,000 Shanghai believers visiting. From February 21 to 24, the exhibition was extended to include believers from other local assemblies. In February 1956, multiple editions of《天風》 featured detailed reports on this topic and reprinted several accusation documents. On February 22, a grand meeting of all assembly believers took place at the 南陽路 assembly to accuse the 倪柝聲 counter-revolutionary group’s crimes, attended by over 3,000 participants, including representatives from Nanjing, Changsha, and Xuzhou.

On June 21, 1956, the Beijing Higher People’s Court tried the 倪柝聲 counter-revolutionary case. 倪柝聲 admitted to the charges in court. Ultimately, he was sentenced to 15 years in prison and deprived of political rights for five years. In October 1958, the Beijing Higher People’s Court sentenced 李淵如 and 汪佩真 to 15 years and 張愚之 to 12 years, respectively. One of their charges included conducting “counter-revolutionary activities intensified following 倪’s arrest.” Other assembly members arrested across the country also faced sentencing in 1958.

It is noteworthy that 倪柝聲 had already been arrested by the government in April 1952.From 1953 to 1955, various assemblies across the country engaged in anti-Three-Self struggles under the pretext of faith, which, in fact, had no substantial relationship with 倪柝聲, and he could not assume a leadership role in these anti-struggles. The counter-revolutionary group case related to the Christian Assemblies in 1956 still bore the name “倪柝聲,” partly because of his significant position within the assemblies, leading the government to view him as the head of the counter-revolutionary group; on the other hand, the Chinese Communist Party sought to utilize the various accusations against 倪, especially concerning his moral issues, as a critical weapon to undermine the assemblies. The high-profile trial of 倪柝聲 during the anti-counter-revolutionary campaign (in contrast to 王明道) and the public disclosure of his crimes indeed inflicted tremendous, even fatal, damage to the assemblies.

  1. Wenzhou “No Religion Experimental Zone” 1958 (支華欣 2000: 41-42; 朱宇晶 2011: 95-96; 舍禾 2015: 483-495)

During the Great Leap Forward, the national Three-Self organizations proposed to thoroughly change the semi-colonial appearance of Christianity by implementing “united worship” in August 1958. Prior to this, Wenzhou in Zhejiang Province had already decided to merge in a Three-Self expanded meeting held in May, planning to implement the merger from July. According to the regulations, all denominational names were to be canceled, personnel unified, with no distinction of sects, and property and finances centralized. Initially, among the 17 churches in the urban area, 6 were allowed to remain open, corresponding to the 6 original denominations: 城西堂 (originally Methodist), 滄河巷堂 (originally Self-Reliance Association), 花園巷堂 (originally China Inland Mission), 永光堂 (originally Jesus Self-Reliance Association), 頭梳腦堂 (originally Seventh-day Adventist), 西城下堂 (originally Assembly).

The model of united worship quickly spread from Wenzhou to various parts of the country. Grassroots congregations from different denominations adopted a method of uniting with other congregations in the same region, significantly reducing the number of grassroots congregations.

According to 沈德溶, a member of the national Three-Self Standing Committee, in the summer of 1958, the head of the Religious Affairs Bureau, 何成湘, mentioned to him in Shanghai the concept of “fly-free cities” and “fly-free countries” proposed in various places in Beijing during the Great Leap Forward. 何 believed that “in terms of religion, could we create a ‘nation free of religious activities’ throughout China?” Following this, 何 visited Wenzhou for inspection, and 沈 believed that the later “leftist errors” regarding religion in Wenzhou were closely related to this visit. 沈所指的，正是 Wenzhou’s early implementation of united worship, and the subsequent establishment of the “No Religion Experimental Zone.”

The Wenzhou “No Religion Experimental Zone” materialized in Wenzhou’s subordinate counties: 平陽, 永嘉, 洞頭, 瑞安, and 蒼南, where all religious activities were essentially halted during the Great Leap Forward. All churches were closed, rather than merged. In the urban area of Wenzhou, the initial 6 opened churches for united worship were further merged down to only 城西堂.

The summary report on religious work from Wenzhou in 1959 stated: “In the city, one Catholic church, one Christian church, and one Buddhist temple will continue to be retained for proper religious activities, while all suburban churches have halted services indefinitely. No public interference will be conducted regarding household gatherings, nor will they be officially recognized; efforts will be made to monitor the situation, actively promote education and persuasion, and remain vigilant against disruptive elements spreading rumors…”

The Wenzhou “No Religion Experimental Zone” reflects the beginning of the CCP’s policy to “eliminate” religion during the Great Leap Forward. This policy would later be implemented nationwide during the Cultural Revolution.

  1. Suppression of the True Jesus Church 1958-59 (Inouye 2018: 208-210; 鄧肇明 1993: 2-19)

During the Great Leap Forward, the True Jesus Church became one of the sects heavily targeted and impacted. The True Jesus Church is an indigenous Christian sect in China, founded in 1917. In March 1951, the True Jesus Church held its 12th General Assembly, where it formulized a reform program expressing a commitment to enhance political education and pursue aid for Korea. In April, Wei Yisai, as president, attended the “Meeting on Handling Christian Organizations Receiving American Subsidies” convened by the State Council and co-signed a joint declaration. In early 1952, Wei Yisai conducted a self-critique expressing his intent to participate in the Three-Self Movement. In March 1953, the General Assembly issued notices to various regions to study the Three-Self reform declaration, resulting in over 13,000 believers signing by September. In April, the True Jesus Church General Assembly Committee for the Three-Self Reform Movement Preparatory Committee was established, and the General Assembly moved its headquarters from Beijing to Wuhan, with the national president and vice-president (蔣約翰 and 劉均安) elected as president and vice-president. During this period, Wei Yisai faced criticism.

In May 1958, Zhang Zhiyi, Deputy Minister of the United Front Work Department of the Central Committee, mentioned the need to eliminate the True Jesus Church during a specialized religious work meeting. This suggestion was quickly relayed to various provinces.On June 5, 1958, a specialized religious work conference in Sichuan Province addressed related issues and required local authorities to “assess the situation.” In January 1959, the province decided to start in Chongqing, where the congregation was concentrated, operating under unified leadership from the party committee. A joint working group composed of the United Front, public security, and religious affairs departments was formed, followed by the establishment of cooperative groups at the county level for unified actions against the True Jesus Church. By the end of May, the campaign had essentially “defeated and discredited the True Jesus Church, achieving the expected objectives,” with the “struggle reaching a conclusion.” For example, in Chongqing, among 135 key members of the district assembly, a total of 82 were targeted (16 arrested, 34 under control, 2 sent for labor re-education, 10 struggled against, and 20 faced public review and debate criticism).

In May 1958, the national Three-Self Movement began simultaneous criticisms of the True Jesus Church. After 《天風》 published reports on the “illegal activities” of the True Jesus Church in Hubei, it emphasized, particularly in an “editorial,” that such “criminal activities against the Party, socialism, and the people” must not be tolerated. In June, following reports on the illegal activities of the True Jesus Church in Hanyang, Hubei, 《天風》 included a “short commentary” and reprinted an article from the Hubei Christian Socialist Education Study Committee denouncing the True Jesus Church. In July, 《天風》 published letters from readers at the True Jesus Church in Beijing, calling for a denunciation of the reactionary group led by 蔣約翰 at the General Assembly of the True Jesus Church.

During the Great Leap Forward, the True Jesus Church was the only sect specifically named and subjected to unprecedented political purging. This may be attributed to its tightly organized national structure and strong “charismatic” tradition. Additionally, the True Jesus Church’s “exclusive” view of the church, asserting itself as the only “true” church, led to its classification as a detrimental element to unity, even though it participated in the Three-Self Movement, resulting in a comprehensive crackdown. After the implementation of united worship, all denominational organizations ceased operations, and congregations were required to merge with other sects. However, some scholars suggest that the True Jesus Church was still able to develop in an underground state.
