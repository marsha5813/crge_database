China: Agency (1950-1960)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

(1) Wu Yaozong (Ying Fuzeng 2012a: xii-lix; Gao 1996: 338-352)

Wu Yaozong (Y. T. Wu, 1893-1979), from Shunde, Guangdong. He graduated from Peking Taxation School in 1913 and then worked at Customs. In 1918, he was baptized and joined the Beijing Congregational Church. In 1920, he resolutely left his job and became a staff member of the Beijing YMCA School Department. After returning from further study in the United States in 1927, he was transferred to the National Council of YMCA’s School Committee, where he engaged in student work and actively participated in promoting the Student Christian Movement in China, until he became the director of the association's publishing house in 1933. In 1938, after returning from further studies in the US for the second time, he served as the director of the YMCA Publishing Department.

Wu's theological beliefs were heavily concerned with social issues, and he actively participated in the Society of Truth and the Society of Life, writing articles for "The Truth Weekly" and "Life" (later merged into "Truth and Life"). He studied twice in the US, both at Union Theological Seminary in New York, where he was deeply influenced by liberal theology. He explored how Christianity faced the challenges of modern science and new trends, reflecting on how Christianity could participate in the transformation of Chinese society. Due to the strong "liberal" and "modernist" colors in his theological thought, the "spiritual faction" has long regarded him as a heretic of Christian tradition and even labeled him as a representative of the “unbelieving faction.” Meanwhile, he was also a major proponent of Pacifism, serving as the president of the Pacifism Society and editing the magazine "Love Only." After the September 18 Incident in 1931, he became involved in the Anti-Japanese Salvation Movement, joining the National Salvation Society, and gradually deviated from pacifism in his thoughts. In response to the developments of the times, he expressed deep hatred towards the evils brought by capitalism, further aligning himself with socialism and exploring how to reconcile Christianity with materialism. During the War of Resistance, Wu established the Christian United Publishing House in Chengdu, engaging in literary work. After the war, as the Chinese Civil War was about to break out, Wu expressed further dissatisfaction with the Kuomintang, becoming an ally of the Communist Party. His publication "Tianfeng Weekly" became a platform for Christians who were concerned about the political situation.

Around the establishment of the new China in 1949, he advocated that Christianity must undergo a thorough self-transformation to adapt to the new era. With the support of the Chinese Communist Party, he initiated the Three-Self Reform Movement (renamed the Three-Self Patriotic Movement in 1954), becoming one of the main leaders of the movement, while also representing the Christian community as a permanent committee member of the National People's Political Consultative Conference (1st to 4th sessions) and a permanent committee member of the National People's Congress (1st to 5th sessions). As the leader of the Three-Self Movement, the issues arising during the promotion of the movement and its harm to the Chinese church made it difficult for him to escape responsibility, leading to him being seen as a "political opportunist" and "apostate." Wu Yaozong passed away in 1979 in Shanghai.

Wu Yaozong left behind a large number of works in both Chinese and English, including books such as: "Social Gospel" (1934), "Pacifism and Social Reconstruction" (1934), "Youth in Times of Change" (1934), "The Problem of the Way Out for Chinese Youth" (1935), "Religious Faith in the Great Era" (1938), "God’s View in the Great Era" (1940), "No One Has Seen God" (first and second editions in 1943, fourth edition in 1946, fifth edition in 1948), "Darkness and Light" (1949), "Christian Speech" (1950). He also edited works including "The Jesus I Know" (1929), "Impressions of the Madras Conference" (1939), "Christianity and the New China" (1940), "Dialectical Materialism Study Handbook" (1950), and "Christian Reform Movement Study Handbook" (1952).

(2) Liu Liangmo (He Hanting 1998: 16-22; Shen Derong 2010: 440-442; Liu Xiaopeng 2017: 140)

Liu Liangmo (Liu Liang-Mo, 1909-1988), from Zhenhai, Zhejiang, was born into a non-Christian family but attended church schools from a young age. While studying at Mingqiang Middle School, run by the Southern Baptist Convention, he began participating in school fellowship activities. He later attended the affiliated high school of Hujiang University, serving as the secretary of the choir and the president of the Hujiang University Baptist Student Fellowship. After being baptized in his first year at Hujiang University’s social science program, he participated in campus YMCA activities. During the January 28 Incident in 1932, Liu joined his classmates in the national salvation movement, supporting the 19th Route Army in resisting Japan. After graduating from university in 1932, he worked as a staff member in the student department of the national YMCA, actively participating in the anti-Japanese national salvation movement.

In the winter of 1934, Liu Liangmo organized a public singing event at the Shanghai YMCA, launching the Salvation Song Movement, and he composed a song titled "Save China." In 1935, he collaborated with Lu Gancheng, the general secretary of the Shanghai YMCA, to organize a choir. In May, he recorded "March of the Volunteers" on a vinyl record, which became an important song for the singing group in Shanghai. When Shen Junru and other members of the "Seven Gentleman" were arrested by the government, Liu also participated in the rescue efforts. In January 1936, the "Shanghai All-Community National Salvation Federation" was established, and Christian representatives in Shanghai issued a declaration opposing appeasement diplomacy, demanding to use force to safeguard national territory and dignity, with Liu as one of the 28 founders. In May, the "National All-Community National Salvation Federation" was founded in Shanghai, where Liu served as a director. Throughout various activities organized by the National Salvation Society, Liu Liangmo led the singing of "March of the Volunteers," earning him the title of "Cheerleader of the National Salvation Society" from Shen Junru and others. In June 1936, during the first anniversary of the founding of the National Salvation Federation, a mass singing event was held at the Shanghai Public Sports Hall, where Liu conducted on stage.

In the summer of 1936, Liu attended the "World Christian Student Alliance" conference held in San Francisco. Upon returning to China, he participated in the YMCA’s "Military Service Department," promoting services for soldiers in locations like Suiyuan, Suzhou, Zhengzhou, and Changsha. During this period, he wrote "ABC of Public Singing," explaining how to teach the public to sing and organize public singing groups.

In 1939, Liu met Zhou Enlai in Jinhua, Zhejiang. Later, Liu was detained for two months in Ningbo on suspicion of being a Communist, but after his release, he applied to the YMCA for further study in the United States.

From the summer of 1940 to August 1949, Liu Liangmo was mainly active in the United States, studying at Crozer Theological Seminary and the University of Pennsylvania. During this time, he promoted China's resistance against Japan across various parts of the US and organized singing groups among overseas Chinese. After the victory in the war, he stayed in the US, working to establish a branch of the "China Democratic League" in America. In September 1946, when Feng Yuxiang gave a speech in the US, Liu acted as his translator.

In August 1949, Liu Liangmo received a telegram in New York from Zhou Enlai through Gong Peng, inviting him to Beijing to announce the formation of the new Political Consultative Conference. He then returned to China with his family. Liu, alongside Wu Yaozong, Deng Yuzhi, Zhang Xueyan, and Zhao Zicheng, became a Christian representative in the Political Consultative Conference. Later, Liu yielded his position to an Islamic representative and became an alternate representative. At the conference, Liu and several other committee members jointly proposed "March of the Volunteers" as the national anthem, which was approved.

After returning to China, he served as the director of the national YMCA's ministry group and participated in the North China Christian Visiting Group, where he met Zhou Enlai in May 1950. Within the Communist Party, the Three-Self Reform Declaration initiated by Wu Yaozong was referred to as the "Wu-Liu Movement," indicating his role in it.

Liu Liangmo played a significant role in the Three-Self Reform Movement: he was a initiator of the reform declaration, a member of the presidium of the "Meeting of Christian Organizations Accepting American Subsidies," an executive committee member of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee, and the leader of the propaganda group. As the head of the propaganda group, he was a crucial figure in promoting the Christian accusation movement. In May 1951, Liu Liangmo published an article in the "People's Daily" titled "How to Conduct an Effective Church Accusation Meeting?" urging churches nationwide to understand that the purpose of the accusation was to eliminate the influence of imperialism within Christianity and that the targets of the accusation were "the imperialist elements hidden within the church, their henchmen, and the morally corrupt from other churches." He also provided guidance on how to conduct accusation meetings: first, form an "Accusation Committee" to study whom to accuse and who to invite to make accusations. Then, invite participants to attend an accusation mobilization meeting to establish in their minds the concepts of why and what to accuse. Following this, hold accusation group meetings in each church and organization to identify the most effective individuals for accusations, and invite them to participate in a larger accusation meeting. He emphasized that the guidance from the Religious Affairs Bureau was indispensable during the preparations for the accusations. To complement the accusations, impactful accusatory speeches could be published in various local newspapers, and exhibitions could be organized. Liu Liangmo's guidance indeed became a template for reference in various places.

Moreover, he was an important theorist of the "accusation" concept. At the beginning of the reform movement, he published several articles in "Tianfeng," focusing on the following points: (1) American imperialism uses Christianity to invade China; (2) Anti-imperialism and patriotism are not opposing forces; (3) Criticism of "super politics" and "Pacifism." The YMCA Publishing House planned to publish the "New Era Learning Series" to help fellow Christians adapt to the new era, and out of the 14 volumes, Liu was responsible for editing seven: "Learning Manual for New Democracy," "Learning Manual for the People's Democratic Dictatorship," "Learning Manual for the People's Political Consultative Conference and Three Major Documents," "Learning Manual for Mao Zedong Thought," "What is the United Front," "How America Uses Religion to Invade China," "Christianity and World Peace," and "Learning Manual for Christians on American Views."

In 1954, the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was established, and Liu Liangmo was elected as an executive committee member. In the second national Christian conference in 1961, he was re-elected as a second-term executive member.

From 1959 onwards, he continuously served as a member of the National Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference (starting from 1964 as a standing member). He was a member of the "China Democratic League" and served as an alternate member of the Central Committee since 1953, becoming a full member in 1979. Starting in 1977, as a representative of the "China Democratic League," he served two consecutive terms as vice chairman of the Shanghai Municipal Committee.

After the Cultural Revolution, Liu Liangmo participated in symposiums on amending the Constitution of the People's Republic of China, expressing opinions on the provisions related to religion. He passed away in August 1988. The Mourning Committee was established in the name of "Comrade Liu Liangmo," and the obituary referred to Liu as "Comrade." Liu Liangmo's grandson, Liu Xin, acknowledged that his grandfather was a member of the CCP underground party. He should be regarded as the most senior Communist Party member within the national Three-Self organization.

(3) Deng Yuzhi (Hoing 1996: 243-262; National Assembly of the Chinese Christian Women’s Association 2000: 1-2)

Deng Yuzhi (Cora Deng, Deng Yu-Chih, 1900-1996), known by the courtesy name Yuzhi and pen name Yuzhi, was from Shashi, Hubei. As a child, she moved with her parents to Changsha, Hunan. In 1915, she enrolled in the Union Girls’ High School run by the American Presbyterian Church, where she served as the president of the student council. During her studies, she actively participated in various patriotic activities, including opposing the "Twenty-One Demands" and boycotting Japanese goods. After graduating in 1919, she married Zhang Dabing, but due to Zhang's family going back on their promise and not agreeing to let Deng continue her studies at Jinling Women's University, she resolutely left the Zhang family. In 1920, she entered Jinling Women's University to study sociology and took a leave of absence in 1922 to return to Changsha, working with the Changsha Christian Women's Association and participating in the first national conference of the Christian Women’s Association in 1923. At the conference, they decided to initiate labor services, while various church conferences established sociology courses such as "Labor Issues" and "Labor Welfare."

After graduating in 1926, Deng Yuzhi held various positions including staff member in the Student Department of the National Association of the Chinese Christian Women’s Association, staff member and chief secretary of the Labor Department, and chief secretary of the Department of Civil Education. When she served as a staff member of the Labor Department in 1928, she was responsible for the Pudong People's School, where she initiated education for women and incorporated activities such as current affairs, singing, and dramatics. By 1930, it was officially named the Women's Night School of the Young Women's Association. Over the next few years, the number of women's night schools in Shanghai increased to six. In addition to Shanghai, she also established women's night schools in Yantai, Tianjin, Wuhan, Chongqing, Guangzhou, and Hong Kong.

From 1929 to 1930, she studied at the London School of Economics in the Social Service Department. In 1932, she worked on research protecting women and children at the International Labour Organization in Geneva, Switzerland. In 1935, she initiated the Shanghai Women's National Salvation Association and participated in founding the National Disaster Education Society in 1936. After the outbreak of war, the Young Women's Association carried out wartime service. Deng served as the deputy head of the Comfort Group of the Shanghai Branch of the Chinese Women's Comforting the Self-Defense Against Japan Association, visiting hospitals to comfort the wounded, writing home letters on their behalf, and providing clothing and food. Later, she established the Hankou office of the National Association of the Young Women's Association.

From 1938 to 1941, while serving as an exchange officer at the American Christian Women’s Association and studying sociology at the State University of New York, she traveled extensively to promote the anti-Japanese national salvation movement. She graduated with a master's degree in 1941. Upon her return, she established a production self-help welfare station for the families of military personnel in Chongqing. From 1943 to 1944, she taught part-time at the sociology department of Jinling University. In 1950, she became the general secretary of the National Executive Committee of the Chinese Christian Women’s Association.

In September 1949, Deng Yuzhi became a Christian representative in the Political Consultative Conference alongside Wu Yaozong, Liu Liangmo, Zhang Xueyan, and Zhao Zicheng. In 1950, she was one of the initiators of the Three-Self Reform Declaration. In April 1951, she was elected as a standing committee member of the preparatory committee for the Three-Self Reform Movement. In July, at the accusation meeting organized by the YMCA and the Young Women's Association, she accused American imperialism of exploiting the Young Women's Association for acts of aggression. In 1954, she was elected as a standing committee member of the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee. During the second national Christian conference in 1961, she was re-elected as the vice-chairman.

In the early days of the new nation, she served as a director of the National Relief Association and as a director of the Chinese Red Cross Society. She passed away on October 1, 1996, at the age of 96.

(4) Zhang Xueyan (Chen Jianming, Wang Jingqiang 2012)

Zhang Xueyan (1901-1950), from Weifang, Shandong, began her studies at Weixian Le Daoyuan (the predecessor of Wenhua Academy) at age 13 but dropped out three years later due to poverty. At 17, she went to the European battlefield as a laborer, responsible for road construction and transporting supplies. She returned to China in 1919 and was hired as an English teacher at Guangwen Middle School run by the Presbyterian Church in Northern America, where she was baptized and became a Christian. In 1922, she took a job in Tianjin working with shipping before enlisting to serve her country in Nanjing, but left after witnessing corruption in the military. In 1929, she worked as an editor at the Guangxue Society in Shanghai.

From 1930 to 1933, she studied at Jinling Theological Seminary. After graduating, she joined the National Association of the Shanghai Christian Youth Association as a religious education officer. In August 1934, she co-founded the semi-monthly publication "Tianjia" in Jinan with Sun Ensan, which was affiliated with the North China Christian Rural Advancement Society, aimed at enhancing the knowledge of farmers. From August 1937 to the summer of 1940, she first studied for a year at the University of Toronto and then transferred to Cornell University in the US, where she earned a doctorate in sociology. In September 1940, she returned to China, arriving in Chengdu in the rear area, continuing to edit "Tianjia," while also teaching and serving as the head of the sociology department at Qilu University.

At the end of 1944, Zhang, along with intellectuals from the technology, education, and cultural sectors in Chongqing, initiated the "Democratic Science Forum," advocating for "unity in democracy and relentless resistance." On September 3, 1945, the Democratic Science Forum was renamed the "Jiu San Forum." On May 4, 1946, she established the "Jiu San Society" in Chongqing, serving as a director and was later elected as an executive director.

In August 1946, Zhang went to Beiping to prepare for the resumption of "Tianjia" and participated in activities against hunger, oppression, Chiang Kai-shek, and American influence. In 1947, the Kuomintang declared the Democratic League an "illegal organization" and ordered its dissolution. The Jiu San Society, along with other democrats and professors from Peking University and Tsinghua University, issued a protest letter.

In early 1948, due to her active participation in the democratic movement, she was wanted by the Kuomintang, prompting her to take a detour through Hong Kong to speak in the United States. She returned to China in 1949 and attended the new Political Consultative Conference as a representative of the Christian community in September. Zhang passed away from a cerebral hemorrhage in Beijing in January 1950, at the age of just 49.

(5) Zhu Youyu (Zhu Youyu 1972; Qian Lifeng n.d.)

Zhu Youyu (Andrew Y. Y. Tsu, 1885-1986), from Songjiang, Jiangsu, was born in Shanghai. His father, Zhu Yutang, was a pastor at St. Stephen’s Church in Shanghai. The name "Youyu" means "friend of fish," reflecting the hope that he would be like the disciple Andrew, drawing people as one would catch fish. In his youth, he attended St. John's University and graduated in 1907. He was ordained as a church minister and assigned to serve at St. Andrew’s Church in Wuxi, Jiangsu. In 1909, he traveled to the United States to study, first attending General Theological Seminary in New York (Bachelor of Divinity B.D.) and later Columbia University. In 1912, he received his Ph.D. with the dissertation titled "The Spirit of Chinese Philanthropy." During this time, he was ordained as a minister at St. John's Cathedral in New York.

Upon returning to China in 1912, he became a sociology professor at St. John's University. He also served as the president of the Shanghai Boy Scouts Association, the president of the Shanghai Alumni Association of St. John's University, the China Secretary of the American University Club, a member of the editorial board of the "Chinese Recorder," a member of the publishing committee of the Christian Literature Society of China, a member of the national committee of the YMCA, and a member of the China Continuation Committee. At the same time, he was a board member of the World’s Chinese Students’ Federation.

In 1924, Zhu Youyu became the chaplain of Peking Union Medical College. In 1925, Sun Yat-sen passed away at the Peking Union Medical College, and his memorial service was conducted by Zhu Youyu and Liu Tingfang.

During the war, Zhu Youyu provided medical services for the front lines from the rear. In December 1939, the Episcopal College of the Chinese Anglican Church approved the establishment of the Yunnan-Guizhou Mission, which was under the jurisdiction of the Guangdong-Hong Kong Diocese. In May 1940, seven bishops of the Chinese Anglican Church—John Curtis (Zhejiang Diocese), Frank Houghton (Dongchuan Diocese), William P. Roberts (Jiangsu Diocese), Zheng Hefu (Henan Diocese), Mo Shouzeng (Guangdong-Hong Kong Diocese), Daniel Trumbull Huntington (Wanggan Diocese), and Francis L. Norris (North China Diocese)—consecrated him as the bishop of the Yunnan-Guizhou Mission at Trinity Church, Shanghai, with the diocese office established in Kunming. In August 1947, the Yunnan-Guizhou Diocese was officially established with Huang Kuiyuan as the bishop. Zhu Youyu served as the first general secretary of the newly established Central Office of the Chinese Anglican Church, located in Shanghai.

In October 1949, the National Christian Council of China decided to convene a national Christian conference themed "Christianity and the New Era," with Zhu Youyu serving as the chairman of the organizing committee. However, following Zhou Enlai's meeting with Christian leaders in May and Wu Yaozong's initiation of the Three-Self Reform Declaration, the national conference was announced to be canceled in June. On June 30, the Executive Committee of the General Synod of the Chinese Anglican Church and the College of Bishops held a joint meeting in Shanghai, resolving that no one could represent the Chinese Anglican Church or the diocese to sign any declaration without the authorization of the Executive Committee, specifically referring to "The Road Forward for Christianity in China" (the Three-Self Reform Declaration). On July 5, the joint meeting formally released the "Letter to Fellow Members of the Chinese Anglican Church," which articulated that Christianity could not compromise with imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism while also denouncing "catering to the powerful and exploiting the masses" as contrary to the Christian spirit. The letter affirmed the achievements of the Anglican Church in the Three-Self reforms and promised to achieve the goal of self-renewal in the shortest possible time. It acknowledged that there were "bad apples" within the church, but stated that their actions only represented a minority and "could not represent the entire Christian community." In other words, the individual transgressions of certain missionaries or Christians should not lead to a wholesale rejection of the values of Christianity. This was obviously a targeted response to the stance regarding the relationship between Christianity and imperialism within the reform declaration. Zhu Youyu emphasized that this letter addressed to all members nationwide was a response generated by the reform declaration because the declaration was made by "a small group of fewer than a dozen individuals," most of whom "were not close to the church" and spoke on behalf of Christians without authorization. The position of the College of Bishops explained why there were no representatives from the Chinese Anglican Church acting as co-initiators. It can be said that the Chinese Anglican Church became the mainstream denomination openly resisting the declaration.

However, as the "People's Daily" publicly supported the reform declaration in September, the 14th annual meeting of the Council also shifted to support the declaration. The changing external circumstances forced the Anglican Church to alter its stance. As early as July 1950, the Central Committee of the World Council of Churches held a plenary meeting in Toronto, Canada, where Zhu Youyu was a committee member and was granted permission to travel abroad for the meeting. Afterward, he visited his children studying in the United States and attended their graduation ceremony. At the end of August of the same year, he went to California to bid farewell to his daughter, leaving his wife and daughter to live together while he returned to China. According to his autobiography, he retired from his church position on November 30, 1950, and left China on December 7. However, later, Zhu was accused, and Bishop Chen Jianzhen stated that Zhu resigned from his position as general secretary of the Central Office of the Chinese Anglican Church, preparing to serve as a bishop in the South China Diocese. After leaving Shanghai on December 7, 1950, he passed through Hong Kong en route to Los Angeles, USA.

In April 1950, Bishop Chen Jianzhen attended the "Meeting on Handling Christian Organizations Accepting U.S. Subsidies" convened by the State Council as the chairman of the Episcopal College of the Chinese Anglican Church and accused Zhu Youyu of being a "lackey of American imperialism." In 1958, Zhu published his English autobiography Friend of Fishermen (translated into Chinese in 1972 as《朱友漁自傳》). Zhu Youyu passed away in the United States in 1986 at the age of 101.

(6) Wang Zhixin (Huang Jinhui 1999; He Qingchang 2000; Xu Yihua 2004: 1-26)

Wang Zhixin (Wang Chih Hsin, 1881-1968) was born in Wuxing County, Zhejiang. He received traditional education from a young age and obtained the title of Gongsheng. He later worked as a Chinese language teacher at Dongwu Third Middle School in Wuxing, Huaying School, Shanghai Biwen Girls' School, and Huihe Girls' School. During the Boxer Rebellion, Wang viewed Christianity as a foreign religion but later converted to Christianity and joined the Methodist Episcopal Mission, South. From 1913 to 1916, Wang served as the editor of "Xinhua Daily" at the invitation of missionary A. P. Parker. From 1919 to 1925, he taught Chinese philosophy at Nanjing Jinling Theological Seminary, and from 1926 to 1928, he participated in the Chinese Christian Literature Society as the editor of its monthly publication. After leaving the society, he, along with Zhang Shizhang and Shen Sizhuang, founded the "New Literature Society" in September and edited "Ye Sheng." Additionally, Wang was invited to teach Chinese at Xiehe University in Fujian, later being promoted to the dean of the College of Literature and chair of the Chinese department. In 1934, he was invited by Liu Zhanyin, the president of Hujiang University in Shanghai, to serve as the head of the Chinese department. In 1948, he retired from Hujiang University and returned to Jinling Theological Seminary to work as the editor of the "Jinling Theological Journal" on a semi-voluntary basis.

From the 1920s to the 1940s, Wang Zhixin introspectively addressed the issue of indigenization of Christianity. He explored the relationship between Christianity and traditional culture, as well as the incorporation of indigenous customs into festivals. He actively responded to contemporary events, reflecting on the societal and temporal significance of faith. He published a large number of articles in "Xinhua Daily," "Theological Journal," "Literary Society," "Ye Ye," "Youth Progress," "True Light," and "Tianfeng." His authored works include "A Study of Christian Buddhism" (1924), "The Concept of God in Chinese History" (1925), and "Pastoral Ministry and Chinese Culture" (1926, as editor). Other works include "The Sources of Chinese Scholarship" (1925), "A Study of Mozi" (1925), "The Philosophy of Confucius" (1925), "Chinese Stories in Ancient Texts with Modern Translations" (1926), "A Study of Mencius" (1933), "The System of Chinese Scholarship" (1934), "Zhuangzi" (1937), "An Outline of the History of Chinese Religious Thought" (1940), and "An Annotated History of Chinese Culture" (1943). He also paid attention to the history of the Chinese church, compiling "A History of Christianity in China" (1925) and writing "An Outline of the History of Christianity in China" (1940) and "A Textbook on the History of Christianity in China" (1940).

As a member of the Kuomintang, Wang also examined the relationship between Christianity and the Three Principles of the People. His other works include "The Basis of the Three Principles of the People in Chinese Culture" (1927), "An Outline of the Study of the Three Principles of the People" (1931), and "Essences of National Reconstruction" (1936).

After the founding of the People's Republic of China, Wang Zhixin publicly self-criticized his book "An Outline of the History of Christianity in China" in 1951, stating that the book failed to recognize imperialist aggression and contained ideological toxins. Shortly thereafter, he began organizing historical materials on Christianity in China, facing criticism for not having a strong people's stance. Wang publicly apologized for this. In 1957, he used the pseudonym "Er Wang" to express his evaluation of the Chinese self-reliance movement, which again sparked controversy due to position issues.

Wang Zhixin passed away in 1968 at the age of 87 from a cerebral hemorrhage at his home in Beijing.

(7) Li Chuwen (Gao Yuan 2016; Li Chuwen 2008: 90-97; Xu Jiatun 1993: 29; Wu Kangmin 2007)

Li Chuwen (Li Chun Wen, Sherwood Li, 1918-2018), from Cixi, Zhejiang, was born in Ningbo and attended Jicheng Primary School in Ningbo. In 1932, he fled to Shanghai, and in 1935, while studying at the affiliated high school of Guanghua University, he participated in anti-Japanese parades supporting the Peking student's "129 Movement." In 1937, he was admitted to the chemistry department of Hujiang University, during which he became a Christian and met Liu Liangmo. At the time, his education was funded by his elder brother, who worked in shipping and was a businessman in Shanghai.

In 1938, Li Chuwen (Li Chun Wen, Sherwood Li) participated in the Shanghai Social Science Training Institute, where he began to be exposed to Marxism. In May 1939, he joined the Communist Party of China and became the president of the Shanghai Christian Students' Federation (Shanghai Lian). After graduating in 1940, he began working with the YMCA. Following the outbreak of the Pacific War in 1941, he left Shanghai for Chongqing with Jiang Wenhan, a staff member of the national YMCA student department. In Chongqing, he met Zhou Enlai at the Xinhua Daily office on Qixing Hill, where he reported on the situation of student movements in Shanghai. Zhou recognized the good cover provided by Li's brother's relationship with the entrepreneur Yu Qiaqing and encouraged him to make good use of these social connections in his work. Zhou directed him to participate in the military service department established by the YMCA for the Chinese Expeditionary Army in Myanmar, as the army included young students, intellectuals, workers, and farmers. Zhou also provided him with the name and address of a contact in Myanmar. Li then traveled from Chongqing to Kunming, but due to the destruction of the road from Kunming through Tengchong to Myanmar by the Japanese military, he turned to the Yunnan YMCA. Li reported these circumstances to Zhou through Zhou Enlai's political secretary Gong Peng, who suggested that Li stay in Kunming.

In May 1942, Li arrived in Kunming and served as a student officer for the YMCA. Through another officer, he met Mei Yiqi, the president of Southwest Associated University. Mei agreed to allow Li to set up a student services organization at Southwest Associated University and provided him a space adjacent to the school. In 1943, he established a student relief committee that raised donations from domestic and international sources. In the fall, the student service center was officially established. He set up a reading room, auditorium, recreation room, tea room, shower room, and barbershop in the student service center. The reading room subscribed to the Nationalist Party's "Central Daily" and "Sao Dang Bao," as well as the Communist Party's "Xinhua Daily." He also provided steamed buns and soybean milk for breakfast to financially struggling students. For those in need, there were medical grants available, and priority was given to "progressive students" for scholarships. The student service organization supported students in their pursuit of democracy and organized activities in Yunnan during the summer of 1944 and winter of 1945 to perform Lao She's play "The Country Above All," promoting anti-Japanese national salvation. Li also received donations from a patriotic businessman in Kunming to support professors such as Wen Yiduo, Wu Han, Pan Guangdan, Zeng Zhaolun, and Hua Luogeng, and assist them in participating in anti-Japanese activities.

In addition, Li met several U.S. "Flying Tigers" ground crew members responsible for mapping during a party at a bishop's home in Kunming. He spoke with them about the situation in China's resistance against Japan, particularly how the Communist Party was conducting guerrilla warfare behind enemy lines. He interpreted or translated the content of "Xinhua Daily" and "Qunzhong" for them. Following the victory in the war, he even assisted three individuals in meeting with Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai in Chongqing.

From 1946 to 1949, Li Chuwen was recommended by the national YMCA to serve as an officer at the World Alliance of YMCAs in Geneva, Switzerland. From 1949 to 1950, he pursued theological studies at Yale University in the United States. In 1950, before finishing his degree, he returned to China and served as an officer in the religious education department of the national YMCA. He also served as a pastor at the International Chapel in Shanghai.

In April 1951, in his capacity as an officer in the religious education department of the national YMCA, he signed the "Joint Declaration of Representatives of Various Christian Organizations in China." When the preparatory committee for the Three-Self Reform Movement was formed, Li was elected as an executive committee member. During the establishment of the first cadre training class for the Three-Self Reform Movement in October 1951, he served as a committee member and vice-chairman of the learning committee.

During the accusation movement, there were excessive attacks on the "spiritual faction." In November 1952, Li expressed in an article for "Tianfeng" that while exposing imperialism's use of the spiritual faction, the "spiritual faction" should not be treated as a target for exposure and attack. He asserted that the main obstacle to the Three-Self Reform was imperialism, "and certainly not the so-called 'spiritual faction.'" In other words, patriotic and devout Christians must recognize that the only boundary the Three-Self Reform movement needs to delineate is "the enemy line between the Chinese people and imperialism," and the targets to strike in the movement "can only be imperialism," rather than the "spiritual faction." He particularly emphasized: "The Three-Self Movement has never and will never demand changes to the beliefs of the 'spiritual faction' or of any other denomination." His articles in "Tianfeng" were mostly religious in nature, such as "I Am the Resurrection and the Life" (1953) and "Romans Chapter Seven" (1954). Even during the Great Leap Forward, this was the case, with articles like "Being Content and Not Being Content" and "Jesus Called Christ" (1958).

In 1954, the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was established, and he held the key position of secretary-general.

After the outbreak of the Cultural Revolution, Li Chuwen also faced criticism and struggle sessions. In 1976, he was isolated by the rebellious factions in Shanghai. In 1977, he was transferred to the Shanghai Municipal Government Foreign Affairs Office as deputy director, concurrently serving as the director of the Shanghai Institute of International Studies and the Shanghai Overseas Chinese Affairs Office, where his party membership began to be "exposed."

In June 1983, he was dispatched to Hong Kong as the deputy director of the Xinhua News Agency's Hong Kong branch. One of his responsibilities was to engage in united front work with the religious community in Hong Kong. At that time, Xu Jiatun, the head of the Xinhua News Agency's Hong Kong branch, referred to Li as a "secret party member working in religious affairs." Hong Kong leftist figure Wu Kangmin described Li Chuwen as follows: "Had it not been for the exposure of his party membership, this soft-spoken and refined pastor, who could preach in English, might have continued to work within the church and even represented the religious community to promote party and state leaders."

In 1988, Li Chuwen was transferred back to Shanghai, serving as a foreign affairs advisor to the Shanghai Municipal Government and as the head of the Shanghai Federation of Social Sciences. Starting in 1993, he held the position of director of the China Welfare Association. He passed away in Shanghai on March 22, 2018, at the age of 100.

(8) Tsui Hsien Hsiang (Chen Zhiheng 2016: 211-266)

Tsui Hsien Hsiang (崔憲詳, H. H. Tsui, 1895-1978), courtesy name Xizhang, was from Gaoyuan, Shandong. In his youth, he studied at Cuiying Middle School in Tai'an and converted to Christianity. In 1912, he was accepted to Huiwun University in Beijing and Yenching University to study liberal arts and theology, during which he participated in protests against the signing of the "Twenty-One Demands" and the May Fourth Movement. After graduating in 1920, he served as a pastor for the American Methodist Episcopal Church in Tai'an. In 1923, he went to the United States for further studies, and in 1926, he earned a Doctor of Theology degree from Drew University. After returning to China, he taught at the Theological Seminary of Qilu University in Shandong. In 1931, he was hired as an evangelism officer for the National Christian Council of China. In 1935, he became the executive officer of the National Council of the Chinese Christian Church.

In 1939, the National Council planned to relocate its office from Shanghai to Chengdu, but the plan was temporarily shelved after the passing of General Secretary Cheng Jingyi in November. Tsui Hsien Hsiang remained in the Shanghai concession. In 1940, the National Council officially appointed Tsui as General Secretary, but he firmly declined. After the Pacific War, the National Council's temporary guidance committee decided to establish a temporary committee in January 1942, with Zhang Bohui, the representative in Western China, serving as executive secretary. In April 1943, after Tsui left Shanghai for Chengdu, he was elected as the General Secretary of the temporary committee.

In February 1946, the National Council standing committee met in Shanghai to discuss the relocation of the Chengdu headquarters back to Shanghai. Tsui presided over the affairs of the National Council and the plan for the church's reorganization. During the Chinese Civil War, Tsui was involved with 12 Christian leaders in issuing a declaration calling for an end to the civil war. In December 1948, the National Council expressed its attitude towards the situation. In October 1949, the National Christian Council of China decided to convene a national Christian conference under the theme "Christianity and the New Era," with Tsui serving as the vice-chairman of the organizing committee. In November 1949, Tsui and 19 other church leaders sent a letter to foreign missionary organizations, stating that the Chinese church must continue to develop under the new regime.

In May 1950, Tsui Hsien Hsiang participated in a North China delegation and was received by Zhou Enlai. During this time, Tsui mentioned to Zhou that church members had previously proposed the "Three-Self" principles of self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation but had not been properly implemented. Zhou appreciated the "Three-Self" proposal, which later became a key point in the reform declaration. Shortly thereafter, Tsui Hsien Hsiang became one of the initiators of the Three-Self Declaration. In early 1951, Tsui, along with Wu Yaozong, Chen Wenyuan, and Wu Gaozi, initiated the "Support the Government's Policy — Declaration of the Christian Community in Shanghai." In April, the government held a meeting to "Handle Christian Organizations Accepting American Subsidies," during which an accusation meeting was held where Tsui became the first church leader to stand up and accuse, targeting the American Presbyterian missionary Frank W. Price. At this meeting, Tsui was elected to the standing committee of the preparatory committee for the Three-Self Reform Movement. In May, Tsui again accused American imperialism of exploiting the council at another meeting of the council. In June, at a mass accusation meeting in Shanghai, Tsui again publicly accused Price, labeling him an "imperialist agent." In August, the National Council of the Chinese Christian Church also held an accusation meeting. In 1954, the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was established, with Tsui serving as vice-chairman. From 1952 onward, he was also elected as a standing member of the Shanghai Political Consultative Conference.

In 1960, Tsui Hsien Hsiang was elected as a standing committee member at the second national conference of Christianity.

He passed away in 1978.

(9) Tu Yuqing (Tu Jizheng, Li Yihua 2007)

Tu Yuqing (Y. C. Tu, Tu Yu-Ching, 1895-1975), originally named Tu Zixi, was born in Hanyang, Hubei, with ancestral roots in Huanggang, Hubei. Born into a Christian family, he was baptized at a young age. His grandfather, Tu Songshan, was a pastor in the Methodist Church. He attended a church school in Hanyang during elementary and junior high school. In 1906, he entered Bowun Academy in Wuchang. After graduating in 1912, he stayed on as an English teacher. In 1913, he enrolled in Tsinghua School and the following year went to the United States. He studied in Wesleyan University in Connecticut for liberal arts, Massachusetts Institute of Technology for civil engineering, and Columbia University in New York for business, earning his master's degree in 1918. After returning to China in 1919, he taught at Nanjing Higher Normal School and Southeast University (the predecessor of Central University). After Sun Yat-sen's death in 1925, the government decided to construct Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum in Nanjing. Tu worked with other engineering personnel on the boulevard leading to the mausoleum.

In 1927, he joined Hujiang University as a faculty member. In 1930, he received a Rockefeller Foundation scholarship to further his studies in atomic physics at the University of Chicago. In 1932, he earned a Ph.D. in physics and returned to teach at Hujiang University, also serving as the head of the physics department.

In June 1946, Tu Yuqing planned to resign from his teaching position at Hujiang to accept an invitation from the National Council of the Chinese Christian YMCA to serve as deputy general secretary. However, he later accepted an invitation from Dr. Yen Huiqing, the Board of St. John's University, to become the president of the university. Although Tu was not from an Anglican background, he took on the challenge, particularly hoping to promote the establishment of a Christian university union.

After assuming office, Tu advocated for the establishment of a weekly student assembly and personally presided over the meetings to communicate institutional measures to students, teaching them the importance of diligence in both character and studies. He cared deeply for the students, frequently checking on their living and learning conditions. Administratively, he suggested renovations to the Chinese faculty dormitory at Siyuan Hall, establishing a dormitory for girls, allowing extracurricular student activities, and resuming the publication of the English weekly "St. John’s Dial." More importantly, he completed the registration of the university with the government. At the same time, he dedicated himself to promoting the establishment of the East China Christian Union University, discussing this matter with Li Zhaohuan, the president of Zhejiang University, and Yang Yongqing, the president of Soochow University.

In 1947, during the Chinese Civil War, inflation and government corruption triggered student movements across the country, impacting St. John's University as well. Tu, who advocated for democracy and recognized freedom of speech, realized he could not pressure students or confine them to the campus. In May, student strikes, protests, and petitions erupted nationwide. On May 20, the tragic incident occurred where Nationalist troops shot students at Wuhan University, inciting national outrage among students. At that time, St. John's University students planned to strike and initiate a propaganda campaign. The growing student movement caused anxiety among the Anglican leadership and dissatisfaction with Tu's administrative style.

In 1948, Tu Yuqing resigned as president and accepted an invitation from the National Council of the Chinese Christian YMCA to become its general secretary. In mid-September 1948, the North American YMCA invited him to visit the United States and examine the work of the North American YMCA. He initially visited Canada and then various places in the United States. Meanwhile, the political situation in China was undergoing rapid changes. In early January 1949, he returned to Shanghai via Hong Kong, leading the YMCA to embrace the challenges of a new era.

In May 1950, he was one of the Christian leaders received by Zhou Enlai and later became an initiator of the Three-Self Reform Declaration proposed by Wu Yaozong. In April 1951, he was elected to the presidium of the "Conference on Handling Christian Organizations Accepting American Subsidies" and served as an executive committee member of the preparatory committee for the Christian Three-Self Reform Movement. In July 1951, at the accusation meeting of the YMCA, Tu Yuqing accused American imperialism of exploiting the YMCA to invade China before and after liberation, while also reflecting on his own experiences and expressing his willingness to strengthen learning.

At the end of 1951, during the Three Anti and Five Anti Campaigns, Tu Yuqing was labeled a "big tiger" and underwent multiple rounds of criticism and struggle sessions. He was hospitalized due to gastric bleeding from extreme stress. However, the "activists" leading the YMCA’s campaign forcibly brought him to the venue, accusing him of having underhanded relations with imperialist elements. Unable to endure such great humiliation, Tu once attempted to jump from a sixth-floor window but was stopped by his wife and family, who prevented him from carrying out his suicidal thoughts. The authorities in the YMCA, however, continued their pressure, confining him and deputy general secretary Jiang Wenhan in an office and demanding confessions. Tu requested to resign as general secretary, but his request was denied, and he remained nominally the general secretary of the national YMCA.

From 1954 to 1955, he was invited to teach physics at Nanjing Normal University, and from 1955 to 1957, he taught physics at Suzhou Normal University. In 1957, he was appointed head of the physics department at Shanghai Normal University. He largely distanced himself from the YMCA and moved out of the YMCA dormitory. However, he still held the position of general secretary of the national YMCA and would sign documents or attend meetings as required. During the Cultural Revolution, he was subjected to isolation and criticism sessions. At one point, he attempted to commit suicide by cutting an artery in his neck with a razor but was saved and later permitted to recuperate at home. However, in July 1968, he and his wife were brought to Shanghai Normal University, where they were separated for further isolation. Nine months later, his wife was sent home, but Tu was sent to a "Christian Learning Class" at 59 Hongkou Road to continue enduring criticism.

In November 1969, Tu was imprisoned at the former Shanghai Youth Detention Center on Caoxi Road and later transferred to a prison near Hongqiao Airport, living as a criminal while in handcuffs. Under severe coercion, he completely broke down mentally and was forced to admit to the charges against him. Tu Yuqing was held on charges of belonging to a "Christian American Special Infiltration Organization" until he was released and returned home at the end of 1972. Due to his poor mental state, he required medical treatment at Shanghai Mental Hospital. He passed away on September 11, 1975.

Christian Population in China (Ying 2009: 65, 75-78)

In 1949, a nationwide survey was conducted by the National Christian Council, which concerned the number of Christians in China. Although the data was not complete, it still held a certain level of authority. This survey was coordinated by Bi Fanyu (Frank W. Price), with the initial edition published in early October 1949 and reprinted in January 1950; both versions were trial prints and contained no differences. In December 1950, the council published a revised lead print edition. A comparison of the two versions shows that for the total number of "church members," the January edition recorded 792,862 individuals (including 18 denominations), while the December edition showed 902,805 (including 21 denominations). At the same time, the council also compiled "A Brief Compilation of Statistics from Various Christian Denominations in China 1949-1950" (a handwritten manuscript) in 1950, which included statistical data from different denominations. The "Compilation" recorded numbers from several denominations not documented in the "Survey," totaling 1,031,021 members. It can be said that the "Survey" and the "Compilation" provided insights into the number of Christians in China and their denominational distribution before and after the establishment of the People’s Republic of China. However, because the data provided by various denominations did not adhere to a consistent year standard (for example, the China Inland Mission provided data from 1934), and because the number of denominations was not exhaustive, the actual number of Christians should exceed one million.

After the establishment of the People’s Republic of China, the State Council requested that church groups register. According to statistics from the Religious Affairs Bureau, in 1950 the total number of believers from 22 Christian denominations across the country was 1,005,699. In fact, some denominations submitted figures to the State Council that were the same as those in the "Compilation" and "Survey," but also included the number of members from church congregations and spiritual service groups. A comparison of the two indicates that most denominations reported the same membership figures, reflecting that the denominations merely submitted existing records during registration. Notably, the State Council’s archival documents recorded the number of members from local denominations such as the Christian Assembly, True Jesus Church, Jesus Family, and Self-supporting Church, which not only helps us understand the status of these local groups but also allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the total number of Christians in China. Regardless, it is currently generally stated that the number of Christians in the early years of the new China is around 700,000, a figure which warrants correction.

(1) Preparatory Committee for the Three-Self Patriotic Movement of Chinese Christianity during the Korean War, 1951 (Xing Fuzeng 1996: 55-62; Xing Fuzeng 2012b: 58-64; Xing Fuzeng 2023: 119-120)

After the Chinese Communist Party intervened in the Korean War, the U.S. government announced on December 16, 1950, that it would control properties belonging to both China and North Korea within its jurisdiction and prohibited U.S. vessels from sailing to Chinese ports. In response to the freezing of Chinese public and private assets in the U.S., the State Council of the Central People's Government issued an order on December 28 to control American property in China and freeze American public and private deposits in China. These actions severely affected the missionary work within China that still relied on American funds for sustenance. On December 29, the State Council announced policies regarding the handling of cultural, educational, relief institutions, and religious organizations that received American subsidies, stating that "Chinese religious groups receiving American subsidies should be transformed into organizations fully managed by Chinese believers, and the government encourages their self-reliance and self-sustenance movement."

From April 16 to 21, 1951, the Religious Affairs Bureau of the Ministry of Education of the Central People's Government held a "Meeting on Handling American Subsidized Christian Organizations" in Beijing. The purpose of this meeting, convened by the government, was to "ultimately, thoroughly, and permanently end the cultural invasion of imperialism that has lasted for over a century." Although the meeting was named to address "the handling of American-subsidized Christian organizations," it was essentially not merely dealing with "technical" issues. A comprehensive view of the meeting’s contents indicates that its core topic was how to accelerate the severing of ties between Christianity and imperialism and how to initiate a "struggle" between the church and American imperialism. As Lu Dingyi, Deputy Director of the Ministry of Culture and Education, clearly pointed out at the meeting, "Dealing with American-subsidized Christian organizations is not a technical issue; it is a struggle. The purpose of the struggle is to eradicate the influence of American imperialism’s cultural invasion over our country that has lasted for over a century." Furthermore, the cultural invasion by American imperialism is not only a historical problem but also a current fact that continues to manifest through Christianity.

As a result, over 150 church representatives from across the country, including many self-supporting churches unrelated to Western denominations, such as Jingdianying (Jesus Family), Ni Tuosheng (Christian Assembly), and Wei Yisa (True Jesus Church), approved the "Measures for Handling American-subsidized Christian Organizations" and the "Joint Declaration of Representatives from Various Christian Churches and Organizations in China." Simultaneously, to more comprehensively promote the Christian reform movement and lead the Chinese church in the task of building a new China, the Beijing meeting decided to establish a new institution—the "Preparatory Committee for the Three-Self Patriotic Movement of Chinese Christianity," which would be responsible for coordinating the reform work of Christianity, with Wu Yaozong as the chairman. All Christian groups and denominations, including the National Christian Council, were to conduct learning and reforms under the guidance of the preparatory committee.

The establishment of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee not only focused on reforming Christianity but also reflected the CCP’s intention to establish patriotic religious organizations in various major religions to achieve unified leadership in the religious realm. In the 1950s, various religions sequentially established patriotic religious organizations supported by the party and the state: the China Buddhist Association (1953), the China Islamic Association (1953), the China Taoist Association (1957), and the Patriotic Catholic Committee of China (1957).

(2) Jinling University and Theological Seminary, 1953 (Xu Rulei 2022: 244-248; Wang Weifan 2013: 1-2)

At the end of 1950, as China intervened in the Korean War and U.S.-China relations fully ruptured, the Chinese government announced a freeze on all U.S. assets in China. The financial aid that Christian missionary work had traditionally relied upon was therefore cut off, and various theological schools faced the brink of closure. In 1951, to reduce expenditures, Jinling Theological Seminary merged with Jinling Women’s Theological Seminary, marking a precedent for school mergers.

In August 1952, a theological education seminar was held in Shanghai, East China, which decided to establish a preparatory committee for the East China United Theological Seminary. In November, Jinling University and Theological Seminary was officially established in Nanjing, formed by the merger of 11 theological colleges from the East China region: Shanghai Anglican Central Theological Seminary, Shanghai Baptist Theological Seminary, Hangzhou China Theological Seminary, Nanjing Jinling Theological Seminary (and Jinling Women’s Theological Seminary), Wuxi North China Theological Seminary, Jinan Qilu Theological Seminary, Zhangzhou Minnan Theological Seminary, Fuzhou United Theological Seminary, Ningbo Trinity Bible College, Zhenjiang Baptist Bible College, and Jinan Mingdao Bible College.

The 11 theological colleges included seminaries under church universities, denominationally affiliated seminaries, and independent theological institutions. The theological stances represented varied from fundamentalist traditions to modernist theologians. At that time, the fundamentalist representatives suggested merging into two seminaries—modernists uniting at Nanjing Jinling Theological Seminary and fundamentalists uniting at Wuxi North China Theological Seminary; however, the meeting was unable to reach a consensus on these two opinions. Luo Zhufeng, the head of the East China Religious Affairs Bureau, stated, "Under the premise of opposing imperialism and being patriotic, all theological thoughts have the freedom to exist; the government will not intervene, nor can Chinese theological thoughts be solely defined by one standard. In other words, since religious faith is free, our theological thoughts must also be free." The principle of mutual respect at Jinling University and Theological Seminary originated from this idea. The educational policy or principles were articulated as four "fundamental beliefs": "1. The entire Bible is God's revelation, including all the essential doctrines related to salvation, serving as the foundation of faith for believers and the criterion for conduct. 1. The one true God is the source of all things and the Father of mankind, full of righteousness and love. 1. Jesus Christ is the Son of God, who became flesh for the redemption of humanity, was crucified, and rose from the dead, becoming the head of the church and the Savior of all ages. 1. The Holy Spirit, as one of the Trinity, performs the work of regeneration and sanctification, bestowing various gifts to believers within the church." Note that all four points are numbered "1," without any distinction in order.

To accommodate different theological traditions, Jinling University and Theological Seminary adopted the principle of "mutual respect" in its early establishment. Among the more than 30 faculty members at Jinling, one-third came from a fundamentalist background, while over 60% of the hundred-plus students also belonged to the fundamentalist tradition. Balancing the debate between "modernists" and "fundamentalists" became a crucial issue that the united seminary had to address. At that time, the Jinling Department of Religious Affairs stipulated that several courses within theology and the Bible were to be offered in separate classes according to "modern" or "fundamentalist" perspectives, with no directives for "unified faith."

Between 1957 and 1959, the China Spiritual Seminary, Chongqing Theological Seminary, and Guangzhou United Theological Seminary were merged into Jinling University and Theological Seminary. Both Chongqing Theological Seminary and China Spiritual Seminary were led by the spiritually-oriented Chen Chonggui and Jia Yuming, respectively. Their participation in the Three-Self Movement was likely influenced by a desire to ensure unity among spiritual factions, allowing them to operate independently. However, following the Anti-Rightist Campaign, many spiritual factions within the Three-Self organizations were largely purged; Chen Chonggui was classified as a rightist, and Jia Yuming was also targeted internally, resulting in both spiritually-influenced theological institutions being merged into Jinling University and Theological Seminary.

In 1961, Yanjing Theological Seminary was merged into Jinling, making Jinling University and Theological Seminary the only seminary in the country, resuming classes in April 1961. However, due to the Great Leap Forward severely impacting religious activities, Jinling could only offer pastoral training courses. Undergraduate programs resumed in 1962. Nonetheless, beginning in 1962, the "Four Clean-ups" movement (cleansing politics, economy, organizations, and thoughts) affected the seminary as it also ceased to enroll new students. With the outbreak of the Cultural Revolution in 1966, the last remaining seminary established a Red Guard headquarters, leading to the forced closure of the seminary. Chinese theological education entered a dark period lasting over a decade.

(3) Yanjing Theological Seminary, 1953 (Xu Yihua 1999: 68-151)

In December 1950, the United States announced a freeze on all Chinese assets within the U.S., resulting in an immediate financial crisis for many educational, charitable, cultural, and religious organizations that relied on American subsidies. On January 15, 1951, the Ministry of Education convened a meeting of higher education institutions receiving foreign grants, during which Minister Ma Xulun emphasized that church schools were an important activity of American imperialism's cultural invasion. In response to the U.S. freeze on Chinese funds and assets, the State Council decided to take over educational sovereignty, transforming colleges that previously received American subsidies into schools managed by the Chinese people. Following the meeting, the Central People's Government's Ministry of Education and the East China Military and Political Committee's Ministry of Education announced the takeover of the United Medical College and Yanjing University. On February 12, Yanjing University was officially taken over by the Ministry of Education.

After the freeze on U.S. dollars, Yanjing University abruptly lost all its subsidies. With the government taking control of the university, the future of the Religious College became a concern. President Lu Zhiwei of Yanjing suggested reorganizing the Religious College into an academic research institution for various Chinese religions, which would allow the government to permit it to function as a department within the university. However, the dean of the Religious College, Zhao Zichan, preferred to separate the university and the religious college, arguing that after the separation, the only solution would be "to allow the church to support and sustain it." To this end, Zhao convened a meeting of all faculty and students, inviting some alumni from Beijing to participate in the discussions. A few days later, Zhao drafted a document titled "The Future Direction of Yanjing Religious College," proposing that the college be renamed Yanjing Religious College, register with the Beijing Municipal Government, and request the Ministry of Education for official recognition as a private religious college.

After the government took over Yanjing University, it quickly began a patriotic ideological education movement, conducting seminars and denunciation meetings throughout the school aimed at purging the poison of American imperialist cultural invasion. Starting in July 1951, the Religious College at Yanjing also initiated a study of criticism and self-criticism. After several months of study, the Beijing Religious Affairs Bureau planned to hold a meeting at the Religious College to denounce American imperialism's use of religion for invasion. This denunciation meeting against American cultural invasion was held on November 24, lasting an entire day, during which Zhao Zichan made accusations.

During the "Three Anti" and "Five Anti" movements, the Beijing Municipal Committee of the CCP dispatched a "work group" to Yanyuan in December 1951 to lead the movements. The president was sidelined, and classes were suspended for the purpose of the movement. Although the Religious College had already separated from the university, the Discipline and Economy Inspection Committee of Yanjing University still unified control over the activities of the Religious College. In early March 1952, Zhao Zichan, as the dean of the Religious College, faced accusations from the entire school on two occasions, requiring him to undergo a thorough self-examination. Ultimately, since Zhao was considered to have a "stubborn" attitude, in March, the "Economy Inspection Committee" charged him with "utilizing religion to systematically and plannedly spread poisonous information to undermine the new China; being one of the leaders in international reactionary religious organizations; as a representative in the Political Consultative Conference, superficially adhering to government policies while acting against them; resisting the Three-Self Reform and willingly siding with the enemy," and "promptly accepting the masses' demand," led to the revocation of his position as dean, which was temporarily taken over by Jiang Yizhen.

In May 1952, the movement was declared over. In November, the Religious College relocated from Yanyuan. As early as May 1952, Yanjing Religious College, along with Beijing Theological Seminary and United Women's Theological College, began preliminary discussions regarding a merger of the three institutions, and by August, negotiations concerning the merger had commenced. In April 1953, the three theological institutions officially merged to form "Yanjing United Theological Seminary," with Wang Zhongzi and Jiang Yizhen serving as the chairman and vice-chairman of the administrative committee, respectively. In September, the Christian Missionary Alliance Bible College from Beijing, Northeast Theological Seminary from Shenyang, and Huabei Baptist Theological Seminary from Kaifeng, Henan, merged into Yanjing. Between 1954 and 1956, several other theological institutions, including Hubei Lankou Lutheran Theological Seminary, Hunan Bible College, Beijing Christian Assembly Truth Institute, China Central United Theological Seminary, and Beijing Xiangshan Spiritual Institute also joined the merger.

In 1961, Yanjing United Theological Seminary was merged into Jinling United Theological Seminary.

(4) Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee of Chinese Christianity, 1954 (Xing Fuzeng 2010: 111-116)

After the establishment of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee in April 1951, nearly three years of efforts resulted in several spirit-faction churches refusing to participate, citing "differences in faith" and "reforming faith out of faith" as reasons; the influence of the Christian Assembly and Wang Mingdao's Christian Church was particularly significant. Strengthening and expanding unity among Christians nationwide under the overarching principle of "Three-Self Reform" became an essential issue that required attention.

From November 27 to December 16, 1953, the Second National Religious Work Conference was held in Beijing. The conference was presided over by Xi Zhongxun, Minister of the Central Propaganda Department and Deputy Director of the Ministry of Education of the State Council, along with Lu Dingyi, Deputy Director of the Ministry of Education, and summarized the work of Christianity from 1950 to 1953, while establishing future work guidelines. According to the conference documents, in order to "carry forward the patriotic reform movement," the focus of future work would be "to win over a large number of devout believers, while also gaining the majority of clergy." This way, it would be possible "to expand the leadership organization of the 'Chinese Christian Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee' by more substantially including representative upper-level leaders from the church, so that it truly becomes a broad patriotic united front organization for all denominations of Christianity in the country." To achieve this goal, the emphasis should be placed on the "patriotic" slogan, rather than the "Three-Self" or "reform" slogans, with plans to establish an official organization in the first half of 1954, considering using the name "Christian Patriotic Association." To unite more believers, the patriotic reform organization and patriotically active individuals must practically "address the religious lives of believers," serving as a fundamental means of connecting with congregational communities and as a primary condition for religious grassroots work.

From May 17 to 22, 1954, the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee held its second plenary meeting, attended by He Chengxiang, Director of the Central Religious Affairs Bureau, Luo Zhufeng, and Chen Yiming, Director of the Shanghai Religious Affairs Bureau. The meeting decided to convene a national Christian conference in Beijing in the latter half of the year and to establish a "formal leadership organization." Thus, it is evident that the national Christian conference was convened primarily based on the directives of the Central Committee of the Communist Party regarding Christian work, and both the purpose of "great unity" and the name of the new organization were completely aligned with the CCP's review of the Christian reform movement. The party-state was not satisfied with only individual denominations participating in the Three-Self Reform; addressing the resistance of influential spiritual factions to the movement became a focus of Christian work.

Before the 1954 National Christian Conference, the preparatory committee sent invitations to representatives of spiritual factions. Shen Derong, the head of the secretariat for the conference, noted that in order to "further expand the anti-imperialist patriotic unity of Chinese Christianity," they decided to invite "representatives from certain sects and groups that had so far not participated or even opposed the Chinese Christian Three-Self Reform Movement." Ultimately, 27 representatives of spiritual factions participated in the Three-Self conference for the first time, with 15 of them from Shanghai. However, in Beijing, due to Wang Mingdao and other spiritual factions' resistance, five invited representatives ultimately refused to attend.

From July 22 to August 6, 1954, the Chinese Christian National Conference was held in Beijing. Wu Yaozong suggested that, for the sake of unity, the name of the soon-to-be-established formal leadership organization be changed from the previously used "Christian Three-Self Reform Movement" to "Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement," stating that "the word 'reform' easily conjures up associations with historical movements in church history." He pointed out that "some fellow believers mistakenly perceive 'reform' as a means to 'overhaul church systems'; others fear that 'reform' would interfere with their own faith, thus adopting a passive observatory attitude toward the Three-Self Reform Movement." Therefore, renaming it the "Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement" would help eliminate factors hindering church unity. He ultimately emphasized that the Three-Self Patriotic Movement would become "the movement of all believers in China, the great unity of all believers in China. Any denomination, any church and believers with differing beliefs should join efforts on the basis of patriotism and a love for faith to make the greatest contributions to the Three-Self Patriotic Movement." He Chengxiang, head of the Central Religious Affairs Bureau, in his speech at the national Christian conference, criticized those who refused to participate in the patriotic movement on grounds of "different beliefs," yet he still hoped the spiritual factions could return to a common patriotic foundation.

Wu Yaozong was appointed as the chairman of the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee, with Li Chuwen as the general secretary. The conference passed the "Letter to All Fellow Believers," reaffirming that "we understand that the purpose of self-governance is not to unify or change church systems, but to completely sever ties with imperialism, enabling us Chinese Christians to unite to govern the church ourselves; self-sufficiency is not about interfering with each church's economy but to entirely cut off economic ties with imperialism, making each church fully responsible to Chinese believers; self-propagation is not about unifying or modifying beliefs, but to thoroughly eliminate the remnants of imperialism's ideological influence, ensuring that what each church proclaims is the pure gospel consistent with the Bible. As for the differences in faith, structures, and rituals existing among churches, we should respect one another." This can be seen as a response to the spiritual factions.

(5) United Christian Publishing House, 1956 (Xing Fuzeng 2012b: 228-232, 239-260)

According to a survey conducted by the National Christian Council of China in 1950, there were several Christian publishing organizations across the country, including the China Lutheran Publishing Department, the China Baptist Publishing House, the China Christian Publishing Association, the China Christian Sacred Education Publishing Society, Tienfeng Publishing House, Tianjia Biweekly, the Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA) Publishing House, the Evangelical Publishing House, the Shizhao Newspaper, Huaying Publishing House, Guangxie Publishing House, the Guangxue Association, the Spiritual Agenda Society, the Minbei Christian Church Publishing House, the Central Office of the China Anglican Church, Shanghai Gospel Radio Station, and the Gospel Blind Society, totaling 17 organizations.

In January 1951, the East China Military and Political Committee requested that all Christian publishing organizations receiving foreign subsidies register with the relevant departments. Under the new conditions of the Korean War, the Christian publishing sector faced severe challenges. On March 16, a Christian publishing conference was held in Beijing, aimed at assisting the Christian publishing industry in overcoming difficulties arising from the cessation of American funding after the outbreak of the Korean War, and discussing how to sever ties with imperialism and eliminate its influence. The conference determined that the Christian Publishing Association would be responsible for reviewing books to purge imperialist influences and provide guidance to various publishing units. It also proposed the idea of unifying the management of Christian publishing and establishing a joint publishing organization.

After the publishing conference, the association actively promoted the establishment of jointly-operated Christian bookstores. Between March and June 1951, the association convened two discussions with Christian publishing organizations in Shanghai and sought input from institutions nationwide. Notably, various organizations responded by indicating "general support in principle, albeit with some considerations regarding the conditions for actual participation," reflecting a reluctance among existing publishing entities toward the idea of unification. The earliest supporters included only the Guangxue Association, the YMCA Publishing House, and the Baptist Bookstore, which sent representatives (Wu Yaozong, Zhang Shizhang, Hu Zuyin, Zhang Renjing, Jin Yanqing, and Zhang Bohuai) to form a preparatory committee, deciding to establish a central joint entity in Shanghai. However, further realizing the merger of various Christian publishing organizations entailed more complex issues, chiefly centered on whether independent publishing entities were willing to yield and comply with the leadership of the Christian Publishing Association.

Between 1952 and 1953, fewer than ten publishing organizations continued operation, collectively publishing 44 titles, reflecting severe impacts on the Christian publishing industry from political pressure. In 1953, the Christian Publishing Association disbanded itself, stating that it had no further reason to exist. Four units, namely the Guangxue Association, the China Baptist Publishing House, the YMCA National Association Publishing Department, and the China Sunday School Association, formed a joint editorial committee in line with the spirit of the Christian publishing conference, marking the first step toward unification. The committee consisted of key editorial personnel from the four units, with Shen Derong (Deputy Leader of the Propaganda Group of the Preparatory Committee for the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement) serving as executive secretary to coordinate the publishing plans of the four units, while each unit remained responsible for its own publishing work. This indicates that the Three-Self Reform Organization had begun to oversee and coordinate the reform plans for Christian publishing. In 1954 and 1955, the Guangxue Association, China Baptist Publishing House, YMCA Publishing House, and China Sunday School Association jointly edited and published three titles under the Guangxue Association: two volumes of "New Sermon Collections" and "The Life and Teachings of the Prophet Jeremiah" (by Li Rongfang). At the same time, the various publishers continued to independently publish more than 30 titles.

In December 1956, the Guangxue Association, China Baptist Publishing House, China Sunday School Association, and YMCA Publishing House united to form the "United Christian Publishing House of China," with Jiang Wenhan serving as the general secretary. The establishment of the United Christian Publishing House marked the formal entry of Christian publishing into an "integrated" era. After its establishment, 13 books were jointly published in 1957. However, following the Anti-Rightist Campaign, the work of the United Christian Publishing House faced unprecedented impacts. Entering the 1960s, "Tianfeng" no longer contained any advertisements for new book publications, reflecting a suspension of the publishing work of the United Christian Publishing House. During the Cultural Revolution, as the Three-Self organizations ceased operation, the United Publishing House faced a similar fate.

(6) Chinese Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Seminary (Tianfeng 1956a: 28, 1956b: 3; Wang Delong 2017: 146-164)

After the establishment of the People's Republic of China, efforts were made to address the Chinese theological education system, primarily through a unification approach that took advantage of the funding disruptions following the Korean War. The merge of various seminaries across the country resulted in the establishment of three "cooperative" seminaries: Jinling United Theological Seminary (Nanjing), Yanjing United Theological Seminary (Beijing), and Guangzhou United Theological Seminary (Guangzhou). By 1956, these three seminaries had collectively "merged" over 30 theological institutions from various regions.

However, apart from the three "cooperative" seminaries, a few independent seminaries managed to continue operations. The first category consisted of seminaries run by spiritual factions that had joined the Three-Self Patriotic Movement: in addition to the prominent Chongqing Theological Seminary led by Chen Chonggui and Jia Yuming, there was also the Huai'en Amateur Theological College run by Qi Qingcai at the Baptist Church. The second category included independent seminaries led by individuals from these spiritual factions, which operated autonomously due to reservations about the Three-Self Movement. These institutions were smaller in scale, had no organizational ties with foreign missions, and primarily relied on faith-based contributions, allowing them to operate even after the "Liberation." Examples include the Xiangshan Spiritual Institute in Beijing (led by Bi Yongqin), the China Christian Worker's Home in Shanghai (led by Jiao Weizhen), and the Bible Society (Shanghai).

During the national Christian conference held in 1954, the Chinese Christian Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee was established, aiming to secure participation from spiritual factions in the Three-Self initiative. At that time, Jiao Weizhen attended the national conference, while Bi Yongqin refused to participate. However, following the arrest of anti-Three-Self figure Wang Mingdao in 1955, many spiritual faction members, including Bi Yongqin, joined the movement, likely to foster unity among spiritual factions. The China Christian Worker's Home, Huai'en Amateur Theological College, and Bible Society did not merge into Jinling or Yanjing United Theological Seminars but chose to collaborate with Jia Yuming's China Christian Spiritual Institute to form the "Chinese Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Seminary," with Jia Yuming as the dean, Jiao Weizhen and Qi Qingcai as vice-deans, Yang Shaotang as the academic dean, and Zhu Guishen (supervisor of the Shanghai Spiritual Service Group) as the chairman of the board. The mission emphasized that "this collaboration focuses on Christ as the head, concentrating strength within each body to cultivate talents for sacred work who love their country and church in the new China." The educational policy prioritized "spiritual life, along with knowledge consistent with the Bible, aiming for a balanced development in faith and life, knowledge and experience, so students can be used by the Lord to establish the church in the new China."

In October 1956, the seminary held its founding ceremony, during which Jia Yuming stated: "'Chinese Christianity' defines our service target, 'Shanghai' is the location of the seminary, 'spiritual' indicates that our seminary emphasizes experiential faith and abundant life, and 'theological seminary' signifies the study of biblical truth and theological knowledge." The seminary aimed to concentrate efforts on cultivating "holy workers" who meet the needs of the times.

However, following the onset of the Anti-Rightist Campaign, Jia Yuming, Jiao Weizhen, Qi Qingcai, and Yang Shaotang were all summoned to Beijing for study. Both Jia and Yang were classified as designated rightists, leading to the suspension of classes throughout the seminary, requiring faculty and students to participate in learning sessions in Shanghai and engage in drought relief labor in Jiading County. In 1958, the Religious Affairs Bureau directed that Three-Self congregations were not permitted to hire students from the Spiritual Institute as clergy, rescinding previously issued appointment letters due to perceived ideological issues, insisting that they continue their studies. This process of ideological correction ended in March 1959, with students from other provinces sent back home, and the Chinese Christian Shanghai Spiritual Theological Seminary was ordered to close, marking the end of the last seminary operated in collaboration with spiritual factions, which ultimately could not escape the tides of revolution.

(1) National Christian Conference and Promotion Association 14th Annual Meeting 1950（Song Jun (宋軍) 2017: 117-159, 213-248; Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 25-29, 43-47）

At the end of January 1950, the Executive Committee of the National Christian Council of China decided to hold a National Christian Conference in Beijing at Yanjing University from August 19 to 27 under the theme "Christianity and the New Era," and established a dedicated preparatory committee to follow up on related work. The committee was chaired by Zhu Youyu (朱友漁), Bishop of the Chinese Anglican Church, with Tu Yuqing (涂羽卿), General Secretary of the National Youth Association, Bao Zheqing (鮑哲慶), General Secretary of the Zhejiang-Shanghai Baptist Convention, Cui Xianxiang (崔憲祥), General Secretary of the Chinese Christian Church National Assembly, and Jiang Changchuan (江長川), Bishop of the North China Conference of the Chinese Methodist Church, serving as vice chairs. Wu Gaozi (吳高梓), General Secretary of the Promotion Association, was designated as the general secretary, and all were responsible leaders of major national denominations and organizational bodies. The committee decided to form four subgroups for the conference: General Declaration, Ministry Program, Reform Plan, and Cooperative Organizations. On March 28, the preparatory committee of the national conference decided to postpone the full meeting. In July, the preparatory committee’s General Secretary, Pastor Wu Gaozi, submitted his resignation in a standing committee meeting, which was approved by the Executive Committee. In August, the Promotion Association again decided to merge the national conference into the annual meeting to streamline and save costs due to their proximity.

The transition from postponement to cancellation of the national conference reflects the difficulties it faced. On one hand, there were evidently differing opinions within the Chinese church on how to reform. The Promotion Association intended to play the role of the representative of Christianity to promote reform (Promotionists), which obviously presented a divergence from the progressive forces outside the system represented by Tianfeng (天風派). On the other hand, and more importantly, the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) was unwilling to see the national conference proceed smoothly. It was evident that the party-state believed the reform work for Christianity should be led by its trusted ally, Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗), and any consensus reached at the national conference regarding church reform would inevitably become a significant obstacle to Wu Yaozong's reforms. While the Promotion Association was preparing for the national conference, Wu Yaozong, with the support of the party-state, launched a signing campaign for a reform declaration, which became a massive movement in contrast to the Promotion Association and seemingly represented two different paths for the reform direction of Chinese Christianity.

The competition for leadership in church reform between Wu Yaozong and the Promotion Association ultimately concluded with the convening of the 14th annual meeting of the Promotion Association in October 1950. To counter Wu Yaozong's reform declaration, the Promotion Association's Vice General Secretary, Victor E. W. Hayward (海維德), requested that the annual meeting issue a "Letter to All Believers," discussing the Three-Self Reform from a religious standpoint. Wu Yaozong was also excluded from the list of the declaration committee. To ensure the annual meeting's direction aligned with the vision of the Promotion Association, it was proposed that missionaries retain their participation in accordance with the original charter. Additionally, Hayward served as the executive secretary of the annual meeting's procedural committee and discussed materials on the "Church Reform Issues" for the national conference during the annual meeting's proceedings to replace Wu Yaozong’s reform declaration.

On October 9, the Eastern China Military and Political Committee held a seminar, with attendees from the Christian side including Wu Yaozong, Miao Qiusheng (繆秋笙), Jiang Changchuan, Wu Gaozi, Tu Yuqing, Jiang Wenhan (江文漢), Ai Niansan (艾年三), Liu Liangmo (劉良模), Chen Jianzhen (陳見真), Shao Jingsan (邵鏡三), and Zheng Jianye (鄭建業); while government representatives included Shu Tong (舒同), Minister of Propaganda for the Eastern China Military and Political Committee, Pan Hanye (潘漢年), Vice Mayor of Shanghai and Minister of United Front Work of the Eastern Bureau, Zhou Erfu (周而復), Secretary General of the United Front Work of the Eastern Bureau, Mei Dajun (梅達君), United Front Work official, and Zhou Lixing (周力行), Director of the Eastern Religious Affairs Office. The weight of the party and government representatives indicates their strong influence. The seminar ultimately established two points regarding the annual meeting: first, foreign members of the Promotion Association's Executive Committee should not attend the meeting; second, the annual meeting should implement the spirit of the Christian reform declaration.

Under the CCP's active intervention, the 14th annual meeting of the Promotion Association held from October 18 to 25 eventually passed a resolution supporting the declaration of "the Path for Chinese Christianity to Promote New China’s Construction" and called on all believers to enthusiastically participate in signing. Additionally, the annual meeting resolved "with utmost determination, to complete the Three-Self Movement within five years." Wu Yaozong was also elected as Vice President of the National Promotion Association amid supporting and opposing forces. After the meeting, major newspapers in Shanghai reported on this annual meeting with headlines like "Cutting off Christianity's Relationship with Imperialism."

(2) Three-Self Reform Declaration Signing Campaign 1950 （Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 25-52; Song Jun (宋軍) 2017: 161-211; Wickeri 1990: 127-133; Bush 1970: 177-187; MacInnis 1972: 158-160）

The brewing of the Three-Self Reform Declaration originated from discussions held by Christian representatives during their visit to Beijing and their meeting with Zhou Enlai (周恩來). The formation of the Christian visiting group was linked to the preparations for the National Christian Conference. It can be said that both represented the Chinese church's reflection on its future and reforms after the establishment of the new China, while also illustrating the complicated trajectory of the differing factions within the church (Promotionists and Tianfengists) transitioning from unity to division.

In October 1949, the Promotion Association held its first Executive Committee meeting after the establishment of the new China. It decided to convene a national Christian representative conference as soon as possible to connect various denominations across the country and establish a national administrative organization. Concurrently, it resolved to organize a visiting group to liaise with churches in different regions to prepare for the national conference. During the meeting, Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗) expressed a desire to convey the significance and resolutions of the People's Political Consultative Conference to the provinces. Thus, the Promotion Association, the National Association of Chinese Christian Youth, and Christian representatives in the People's Political Consultative Conference jointly initiated the formation of the visiting group.

In April 1950, the North China visiting group announced its formation, consisting of Wu Yaozong, Liu Liangmo (劉良模), Wu Gaozi, Tu Yuqing, Cui Xianxiang, Wang Zhiizhong (王梓仲), and Deng Yuzhi (鄧裕志). The delegation visited Jinan from April 15 to 18 before moving to Beijing. On April 30, Wu, Liu, Deng, Tu, Cui, Wang, along with Zhao Zichan (趙紫宸) from Yanjing Theological Seminary and Zhao Fusan (趙復三), Vice General Secretary of the Beijing Youth Association, held discussions on religious issues with Xu Bing (徐冰), Deputy Minister of the Central United Front Work Department, where Wu Yaozong expressed a wish to meet with Zhou Enlai.

On May 2, Zhou Enlai met with Wu Yaozong, Liu Liangmo, Deng Yuzhi, Cui Xianxiang, Wang Zhiizhong, Tu Yuqing, and Jiang Changchuan, among others, at the State Council, accompanied by Chen Qiyuan (陳其瑗), Deputy Minister of the Ministry of Internal Affairs of the Central People’s Government. On May 6, Wu Yaozong and others returned to the State Council for a second discussion. The attendees included Ai Niansan (艾年三) from the Shanghai Chinese Evangelical Church, Yang Xiaopeng (楊肖彭), General Secretary of the Tianjin Christian Youth Association, Ling Yuxiuai (凌俞秀靄), Chairperson of the Board of the Beijing Women's Youth Association, Ling Xianyang (凌賢揚) from the Beijing Diocese of the Chinese Anglican Church, and Zhao Fusan, among others. Chen Qiyuan and over ten responsible officials from relevant departments were also present, and the conversation lasted for more than three hours. On the evening of the 13th, Zhou met with Wu Yaozong and 19 others for the third time, adding Zhao Zichan, Lu Zhiwei (陸志韋), President of Yanjing University, Jiang Changchuan, Gao Fengshan (高鳳山), Principal of Beijing Huiwen High School, Pang Huitian (龐輝亭, also known as Pang Zhikun) from the Beijing Congregational Church, Chen Wenrun (陳文潤), General Secretary of the Beijing Women's Youth Association, Liu Weicheng (劉維誠) from the Shanghai Christian Mutual Aid Society, Huo Peixiu (霍培修) from the Tianjin Congregational Church, and Zheng Xisan (鄭錫三), Chair of the North China District and the Tianjin Methodist Church. This discussion lasted four and a half hours and concluded at 3:30 the following morning.

Christian representatives had high hopes for the meetings with Zhou Enlai. Their primary concerns were the implementation of policies on religious freedom and corresponding management measures. They expected the central government to issue orders requiring local people's governments to strictly enforce the provisions of religious freedom outlined in the common program to help resolve current issues facing Christianity. However, Zhou pointed out that the main obstacle faced by Christianity in China was its association with imperialism. The government required religious organizations to reckon with and sever ties with imperialism, establishing autonomous, self-sufficient, and self-propagating churches.

At Zhou Enlai's request, Wu Yaozong discussed with church colleagues modifying the originally prepared "Preliminary Opinions on Handling Christian Issues," which was later renamed "The Path for Chinese Christianity Moving Forward," printed and submitted to Zhou Enlai. It is noteworthy that Wu Gaozi, the formal representative of the North China visiting group, was consistently absent from the list of Christian leaders that Zhou met with three times. Wu later indicated that this was due to Zhou's intention to exclude him. At that time, Wu held the dual roles of General Secretary of the Promotion Association and General Secretary of the National Conference Preparatory Committee, and Zhou's exclusion of Wu Gaozi clearly indicated the CCP's favoritism toward Wu Yaozong while undermining the Promotion Association's position, thus impacting the preparations for the national conference.

On May 15, Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗), Liu Liangmo (劉良模), Tu Yuqing (涂羽卿), and Ai Niansan (艾年三) left Beijing to continue their visit to Xi’an. During the train ride, Wu Yaozong revised the "Path for Christianity Moving Forward" and sent this draft to Beijing. On the 20th, relevant departments of the Central Committee and State Council convened a discussion on the fifth revision of the declaration. While in Xi’an, Wu received Zhou Enlai’s response to the fifth draft and began inviting dozens of colleagues in the country to co-sign. In order to garner support from more church leaders to sign as initiators, Wu made some concessions on certain opinions. For instance, he accepted the suggestions from the Shanghai Christian leaders, changing "Christian organizations should adhere to the principle of not using foreign personnel" to "Chinese Christian churches and organizations that still rely on foreign talents and economic assistance should immediately formulate specific plans to achieve the goal of self-sufficiency in the shortest time possible." Later, he omitted the word "immediately" to avoid misunderstandings about the need to sever foreign economic ties right away. Additionally, he removed the references in the preface regarding discussions with Premier Zhou, replacing them with an introduction emphasizing the significance of the declaration itself. Meanwhile, church leaders in Beijing altered the introduction's wording from "the countries that brought Christianity to China are mainly imperialist countries" to "the people who brought Christianity to China are mainly from these imperialist countries." Wu had indicated to the Shanghai church leaders that all revisions must be approved by Zhou Enlai. However, regarding the fundamental view of Christianity’s relationship with imperialism, Wu Yaozong remained uncompromising. The stance against imperialism elicited a strong backlash from church members. On June 6, the Youth Association held a discussion meeting on the declaration, which faced fierce opposition from participants. On July 26, over 20 executive members of the Promotion Association requested Wu to remove the clause in the declaration that criticized imperialism.

The "Reform Declaration" consists of four parts: preface, general tasks, basic policies, and specific methods. The preface primarily acknowledges that Christianity, during its over a hundred years of history in China, has "intentionally or unintentionally, in tangible or intangible ways, established relationships with imperialism." Even after the establishment of the new China, imperialism still sought to use Christianity for subversive purposes. The overall task for the Chinese church moving forward is to fully support the common program and, under the government’s leadership, oppose imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism, and strive to build a new China. The basic policy involves two aspects: first, the Chinese church must recognize the reality of being exploited by imperialism in the past and eliminate "the influence of imperialism within Christianity"; second, achieving autonomy, self-sufficiency, and self-propagation as the goals for Christian reform in a short time frame. As for the specific methods, concrete plans must be devised to explore how to achieve self-renewal in a short period and improve the church in both religious and general affairs.

On July 28, Wu Yaozong finalized the last (eighth) draft named "The Path for Chinese Christianity to Promote New China’s Construction," along with the co-signatures of 40 Christian initiators, and sent letters to over a thousand Christian leaders nationwide to solicit their personal support through signatures. Initially, Wu planned only to publish the declaration and had no intention of turning it into a widespread signing campaign. In June, Zhou Enlai directed that the declaration be transformed into a signing campaign, intending to mobilize public sentiment to compel church leaders to accept the reform direction desired by the CCP and to establish Wu’s leadership position in the Christian reform movement.

The first round of signatures concluded at the end of August 1950, collecting 1,527 names. On September 23, the People's Daily published the full text of the declaration on the front page along with the complete list of the first batch of signatories. It also featured an editorial titled "The Patriotic Movement of Christians" to promote the declaration. The editorial affirmed the patriotic movement of Christians and highly praised the signing campaign.

By the end of September 1950, Tianfeng published a second list, raising the total number of signatories to 3,268, an increase of 1,741 from the first batch. Following the People's Daily's strong endorsement of the declaration, various gatherings and seminars supporting the Three-Self Movement and the Reform Declaration occurred across the country. By early December, the number of signatories had grown to 26,727, and by the end of December, it surpassed 78,596. By the end of January 1951, the reform declaration had nearly 90,000 signatures. The involvement and support of political forces played a significant role in the development of the signing campaign for the reform declaration. By April 1951, the number of signatures had increased to 200,000. As of September 1952, the total number of signatories reached 338,000.

(3) Christian Publishing and Censorship 1951（Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 229-235）

On March 16, 1951, the Christian Publishing Conference was held in Beijing with the aim of assisting the Christian publishing industry in overcoming the difficulties arising from the cessation of American funding following the outbreak of the Korean War. The conference also discussed how to sever ties with imperialism in the publishing industry and eliminate its influence. The timing of this meeting coincided with the nationwide Anti-American Aid Korea movement, making the eradication of the "toxic influence" of imperialism a key focus of the party-state's propaganda efforts.

To cleanse Christianity's publications of "toxins," a comprehensive review of Christian books and periodicals commenced after the conference. At that time, the China Christian Publishing Association established ten criteria for the review of publications: (1) authors recognized as disreputable within Christianity; (2) direct or indirect, explicit or implicit opposition to the Communist Party and Soviet Union, or slander against the People's Government and its leaders; (3) containing imperialist, capitalist, or feudalistic ideas; (4) emphasizing transcendental "apolitical" ideas harmful to patriotism; (5) propaganda for imperialism or imperialistic elements; (6) distorting or misunderstanding facts that insult Chinese culture and harm the self-esteem and confidence of the Chinese people; (7) containing provocative or divisive ideas that hinder the unity of the Chinese people and government; (8) detrimental to the Anti-American Aid Korea effort; (9) blindly promoting the "Universal Church" manipulated by imperialism without distinguishing friend from foe; (10) opposing or undermining the Three-Self Movement.

Under these standards, a large-scale political review of Christian publications was initiated. In May 1951, the publishing association required all publishing institutions to report their review results by the end of July. At the same time, it called upon over 200 church organizations in Shanghai to conduct similar reviews of their collections and report back to the association. In July, the publishing association received review reports from 17 publishing institutions, including the Guangxue Association, the Youth Association Bookstore, the Baptist Bookstore, the Tianfeng Society, the Shizhao Newspaper, the Chinese Bible Society, the National Sunday School Association, the Chinese Anglican Church Book Committee, the China Christian Evangelistic Association, the Guangxie Bookstore, the Gospel Bookstore, the missionary bookstore, and several Christian student associations from universities (14 of which were located in Shanghai), as well as the Chinese Christian Bible Society (in Hankou) and the Chinese Christian Church of Sichuan’s Literary Department (formerly the Yinghua Bookstore in Chengdu). Among the 649 titles submitted for review by the Guangxue Association, Youth Association Bookstore, and Chinese Baptist Bookstore, 352 were deemed problematic, accounting for 54.2% of the total. These titles containing "toxins" were either removed from sale or revised.

In June 1951, Wu Yaozong, Chairman of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee, stated at the Christian community's mass accusation meeting held in Shanghai that American imperialism utilized Christianity as an "important tool for cultural invasion." He claimed that in addition to the Guangxue Association and other publishing agencies disseminating a large number of reactionary publications that spread toxins, the Bible itself had become a tool to sedate the people and instill a mindset of servitude. At the same meeting, Hu Zuyin, the volunteer General Secretary of the Guangxue Association, also accused that "the motivation behind imperialism establishing the Guangxue Association was not to promote the gospel of Jesus but to numb the Chinese people's thoughts and eliminate their resistance to imperialism." He critiqued from the perspective of "cultural invasion," admitting that among the nearly two thousand titles published by the Guangxue Association, at least sixty percent were translations from American publications, serving as a pathway for spreading pro-American, idolizing America, and fear of America sentiments.

In August 1951, the China Christian Publishing Association, Guangxue Association, and Chinese Bible Society jointly held a large meeting in Shanghai to expose the crimes of American imperialism in utilizing the Christian publishing industry to invade China. During the meeting, Zhang Bohuai, General Secretary of the Publishing Association, pointed out that "many Christian publishing institutions are almost entirely controlled by elements of imperialism," and their publications disseminated imperialist toxins. "Whether it is the traditionalist or the reformer, the spiritualist or the social gospel advocates," they have all been exploited by imperialists. This accusation meeting targeted not only the various toxins in published materials but also directed its criticism towards the Bible, alleging that it had become a tool for imperialistic cultural invasion. Miao Qiusheng (繆秋笙) from the Chinese Bible Society stated that spiritualist missionaries misinterpreted the Bible, spreading the toxic ideas of apolitical, transnational, and transcendent concepts along with the notion of "loving one’s enemies," which facilitated imperialist aggression. During the meeting, Xie Zongao and Liu Meili from the Guangxue Association also conducted comprehensive self-criticisms.

In September 1951, Tianfeng published an editorial titled "Eradicate the Influence of American Imperialism in the Christian Publishing Industry," stating that American imperialism had, for many years, used the guise of "literary evangelism" to publish a large number of publications containing various ideological toxins. The reviews of publications and the accusations against publishing institutions were undoubtedly an indispensable part of the overall Communist Party's work on Christian reform. This review work and the campaign against Christian publications were undoubtedly significant events in the history of Christian publishing in China, with the publishing industry facing severe blows from a trend of politicization.

(4) The First Cadre Training Course of the Christian Three-Self Reform Movement（Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1996: 77-80; 2012: 144-145）

In November 1951, the Preparatory Committee established the "First Cadre Training Course of the Christian Three-Self Reform Movement" in Shanghai, aiming to train a group of cadres, especially active youth, to serve as the core for promoting the Three-Self Reform. Similar training courses were also organized in various locations. The Three-Self Reform training course lasted three months, and 99 participants came from different denominational churches and groups in Shanghai. Besides members of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee, the religious affairs departments of East China and Shanghai sent staff, including Chief Yao Piaoting and Director Luo Zhufeng, to assist and guide the teaching.

The preparatory committee for the training course was chaired by Tu Yuqing (涂羽卿), with Deng Yuzhi (鄧裕志) as vice chair and Huang Peiyong (黃培永) as secretary, alongside other members including Liu Liangmo (劉良模), Li Chuwen (李儲文), Situ Tong (司徒桐), Shi Ruzhang (施如璋), and Shen Derong (沈德溶). The list of responsible personnel demonstrates that the cadre training course was entirely dominated by the Youth Association faction, reaffirming their vital role.

The training course consisted of five main procedures: (1) clarifying the purpose of the training and correcting the learning attitude; (2) understanding the greatness of the motherland: comparing old and new China, recognizing the great achievements and future of the new China, and understanding how the new China came about; (3) understanding the essence of imperialism and how it uses Christianity to conduct aggression: recognizing the essence of imperialism, understanding American imperialism's aggression against China (including cultural invasion), and understanding how imperialism uses Christianity for invasion; (4) understanding the significance of the Three-Self Reform Movement and the government's religious policies; (5) learning to summarize: writing personal learning reflections, group discussions, and group summaries. The course procedures indicate that participants were required to learn to support the new China through anti-imperialist patriotism; as believers or church workers, they needed to support the Three-Self Reform Movement as its foundation.

One of the participants, Shen Yifan (沈以藩), son of Bishop Shen Zigao (沈子高) of the Chinese Anglican Church, critiqued his past thinking in his personal learning summary: "I had long believed that my advantage was in the realm of imperialism. In church schools, from elementary school to university to seminary, it seemed I had a smooth path; thus, I hoped to study abroad in the UK and the US for credentials and later secure a high position in the church upon returning home. The liberation of China shattered all my personal plans; therefore, I did not love the new China, and at the onset of liberation, I held strong thoughts of 'changing the world.' When the Korean War broke out, I felt happy, believing the 'time for change' had come. Through years of education in reality, particularly in this training course, I understood the essence of my motherland, recognized its great achievements and bright future; I contrasted my current happy life with the past, and emotionally affirmed the inseparable fate of my destiny and the new China’s destiny. I must stand from the perspective of the people for my own benefit."

On December 11, the training course held a graduation ceremony, attended by over 400 people. In his summary report, Li Chuwen indicated that after the training, participants recognized the necessity of firmly standing on a "people's standpoint" and clearing the "toxic influence" of a "Christian standpoint." All participants also acknowledged the importance of self-criticism and ideological learning while further solidifying their understanding of the significance of the Three-Self Movement.

(5) Institutional Adjustments and Nationalization of Church Schools 1952（Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 189-210）

In the early years after the establishment of the People's Republic of China (PRC), the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) adopted a policy of "protection and maintenance, strengthening leadership, and gradual transformation" towards the old education system, particularly private education. In August 1950, the Central Committee of the CCP issued instructions regarding Catholic and Christian issues, reaffirming that church schools were "private enterprises" and that the government would treat them equally under the principle of "balancing public and private interests." The government only stipulated: (1) church schools must include political courses as compulsory; (2) religious elective courses could be offered in church-run colleges; (3) no exhibitions or mass meetings promoting religious or anti-religious sentiments should be held at schools; (4) students of faith and non-faith should not criticize each other based on beliefs but should unite against imperialist elements.

By the end of 1950, with the deepening of the Anti-American Aid Korea movement, a wave of accusations against imperialism for using church schools to conduct cultural invasion swept across the country, igniting intense mass movements in church schools. The central government's policies towards church schools also entered a new phase, with movements against American imperialism’s cultural invasion initiated at church schools across various regions. By the end of December, both the U.S. and China had frozen each other's assets and public and private deposits, prompting the CCP to decide to utilize the new situation of the Anti-American Aid Korea movement to denounce American imperialism’s cultural invasion in China. From January 1951 onward, church schools fully engaged in the wave of patriotic Anti-American campaigns.

From January 16 to 22, 1951, the Ministry of Education convened a meeting called "Handling Foreign-Subsidized Colleges." Attendees included leaders from the education departments or cultural and educational committees of East China, Central South, and Southwest, as well as the chairpersons, principals, and representatives of 85 teachers and students from 19 colleges receiving foreign subsidies. Minister of Education Ma Xulun stated that church schools were important activities for American imperialist cultural invasion. In response to American freezes on Chinese assets in the U.S., the government decided to reclaim national educational sovereignty, converting colleges previously receiving American funding into schools run by the Chinese people themselves. He criticized church schools as vital pathways for American "cultural invasion," claiming they spread poisonous thoughts of pro-Americanism, admiration for America, and fear of America, while attempting to destroy Chinese national culture and undermine the souls of Chinese youth. Deputy Director of the State Council’s Cultural Education Committee, Lu Dingyi, further characterized the nature of addressing these issues as a serious "political struggle against American imperialism."

Following the meeting regarding foreign-subsidized colleges, the military and political committees of East China, Central South, and Southwest convened meetings in February 1951 to discuss issues related to secondary schools. By the end of 1951, the Ministry of Education completed a summary report on handling schools that received American funding. The report's overall evaluation of church schools stated, "They are the cultural invasion bases set up by imperialists disguised in religious clothing within China."

In the fall of 1952, the Ministry of Education commenced large-scale structural adjustments to higher education institutions, with all church universities being canceled and merged with other colleges. From that point on, church universities became part of history in China. Meanwhile, the Ministry of Education issued directives to ensure that all private primary and secondary schools would be transitioned into public schools by 1954. The "nationalization" of private schools marked the formal end of the transition phase of the old Chinese educational system. However, the nationwide undertaking of private school administration was a massive project involving substantial resources. As a result, the original plan to complete the transition by 1954 had to be extended until 1956 to be essentially fulfilled.

(6) The Christian Historiography Accusation Movement（Xu Yihua (徐以驊) 2004: 14-18; Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 1996: 80-81; 2005: 212-219）

As early as the end of 1951, the renowned Chinese church historian Wang Zhixin (王治心) made a self-criticism regarding his 1940 publication "An Outline of the History of Christianity in China," stating that the book had "a very vague understanding of British and American imperialism... and contained numerous ideological toxins of imperialism, even falling into reactionary rhetoric." Therefore, he "requested all colleagues who possessed this book to rigorously destroy it to prevent the spread of this ideological toxin."

From September to November 1953, Wang Zhixin published research on topics such as Christianity and unequal treaties, the church question, and the Taiping Rebellion in the journal "Xie Jin," focusing on "Research on Christian (Church) Historical Materials." In the article, he criticized imperialism for using Christianity to invade China, arguing that the activities of missionaries in China were "directly proportional" to the imperialistic aggression of major powers. Thus, "the history of Christian missionary work in China over the past 140 years amounts to a history of imperialism's invasion of China."

Although Wang Zhixin (王治心) attempted to apply the framework of imperialistic invasion history to interpret the history of Christianity in China, this approach once again drew criticism. In February and March 1954, the journal "Xie Jin" (協進) published several articles criticizing the "position" in Wang’s historical writing as having serious issues. Critics argued that while Wang acknowledged the fact that Western missionaries utilized Christianity for invasion, he did not elaborate sufficiently. Moreover, Wang regarded the courageous anti-imperialist actions of the Chinese people—known as "church incidents"—as mere "persecutions," leading to the perception that these incidents were triggered by "the ignorance of the common people and the xenophobia of local officials." This reflected Wang’s insufficient understanding of the ironclad fact regarding imperialism’s use of religion as a tool for invasion, and he failed to clearly correlate Christianity with the unequal treaties. In a church atmosphere where anti-imperialism was being actively promoted, the publication of these articles was bound to have negative effects and create considerable confusion among believers.

Since the discussions involved the issue of imperialism's use of Christianity for invasion, the matter was not merely an academic debate but a political position statement from the outset. Consequently, Wang Zhixin responded promptly, admitting his inadequate understanding and improper wording. He reiterated, "If it is said that my two articles intentionally employed disingenuous writing to openly criticize while secretly praising, or to sneak in a foot, I can firmly deny such intentions and malicious purposes. After three years of study, with assistance from my colleagues, I can acknowledge that my understanding of the anti-imperialist spirit and the Three-Self Reform has continuously improved." Besides Wang Zhixin, Lin Yongyu (林永俁), an editor of "Xie Jin," also reflected on the issue. He acknowledged that when dealing with the history of imperialism's use of Christianity in China, failing to "truly stand firm on the people's position and genuinely grasp scientific historical viewpoints and methods" would prevent one from assembling a true "historic truth" from abundant historical materials.

From 1953 to 1955, "Xie Jin" published numerous critical articles regarding the history of Christianity in China, primarily addressing the evaluation of the actions of missionaries such as the Morrison family, Bo Jiale (伯駕), Li Lezhi (李樂知), Li Timotheus (李提摩太), and Pi Zhiwen (裨治文). Additionally, Tang En (湯恩) compiled a series of historical materials under "American Imperialism's Long-term Control" (including information on Chinese Christian universities, seminaries, and Bible projects).

In 1957, Wang Zhixin, under the pen name "Er Wang" (二忘), discussed the early independence movements of Chinese Christianity, distinguishing between "old Three-Self" and "new Three-Self" to differentiate the movements for church autonomy and authenticity before and after 1949, leading to a debate on how to evaluate the independence movements. Critics argued that he affirmed the "old Three-Self" while forgetting the fundamental people's position, suggesting that "false Three-Self" and "true Three-Self" should be employed for evaluations, as the independence movement and authenticity movement before 1949 were merely tools for imperialism to conduct its "more insidious and malicious" invasions of China. In March 1958, the editorial department of "Tianfeng" (天風) officially concluded this debate. They ultimately selected a response written by Wang Yi (王怡) to close the matter, publicly releasing a document entitled "Reference Material on the Issue of Autonomy," which had been printed by the secretariat during the National Christian Conference in 1954. While the editors stated that "there is no intention to conclude this discussion, as historical research issues can continue to be explored and debated," Wang Yi and the content of the document fully reflected the official stance and tone on the relevant issues.

Wang Yi first distinguished between the imperialistic missionaries and the Chinese believers who led and participated in the independence movement. He criticized the imperialist crimes of missionaries who invaded China and manipulated the Chinese church. Conversely, he was willing to acknowledge that the independence movement reflected the aspirations of early 20th-century Chinese Christians for autonomy, even suggesting that the issue of "changing the nameplate" should not be uniformly condemned. He seemingly avoided the question of whether the "old Three-Self" was necessarily synonymous with the "false Three-Self," only acknowledging that within the independent churches, there were indeed "false independence" cases that had been exploited by missionaries. Another distinction Wang wanted to make was regarding those in independent churches who had already been labeled as counter-revolutionaries. Thus, figures like Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲) and Jing Dianying (敬奠瀛) were deemed as false representatives of independence, yet within their churches, there remained a significant number of misled believers who should not be hastily dismissed. Under the dual pressures of imperialism and feudalism, lacking the leadership and support from the Chinese Communist Party, it was clear that the achievements of the "old Three-Self" could not be compared to those of the "new Three-Self."

The evaluations of historical materials and certain issues regarding the history of Christianity in China during the 1950s can be seen as a result of the official attempts to establish a politically correct orthodoxy in historiography after 1949. Xu Yihua (徐以驊) described this as a "historical accusation movement." The issues involved, such as how to evaluate missionaries, the missionary movement, the independence movement, and the authenticity movement, remain contentious topics within the discourse on the history of Christianity in mainland China.

(7) The Three Major Witnesses and Ten Major Tasks 1956（Luo Weihong (羅偉虹) 2014: 712-716）

In January 1956, Mao Zedong (毛澤東) convened a high-level state council meeting, instructing to "continue to consolidate and expand the people's democratic united front, uniting all forces that can be united." Following Mao’s proposal, the Central Committee of the CCP held a special meeting regarding issues related to intellectuals, during which Zhou Enlai (周恩來) expressed the Party's attitude towards engaging with intellectuals. Subsequently, Mao delivered two important speeches in April and May ("On the Ten Major Relationships" and "Let a Hundred Flowers Bloom, Let a Hundred Schools of Thought Contend"), reiterating the CCP's concern for economic development and its determination to grant greater freedom to intellectuals.

Against this relatively relaxed political backdrop, the Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee of Chinese Christianity convened its second plenary (expanded) meeting in March 1956, attended by a total of 249 representatives and participants, exceeding the number at the National Christian Conference in 1954. The meeting added 13 new members and 4 additional standing committee members, including spiritualists newly participating in the Three-Self Movement. Several "spiritualist" figures, such as Jia Yumiao (賈玉銘), Yang Shaotang (楊紹唐), Sun Hanshu (孫漢書), Zuo Furui (左弗如), and Yan Jiala (閻迦勒), spoke during the meeting.

Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗) delivered a report titled "On the Three-Self Patriotic Movement of Chinese Christianity," proposing the well-known "Three Major Witnesses and Ten Major Tasks." The so-called "Three Major Witnesses" represent the witness of achieving the Three-Self of the Chinese Church, the witness of participating in socialism, and the witness of striving to defend world peace. In explaining "the witness of Three-Self," he stated, "Christianity in China was introduced from abroad. Evangelism is inherently a beautiful thing; many pious believers from abroad have dedicated many prayers and donations to the work of spreading the gospel. Unfortunately, imperialism has utilized missionary work to carry out deceptive activities, closely linking the mission society with colonialism..." After years of effort, the Chinese church can free itself from the ties of the mission society and colonialism, and Chinese Christians can unite to establish and strengthen their own church, embarking on the path of self-governance, self-sufficiency, and self-propagation. He pointed out that the fundamental significance of the Three-Self Patriotic Movement is for Chinese believers to manage their own churches, which "does not mean that the Chinese church is severed from the spiritual fellowship among Christians of various countries," and opposing colonialism and the aggression of colonialist countries is not targeted at "Christians and peoples of other countries." Therefore, believers from different national churches are fellow members of the body, with Christ as our common head. Achieving complete autonomy, self-sufficiency, and self-propagation will enable Chinese believers to bear witness to the gospel of Christ more effectively. He reaffirmed, "We highly value the rich heritage of Christianity worldwide over the past two thousand years, as this invaluable heritage bears testament to the work of the Holy Spirit among believers in various times and places." The fully realized Three-Self church in China would also contribute to the spiritual fellowship of Christians worldwide.

What Wu articulated regarding "Three-Self" was no longer centered on struggle and accusation; it restored more religious significance. The relationship between Christianity and imperialism was no longer intrinsically connected, as was the case during the accusation movement, but instead reverted to a layer of "being utilized." More importantly, as he explained the mission of "Three-Self" at the time, he lacked the previous tendency to be exclusionary. He acknowledged the historical efforts of foreign Christians motivated by religious sentiments to spread the gospel while affirming the spiritual exchanges between the Chinese church and universal Christians, as well as the Chinese church's inheritance and development of the two-thousand-year tradition and heritage of the church, assigning a fresh and profound meaning to "Three-Self."

The report on the "Ten Major Tasks" also showcased ideas and directions for church construction. Among these tasks, three were political and social in nature (actively participating in socialist construction, striving for world peace, and promoting patriotic education), while the other seven were centered around the church: consolidating and expanding unity, mutual respect, improving church structures, establishing self-sufficiency promotion committees, advancing self-propagation studies, enhancing publishing to meet the spiritual needs of believers, strengthening theological research, and cultivating talent. Wu Yaozong's (吳耀宗) assertions regarding "building the church" indeed ushered in a new and more relaxed atmosphere for the Chinese church, which had been subjected to years of political movements and had raised the banner of "struggle."

(8) The Overseas Connections of Chinese Christianity 1956-57（Matsutani 2016: 175-193; Zhao Tian’en (趙天恩) and Zhuang Wan-fang (莊婉芳) 1997: 94-95）

After the establishment of the People's Republic of China (PRC), Western missionaries withdrew from China, declaring the end of the missionary era. Simultaneously, under the political climate of anti-imperialism and patriotism, the Three-Self Movement not only aimed to sever the relationship between the Chinese church and imperialism but also actively implemented an accusation movement, denouncing Western missionary societies and missionaries as instruments of imperialistic invasion in China. Against this backdrop, the overseas connections of Chinese Christianity had fundamentally stagnated. In 1952 and 1955, British Bishop Hewlett Johnson and a Quaker delegation from the UK visited China at the invitation of the Chinese Committee for World Peace. External visits were primarily limited to Eastern European communist countries; for example, in 1952 and 1954, Liu Liangmo (劉良模) and Bishop Zheng Jianye (鄭建業) attended assembly conferences at the invitation of the Christian Democratic Alliance in East Germany.

Starting in 1956, the Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee made significant breakthroughs in its external relations. In March 1956, the national Three-Self organization invited foreign visitors, including Gustav Nystrom (戈斯塔夫·尼斯特羅姆) from the Swiss Evangelical Church, Josef L. Hromadka (赫魯瑪德卡) from Czechoslovakia, Bishop Jano Peter (彼得主教) from the Hungarian Reformed Church, and Bishop Rajah Manikam (孟尼根監督) from the Indian Lutheran Church to visit China. In May 1956, Bishop Hall (何明華) of the Hong Kong Anglican Church visited China. Between May and October, the Three-Self Movement received various visitors, including a Japanese Christian Women's Association official, the head of the Hong Kong Anglican Church’s St. Stephen's Children’s Home, the Secretary-General of the Indian Christian Church Federation, and representatives from the Australian Anglican Church. From February to September 1958, delegations from the University of Hamburg’s theological seminary, Japanese Christian missionaries, and Hungarian Christian representatives visited China.

Regarding external visits, in the summer of 1956, Bishop Ding Guangxun (丁光訓) attended the preparatory meeting of the Lambeth Conference of the worldwide Anglican Communion in the UK and subsequently served as an observer at the World Council of Churches (WCC) Central Committee meeting held in Hungary. During this time, the General Secretary of the WCC suggested upgrading his observer status to that of a representative, which Ding did not agree to. The WCC had been accused of being a tool of imperialism during the accusation movement. Afterward, Ding attended a conference of the World Student Christian Federation in West Germany (with Zhao Fusan (趙復三) and Huang Peiyong (黃培永) also participating). In September, a Chinese Christian delegation attended the eighth representative assembly of the Christian Democratic Alliance in East Germany. In 1957, representatives of the China Christian Church first visited India, followed by a delegation from the national Three-Self group (including Ding Guangxun, Huang Peiyong, Shen Derong (沈德溶), and Chen Jianxun (陳建勛)) visiting Hungary, where Ding Guangxun was awarded an honorary doctorate at the Debrecen Theological University. In November, the Chinese Christian delegation visited Hungary at the invitation of the Hungarian Christian Fellowship.

It is evident that in 1956 and 1957, prior to the Anti-Rightist Movement, the national Three-Self organizations made efforts to revive contacts with the universal church through both receiving and visiting delegations. To align with Wu Yaozong’s "Three Major Witnesses and Ten Major Tasks," particularly regarding "promoting fellowship and friendship across national Christians and peoples," the national Three-Self organization established the China Christian International Liaison Committee in May 1956. The countries and organizations they engaged with were no longer limited to Eastern European countries but also included attempts to connect with overseas denominations (such as the Anglican Communion) and even with past targets of denunciation during the Korean War (the WCC), indicating a breakthrough for Chinese Christianity amidst a relatively relaxed political atmosphere in 1956 and 1957.

However, with the onset of the Anti-Rightist Movement, the Chinese political landscape shifted leftward once again, and the aforementioned space for engagement no longer existed; the China Christian International Liaison Committee could not continue its operations. In 1958, Bishop Ding Guangxun (丁光訓) also canceled his planned attendance at the Lambeth Conference of the worldwide Anglican Communion in London.

(9) The Publication of "Shengguang" 1957（Wang Weifan (汪維藩) 2013: 2）

According to one of the resolutions from the Second National Conference in 1954: "Under the common goals of anti-imperialism, patriotism, and love for the faith, we should promote the unity of all believers in the Christian church and further develop the Three-Self Patriotic Movement, adopting the principle of mutual respect for the differences in faith, systems, and rituals among various churches." At the end of 1956, Bishop Ding Guangxun suggested that the "Nanjing Three-Self" publish a devotional periodical. The publication invited Jia Yumiao (賈玉銘) to name it "Shengguang," initially planned as a bimonthly. The editorial team included Rev. Li Chenzhong (李晨鐘) from the spiritualist faction (who graduated from Hunan Bible School and formerly served as a pastor in the Sucheng Christian Church in Anhui) and Wang Weifan (汪維藩), a graduate of Jinling Union Theological Seminary.

The purpose of "Shengguang" was to "expound the truth of the Christian gospel; cultivate the spirituality of brothers and sisters in various places; and provide materials for Bible studies, spiritual development, preaching, and pastoral work." The inaugural issue was published on March 1, 1957, featuring contributions from Jia Yumiao, Jiang Peifen (蔣佩芬), Yang Shaotang, Sun Hanshu, Han Peter (韓彼得), Cai Wenhao (蔡文浩), and Li Chenzhong, most of whom came from a spiritualist background. The publication had about 5,000 subscribers in 1957.

However, from the early summer of 1958 onwards, the religious sector in Jiangsu Province launched an Anti-Rightist Movement. The bimonthly "Shengguang" ceased publication after its seventh issue (February 1958). Nonetheless, in the "Editor's Note" of that issue, there was no indication of plans to suspend publication, and it continued to encourage subscriptions and contributions.

(10) Joint Worship 1958（Luo Weihong (羅偉虹) 2014: 729-740）

The year 1958 marked a watershed for the development of the church in China. With Mao Zedong initiating the Great Leap Forward, announcing the transition to a communist society, it also signaled the arrival of an era characterized by "recklessness" and extreme leftism. Amidst the national context of radical advancement, the supposed communist paradise was set to be realized in Chinese society.

The existence of Chinese Christianity, particularly its diverse denominational structure, was increasingly seen during the Great Leap Forward as an obstruction to the socialist path, facing severe impacts. In the second half of 1958, "Tianfeng" (天風) began characterizing the organizational structures of Chinese churches and church groups as "essentially retaining a semi-colonial appearance from the era of imperialist rule," arguing that "the remnants from imperialism were still preserved intact." This semi-colonial appearance included: first, the distorted distribution of churches; second, sectarian opposition and church division issues; third, the agent organizations from the era of missionary imperialism; and fourth, the reactionary control of churches. The article further stated: "We must fundamentally change the semi-colonial appearance of Chinese Christianity in the ongoing process of deepening the socialist education movement." In this context, the Three-Self organizations, under the banner of changing the "semi-colonial" appearance, implemented joint worship nationwide.

Zhejiang was one of the provinces that responded more quickly. Between April and May 1958, the Ningbo church proposed four restructuring plans, advocating: first, the unified management of economic resources; second, the unified arrangement of pastoral staff; third, the unified management of church property; and fourth, the establishment of production institutions in which pastors would engage in productive labor. The three major church organizations in Hangzhou (the China Christian Church Zhejiang Conference, the Hong Kong Anglican Church Zhejiang Diocese, and the Zhejiang-Shanghai Baptist Convention) also resolved to work together in August. In Wenzhou, an expanded meeting of the city's Three-Self committee held on May 26-27, 1958, decided to abolish original denominational names, unify personnel regardless of denominational affiliation, and manage economic resources collectively. Regarding faith traditions, practices such as baptism, immersion, anointing, and sabbath observance were to be freely practiced, while communion was to be held monthly using the method of multiple cups. The meeting also resolved to implement joint worship on the first Sunday of July. During the Great Leap Forward, only a handful of churches were open in Wenzhou, such as Chengxi Church (Methodist), Canghexiang Church (China Christian Self-Governing Church), Huayuanxiang Church (China Inland Mission), Yongguang Church (Chinese Jesus Self-Governing Church), and Toushonao Church (Seventh-day Adventist), while all other churches halted activities. After July 1958, only Chengxi Church remained as a venue for Christian activities across the city.

The model of joint worship quickly spread from Wenzhou to other parts of the country. Local congregations of various denominations began to amalgamate based on geographic proximity, leading to a significant reduction in the number of local churches. For example, in Shanghai, there were 208 churches in 1950, but following the implementation of joint worship, only 22 remained open, eventually dropping to 8. In Beijing, the number decreased from 65 churches in 1949 to just 4 by 1958. In Jiangsu Province, there were 321 open churches at the end of 1957, but by early 1959, only 83 remained, with Nanjing reducing from 23 larger churches to just 7. Sichuan Province went from a total of 134 churches before 1958 to 67 after the introduction of joint worship. In Qingdao, 50 meeting points were consolidated into 10, and later reduced to 7. Hangzhou merged 28 churches into 5, and due to pastoral staff attending training, it eventually maintained only 1, which after the training finished, opened 3 churches (later reduced to 2).

The implementation of joint worship effectively dismantled the denominational structure and significantly strengthened the control of the Three-Self organizations over Christianity. Since the founding of the People's Republic of China (PRC), the national and regional structures of various denominations remained intact, while the development of the Three-Self organizations led to leadership positions being assumed by reform-minded individuals. However, the existing denominational framework still maintained a degree of autonomy, making it difficult for the national Three-Self organizations to exert complete control over the economic and personnel rights of national denominational bodies. After the implementation of joint worship, denominational organizations ceased operations, and the various levels of Three-Self organizations became the higher leadership bodies for urban and rural grassroots churches. This state of the Three-Self fully leading the "church" fundamentally transformed the denominational structure of Chinese Christianity, reflecting the party-state's proactive use of the political climate to implement more radical religious policies and also serving as a necessary outcome of a highly centralized and unified leadership system.

During the Great Leap Forward, Wenzhou became a pilot for a "religion-free zone," as policies aimed at eradicating religion were officially put on the agenda. The implementation of "joint worship" across the country drastically reduced the number of grassroots churches, with many pastors being reassigned to other occupations, particularly factory work. All of this was undoubtedly part of the broader policy to "eradicate religion."

(11) Political Movements and Christianity in the 1950s（Ying 2014）

Since the founding of the PRC in 1949, the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) has consolidated its one-party rule through a series of political and mass movements (such as suppressing counter-revolutionaries, the Anti-American Aid Korea movement, ideological transformation, the three anti and five anti campaigns, purging counter-revolutionaries, and the Anti-Rightist Movement). Concurrently, economic transformations (such as land reform and collectivization) enabled the state to gain control over land and other agricultural factors. The state acquired the power to control and allocate the vast majority of resources within society. To achieve even the most basic conditions for survival, members of society had to obtain the necessary resources from the state, leading to a highly unified total society. Under this integrated model, the party-state penetrated grassroots social life with unprecedented depth and breadth, ensuring that social integration at the local level was subordinated to the political integration at the national level, and that both achieved a high degree of consistency. However, the effectiveness of this integration model came at the cost of destroying the independence and autonomy of society. In other words, the "totalism" referred to by Zou Tong (鄒讜) indicates that the power of political institutions can at any time invade and control all levels and domains of society without restriction; although it reached its zenith during the Cultural Revolution, its development began with political movements from the early 1950s following the establishment of the PRC.

In the 1950s, as China moved towards totalitarianism, every political movement, regardless of its original purpose or target being related to Christianity, inevitably impacted the Chinese church, since Christianity was seen as a heterodox force associated with imperialistic aggression. For instance, during the political movements in the early years of the CCP, land reform not only mobilized urban churches to participate in rural land redistribution, expressing support and gaining insights through direct involvement but also led to rural churches being accused of being "landlords," resulting in the occupation of church buildings and the confiscation of church property. Similarly, the Anti-American Aid Korea movement targeted "American imperialism," thus making Christian churches and their affiliated institutions primary targets for denunciation and accusations of "cultural invasion" by America. Although the focus of ideological transformation was on intellectuals transitioning from the old society, Christian individuals also had to engage in criticism and self-criticism to transform their thinking, thereby purging the religious toxins of imperialism. The "Three Anti" campaign aimed at "anti-corruption, anti-waste, and anti-bureaucracy," targeting officials within government agencies, while the "Five Anti" campaign focused on "anti-bribery, anti-tax evasion, anti-theft of state assets, anti-inefficiency, and anti-theft of state economic information," primarily aimed at capitalists. However, significant figures within Christian enterprises were also accused of violating "Five Anti." Furthermore, the Three Anti and Five Anti campaigns merged with ideological transformations, evolving into various "ideological heavyweight" campaigns. These movements were intertwined and carried out through intense mass mobilization tactics (such as denunciation meetings) with the goal of identifying and struggling against class enemies (American imperialism's agents, bad elements, and big figures).

The methods of mass mobilization continued to be applied in political movements after the mid-1950s, including the crackdown on counter-revolutionaries in 1955 and the Anti-Rightist Movement (or Socialist Education) from 1957 to 1958. In these nationwide political movements, Christianity also inevitably faced the task of purging counter-revolutionaries and rightists embedded within the church.

During the Great Leap Forward in 1958, Christianity had to fully cooperate in the comprehensive transformation towards a communist society. Not only did local churches actively align themselves with the Party, but pastors were also categorized as members of the exploiting class, and religious ideologies were temporarily labeled as "toxins." Furthermore, the joint worship initiative further dismantled the denominational structure of churches, with a significant number of churches nationwide being closed under the pretext of merging.

Key Theological Issues

(1) Expelling the Poison of Imperialism (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 127-130, 136-140; Ying (英) 2022; Wickeri (威克里) 1990: 133-146)

In January 1951, Wu Yaozong (吳耀宗) wrote in Tianfeng that the issue is not "who should transmit" but rather "what to transmit" in the context of autobiography. If having a Chinese person transmit the gospel means completing the "autobiography," then the problem seems to have been thoroughly resolved. Most Western missionaries have now retreated, leaving the task of evangelism to Chinese believers. However, Wu firmly believed that the question of autobiography is not that simple. He was concerned with: what should Chinese believers "transmit"? If what they transmit is still the Western theology, the Chinese church remains under the influence of Western imperialism’s poison. Thus he stated: "If we really want to achieve 'autobiography,' Chinese believers must explore the treasure of Jesus' gospel for themselves, break free from the shackles of Western theology, critically assess the escapism in thought, and create their own theological system; only then can we express the true spirit of Jesus' gospel in the new China."

Wu's explanation of autobiography pointed out that, under the new China's environment, a prerequisite for reaffirming the "true spirit" of the Christian gospel is to expel the influence of imperialism's poison on Christianity. The "poison" mainly involves two aspects: (1) reformism; (2) superpolitics.

In terms of reformism, it primarily targets the social gospel movement concerning the reform of Chinese society. The YMCA can be seen as a representative in this regard. On July 27, 1951, the National Conference of the Chinese YMCA and the National Conference of the Young Women's Christian Association held a meeting to accuse American imperialism of using "reformism" to invade China through the YMCA. What is reformism? Wu stated: "Any approach that whitewashes the surface, evades class struggle, and opposes using revolutionary means to overthrow the old social system is reformism." He pointed out that the greatest task of the Chinese revolution is to overthrow the three main enemies: imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism. However, the YMCA not only does not oppose these three enemies but also attempts to "deceive the masses" through education, rural service, and labor welfare work, "obscuring the Chinese people's understanding of the domination of these three enemies in China." Deng Yuzhi (鄧裕志) from the Young Women's Christian Association also pointed out during the accusation that the YMCA advocates a "third way," trying to propose "Christian social democracy" beyond capitalism and communism. The purpose of this claim is to "fragment the revolutionary strength of the colonized people" and cultivate accomplices for imperialism.

Another individual, Liu Liangmo (劉良模), who became a backbone of the nationwide Three-Self Movement and the director of the YMCA ministry, expressed in a discussion on the issue of Christian ideological reform in January 1952 that the expectation of reformism to reshape society through "incremental legal methods, without revolution," is essentially "reactionary." Because it "dissipates revolutionary strength, numbs the people's resistance to reactionary rule, and prolongs the lifespan of reactionary rule."

If the target of criticism against the "poison" of reformism is the "social gospel" faction within Chinese Christianity, then regarding the "spiritual faction," the dispersed poison is "superpolitics." Superpolitics, according to Liu Liangmo, a representative of the Christian Political Consultative Conference in March 1951, is the attitude that "Christians should not meddle in politics." He believed that "this is an excuse used by a minority of bad elements within Christianity to deceive believers and create a rift between believers and our people's government." Superpolitics itself "is a political attitude." Later, Wu Yaozong pointed out: "American imperialism has utilized the 'spiritual faction' within Christianity, cloaked in an extremely mystical religious guise, distorting the Bible, spreading poisonous thoughts of 'superpolitics,' anti-communism, anti-Soviet, and anti-people, attempting to incite discord between millions of devout Christians and our people's government."

As a member of the spiritual faction, Elder Zheng Zhengguang (鄭證光) from the Fuzhou meeting place acknowledged that the spiritual faction presents itself as "transcendent," deceiving socially passive Christians with the lie that Christians are celestial beings who should not "meddle" in earthly affairs, thereby spreading the toxic thought that Christians should be "superpolitical!" As a result, Christians are reluctant to resist the oppression of the reactionary ruling class and the invasion of imperialism. After the founding of the new China, "superpolitics" also diluted the will of the people's revolution and weakened the strength of the people's revolution.

At the end of 1952, the Nanjing United Theological Seminary, formed by the merger of 11 seminaries and Bible schools in East China, was officially established, and in September 1963, it published the Nanjing United Theological Journal, assuming the mission of constructing a new era Christian theology. Vice President Cheng Zhiyi (誠質怡) stated in the "Inaugural Statement": "In the past, Christianity was exploited as a tool for invading China, resulting in absurd and heretical thoughts being disseminated as 'theology,' distorting the pure faith. In the future, we must not only expel the poison but also actively promote the truth of salvation through Jesus Christ, uniting with the vast number of patriots and believers to advance the Three-Self Reform Movement."

(2) Wu Yaozong's Theological Innovation and Reconsideration (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2014; 2016)

As the initiator of the Three-Self Movement in Christianity, Wu Yaozong believed that after breaking free from the fetters of Western theology and critically assessing escapism in thought, Chinese Christians must create their own theological system. He held great expectations for the construction and innovation of Chinese theology, believing it is key for Chinese Christianity to establish itself in the new China. This involves two parallel theological fronts: (1) a critical assessment of what he termed "escapism" in theological thought, thus reforming Christian theology; (2) establishing a harmonious relationship between Christianity and materialism. Both can be traced back to Wu's theological roots before "liberation."

In Wu Yaozong's reform of Christian thought, while advocating anti-imperialist patriotism politically, he also incorporated elements of religious or theological transformation. In 1952, he pointed out that after studying the impact of imperialism on missionary movements, he clearly saw the errors the church had made in interpreting Christianity, which had strayed from Jesus’ teachings. The biggest problem was an excessive emphasis on the transcendence of God while neglecting His immanence. The former highlighted human sinfulness, thus intentionally or unintentionally denying how God works through people. Meanwhile, the focus on sin led theology toward eschatology—final judgment and eternal life. Wu Yaozong advocated for a balanced integration of both elements, termed "dialectical unity," to correct the issues of traditional theology being intertwined with feudalism and capitalism, facing the current social changes with a fresh attitude, and discovering God's will in the revolutionary movement.

In relation to this new theological system, Wu Yaozong proposed five points: (1) to reconstruct a view of God that is both transcendent and immanent, effectively applying it to the current world situation; (2) to acknowledge the universality of God's revelation, recognizing that even those who do not know God can be pathways to realizing His will. It is necessary to break the camp distinctions between "Christians" and "non-Christians," or "theistic" and "atheistic," as Christians and theists do not monopolize the understanding of truth; (3) to view nature and society as arenas of God's work, and to regard natural and social sciences and the laws governing nature and society as methods of God's work; (4) to have a new faith in humanity, that God works through people. Both optimism and critical awareness towards human nature are essential; (5) to not perceive God's kingdom as a distant future reality, for with a clear understanding of reality, the kingdom of God begins in the present.

In Wu Yaozong's thought, another concurrent line of advancement in Christian theological innovation is the issue of harmony between Christianity and communism. In fact, he began exploring the relationship between the two as early as the 1930s, and his book No One Has Seen God, published in the 1940s, meticulously explained theoretically that the two are not contradictory and can complement each other in various ways. The discussion of harmony also involves two different approaches for Christians and materialists.

For Christians, he hoped to clarify the relationship between God and truth, thereby resolving the contradictions and oppositions between Christianity and communism, as well as materialism. At the same time, while affirming the ideal of a classless and exploitation-free society pursued by the communist revolution, this is actually aligned with Christian beliefs. Therefore, what Christians must urgently do is not to deny the goodness and truth of those "outside of the church," because "all goodness comes from God." He cited the liberation of China as an example, referring to it as "a story that astonishes heaven and moves gods," and the construction and victory in the war to resist America and aid Korea after liberation as "a miracle in world history." He criticized Christians who denied the good deeds of non-Christians after liberation, fundamentally violating the teachings of Jesus Christ. Using Matthew 25 as an example, he pointed out that on the day of judgment, the distinction between "the righteous" and "the unrighteous" hinges primarily on whether they have followed God's will.

In addressing materialists, Wu primarily aimed to explain three aspects: (1) Christianity does not contradict materialism: Although Christianity is not materialism, it can accept the truths of materialism, and the idealistic elements that must exist within Christianity do not necessarily conflict with materialism. (2) Christianity and materialism can complement each other: Theoretically, Wu always believed in the absolute and unique nature of God's truth, which can supplement or correct the weaknesses and biases of materialism. However, due to the corruption of real Christianity, it cannot fully unleash the critical elements of faith. Regardless, the Communist Party, in just thirty years, has managed to "lead the Chinese people to accomplish the revolutionary mission of this ancient nation," proving in practice to be superior to Christianity. How to integrate theory with practice and rethink the relationship between the two is what he was concerned about; (3) Will Christianity, as materialism claims, inevitably perish like other religions? On one hand, he believed that religions moving toward extinction would only be superstitious, inferior religions, akin to the "opiate of the people" described by Marx, being escape mechanisms from reality; on the other hand, Wu Yaozong was concerned not with whether the form of religion exists, but with the essence of religion. He firmly believed that if the essence of religion is the pursuit, admiration, and obedience to "truth," this "high-level religion" expressed through religious sentiments "does not change with shifts in religious form."

It can be said that only the reformed Christian theology can harmonize with materialism. However, the efforts for innovative theology became a theological consideration for the spiritual faction, which did not participate in or even opposed the Three-Self Movement (considering reform as equivalent to abandoning the faith of the spiritual faction). The Communist Party, in an effort to unify the spiritual faction, needed to restrain Wu's thoughts in this area to prevent hindering the development of the Three-Self Movement. At the same time, the assertion of harmonizing Christianity with materialism infringes on an ideological taboo of the Communist Party; materialists and idealists can collaborate politically, rather than forcedly constructing a harmony and complementarity between the two on a theoretical level. Thus, Wu's two lines of thought regarding the construction of a new theology were not tolerated in the realities of political life, constrained by the party state, and could not be developed.

(3) The “Débâcle” of Christianity in China? 1951 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 285-286, 303-306; Hood (胡德) 1991: 185-222)

In 1951, the renowned missionary journal International Review of Missions published an article written by "A China Missionary," which sparked significant controversy and response within the Western missionary camp. The article was titled First Thoughts on the Débâcle of Christian Missions in China, summarizing the author's reflections on the failure of the Western missionary movement in China after their withdrawal from the country. At that time, many missionaries were leaving China, marking the formal "fall" of the world's largest missionary field and putting an end to over a hundred years of Western missionary efforts in China. "A China Missionary" believed that the missions and missionaries bear some responsibility for the termination of the missionary movement in China, and this "débâcle" is not without God’s "judgment."

The true identity of "A China Missionary" is David M. Paton (裴大衛), a member of the British Anglican Church, who had previously served as a missionary in Chongqing and Fujian, leaving Fuzhou to return to his country in January 1951. Shortly after returning, he participated in a Far East consultation convened by the International Missionary Council, where he presented seven lessons learned. His article, First Thoughts on the Débâcle of Christian Missions in China, is based on these points. In 1953, he published the book Christian Missions and the Judgment of God in London, further elaborating on his views.

He indicated that the end of the missionary era could be understood as God's will, the work of Satan, political factors, tragedy, or a combination of these explanations. However, he preferred the term "débâcle," as the cessation of missionary work represents God's judgment on them, aimed at highlighting their internal weaknesses. Paton further explained the theological foundation behind his viewpoint, arguing that biblical teachings have depicted "evil in history" as a manifestation of God's punishment (wrath of God), and the tumultuous historical process reveals God's purposes. God's actions are evident not only in the religious world but also in communist China. Moreover, the church is not only the recipient of God's grace but also the target of judgment. For this reason, he was convinced that God speaks through political and social revolutions. Although the Communist Party does not recognize or even denies the existence of God, it has acted as an irresistible force (force majeure) that brought an end to the era of missionary work in China, which should be understood as the execution of God's will.

Paton was well aware of the controversy surrounding his views and thus emphasized that the Communist Party is the instrument of God's judgment, but this does not mean the Communist Party is on God's side. Just as God used the Assyrians (亞述人) to judge Israel, the Assyrians eventually vanished from history, while God's chosen people continued to exist. Furthermore, he also pointed out that God's judgment and mercy are actually inseparable, as Christians are both righteous and sinful; thus, the purpose of God's judgment is to make missionaries aware of their failures and pride, prompting them to seek repentance.

Paton's views sparked extensive discussion among missionaries. Many missionaries acknowledged that there were failures in the missionary movement in China, but opposed equating these failures with God's judgment. For example, Charles C. West (魏士德) from the Presbyterian Church in the USA objected to the framing of "judgment," stating that although there were aspects of missionary policy that warranted critique, the disappearance of institutions established in China, the capitulation of Chinese church leaders under pressure, and the collapse of ecumenical fellowship and Christian social witness were fundamentally a "tragedy." Many "good things" were attacked under the banner of anti-imperialism; how could this possibly be understood as God's judgment? S. H. Dixon (丁克生) from the British Methodist Church believed that the end of the missionary movement was entirely the result of political power factors, and thus should not be seen as a direct judgment on the missionary community and its work. While the experiences of missionary work in China indeed had shortcomings, even if they led simpler lives, fully committed themselves, adapted more to the lives of the Chinese people, or had more comprehensive policies on social issues, they ultimately could not escape the accusations of communism. The Communist Party simply did not allow the existence of independent hostile groups outside of the state.

Victor E. W. Hayward (海維德), a Baptist from the UK who served as the deputy general secretary of the National Christian Council of China, also strongly opposed the notion that God used the Communist Party to judge the failures of missionaries. He argued that everything happening in China should be understood from a social and political perspective, rather than questioning why God brought missionaries away from China or why He used the Communist Party as "unwitting servants" to accomplish this important task. He interpreted God's judgment as the opportunity for people to discover their failures, seek atonement, renew their faith and commitment, realign their direction, and learn how to better serve the Lord during a crisis. Hayward acknowledged that there were many aspects of the missionary movement that warrant examination; in fact, he did not seem to have significant disagreements with Paton based on his experiences with missionary work in China. The question remains whether the missionary movement was a total failure and whether the Communist Party was merely an instrument of God's judgment on the missionaries.

From the discussion above regarding whether God used the Communist Party to judge the missionary movement, it is evident that, despite differing positions among various missionaries, they all conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the missionary work in China. Even those who disagreed with the idea of a complete failure and that God utilized the Communist Party acknowledged that there were shortcomings in the missionary movement in China, and individual missionaries’ attitudes and actions were not perfect.

(4) Reflections and Lessons from the Missionary Movement in China (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2012b: 287-303, 309-311; Ling (凌) 1999: 181-218; Bush (布什) 1970: 61-68)

The comprehensive withdrawal of missionaries from 1951 to 1952 officially marked the end of the modern Protestant missionary movement in China. Throughout the 140-plus years of history, the Boxer Rebellion in 1900, the Northern Expedition from 1925 to 1927, and the Sino-Japanese War from 1936 to 1945 represent three significant challenges faced by the missionary movement. However, even during the period when many missionaries withdrew from the interior to Shanghai, or even left China entirely, they quickly returned after the situation stabilized. In the late stages of the War of Resistance, the mission societies also actively planned for post-war reconstruction. Nevertheless, when it came to the withdrawal in the early 1950s, missionaries had no hope of returning to China. As Bi Fanyu (畢範宇) stated: "Mission societies will never be able to return to China in their old forms. Regardless of the duration of the communist regime, the status of foreign missionaries has permanently changed. They cannot return to 'control' schools, hospitals, or churches. Chinese people who oppose Christianity would not welcome the re-entry of Western denominations or mission societies using personnel, finances, and properties to control the church." This represented not only the "end of the missionary era" but also the conclusion of an entire epoch. Many missionaries and missionary organizations that withdrew from China conducted a comprehensive review of the missionary movement, resulting in a large-scale "Re-thinking Mission."

This reflection involved two key points: (1) How do we explain the success of the Communist movement in China? Does Christianity bear responsibility for being unable to prevent its success? What went wrong with Chinese Christian theology? Some missionaries attributed the success of communism to Christianity's neglect of the needs of Chinese society, while others countered that social gospel diluted the true essence of the gospel. This mutual criticism essentially continued the debate between 20th century liberalism and fundamentalism. Missionaries concurrently used this opportunity to evaluate the effectiveness of theology. However, whether the strategy of indigenizing theology was flawed is one matter, while whether Christian theology could successfully prevent the communist revolution is another. In reality, the key to the Communist Party's success, whether filling a cultural vacuum or leading a nationalist revolution, presupposed that Christianity was a competitor to communism in the eyes of missionaries. The responsibility of Christianity was to prevent the spread of communism. Yet some missionaries questioned whether Christianity could genuinely stop the tide of the Chinese revolution. Holding the belief that Christianity has the ability to block communism is evidently unrealistic.

(2) How did missionaries address the Communist Party's accusations regarding imperialism and cultural aggression? Do their cultural, social, and political shortcomings mean they are tools of imperialism? Some missionaries firmly believe that the gospel of Christ is inherently good. The accidental relationship between the missionary movement and imperialism is indeed unfortunate, and many criticisms directed at Christianity, such as missionaries being employed by imperialist governments to aid in the invasion of China or that the purpose of missionary work was to enslave the Chinese people, are unfounded. Others admit that the entire history clearly reveals that unequal treaties and protective clauses for missionaries made it impossible for Christianity to escape accusations of being imperialist. In the context of these unequal treaties, missionaries could not evade the charge of being imperialistic agents. They should seriously reflect on their historical dependence on governments in China and other missionary fields. However, they also acknowledge that the vast majority of missionaries were influenced by a sense of Western superiority, equating the gospel with Anglo-American lifestyles. Even their appreciation of Chinese culture often comes across as superficial, arrogant, and strategic rather than objective. The more "Westernized" Chinese church leaders became, the more secure their positions, which resulted in a complete disconnection from their traditional cultural roots. The Western sense of superiority among missionaries is also reflected in their living standards in China. Missionaries unanimously acknowledge that their treatment and benefits in China far exceeded those of their Chinese counterparts. Some missionaries admitted that they could not fully commit to living closely with Chinese people and aligning with local workers. In terms of church ministry, many missionaries felt remorse for failing to help Chinese churches achieve self-governance and self-support. They also repented for their paternalistic tradition. However, even when some missionaries reflected on the term "imperialism," their understanding of this concept did not fully align with the Communist definition. Those missionaries who were more critical of the missionary movement in China targeted either the relationships between individual missionaries and the government or the overall missionary movement's position within the colonial expansion era and the protective issues arising from the unequal treaties. They remained cautious regarding the Communist Party's equivalency accusations between the missionary movement and colonialism and imperialism.

Moreover, in June 1951, the Division of Foreign Missions of the National Council of the Churches of Christ in the USA held a three-day conference in New York, where they distributed questionnaires to returning missionaries. The questions posed to these returning missionaries were: (1) What lessons have you learned from your missionary experience in China that could help avoid repeating mistakes in other missionary fields? (2) What lessons can you bring to other missionary fields? (3) If you could return to China, what changes would you make in your life and work? A total of 152 missionaries completed the questionnaire, which was then compiled into a report titled "Lessons to be Learned from the Experiences of Christian Missions in China."

Many missionaries, after leaving China, went on to serve in countries around the world. Some pointed out that thousands of missionaries would carry special experiences with them, and this was not merely a geographical transition; the events that had occurred in China would likely be replicated in other areas. They needed to remember the lessons learned in China: missionaries must always keep in mind that the testimony of universal mission must lead to the establishment of indigenous churches, and that self-sufficiency of the church is also an indispensable goal of missionary work. At the same time, missionaries should possess adaptability to face various changes, avoiding equating their work with their personal identities, and thereby establishing their sense of security.

(5) The Theological Mass Movement 1953-1956 (Ding Guangxun (丁光訓) 1986: 7-13; Shen Yifan (沈以藩) 2000: 33-35; Wang Weifan (汪維藩), Ji Fengwen (季鳳文) 2011: 487-489; Luo Weihong (羅偉虹) 2014: 705-712; Wickeri (威克里) 1990: 243-280)

According to statements from Chinese Christian figures in the 1980s (Ding Guangxun (丁光訓), Shen Yifan (沈以藩), Wang Weifan (汪維藩)), a "Theological Mass Movement" took place in the Chinese church between 1953 and 1956. Shen Yifan pointed out that this "theological rethinking" mainly revolved around four questions: (1) Is the world governed by Satan, or is it ruled and managed by God? (2) Is human nature entirely depraved, or does it still reflect the "image of God" in some aspects? (3) What is the relationship between belief and unbelief? (4) What is the relationship between faith and morality? In fact, the term "theological mass movement" does not appear in church literature from the 1950s and is likely a categorization and interpretation from the 1980s. However, the issues discussed were indeed raised by the Three-Self organizations at that time. Rather than an active construction of theology, it could be seen as a passive response or counter to the anti-Three-Self spiritual faction theology, primarily targeting Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲) and Wang Mingdao (王明道).

In August 1953, Tianfeng published an article signed by "A Group of Readers," criticizing the Shanghai Gospel Book Room for its publication of Holiness Without Blemish in April 1953 (originally a manuscript by Ni in 1939). The article claimed that its content slandered the new society while feigning spiritual piety to oppose the new China and the Three-Self Reform. This group of readers rebutted the book's emphasis on the "source issue," stating that implying "the source of the new China is bad" slanders the nation and its people. They further criticized the book's claim that "the church has never sinned," asserting this denies imperialism's use of Christianity. They noted that the mention of "Satan's work is to accuse" was directed at the denouncement movement within the Three-Self Reform. The article concluded by questioning why the wise government authorities today and the Three-Self reform authorities do not responsibly address such situations, in which "some in the church have misused the freedom of faith and freedom of publication to directly or implicitly spread poison detrimental to the motherland, the church, and the Three-Self Reform."

In addition, Ding Guangxun, the president of Nanjing United Theological Seminary, wrote in the September 1953 issue of the Nanjing United Theological Journal, criticizing certain individuals for unilaterally expanding the concept of "justification by faith," which not only formed a division between believers and non-believers but also generated opposition between believers and the people. Ding argued that theology must first reconcile with the people in order to bear witness for the Lord and evangelize. Therefore, Christians must first love their beloved motherland, connect with the people of the country, integrate into the population, "think as they think, care about what they care about, love what they love, and hate what they hate." Only by establishing common ground with the people could they effectively evangelize.

In January 1954, Wu Yaozong also indirectly refuted the views expressed in Holiness Without Blemish, stating that the book posited: "The issues that God cares about are not questions of good and bad, but of source," and "Humans are asked whether they have love, but God asks where that love comes from," which he deemed "absurd." Wu referenced the story of the Good Samaritan, asserting that Jesus' standard is to consider a matter based solely on its good or bad outcomes, without inquiring into the source. God's will is to "do good," hence, "a good person, regardless of whether they profess Christian faith, is accepted by God." He further asserted that "all goodness comes from God," and thus posed the question: "Can those who believe themselves to be atheists, yet are actually adhering to God's will, not also recognize God's truth and demonstrate God's goodness?"

Why did the Three-Self organizations criticize Holiness Without Blemish at the end of 1953? This is likely related to Christians using "faith" as a reason to oppose the Three-Self Movement at that time. The book was originally a message delivered by Ni Tuosheng in Shanghai in 1939 and had not been organized or published until it was finally transcribed and published in 1953 under the oversight of Li Yuanru and Tang Shoulin after being dormant for 14 years. While the content of the book was not specifically aimed at the new China or the Three-Self Reform, the national Three-Self organizations determined that its theological viewpoints had become a rationale for opposing the Three-Self Movement (the church is holy, but the devil uses the sins within the church to accuse it; the world is under the devil's control and filled with sin, thus believers should distance themselves from the world), leading believers to inadvertently develop hostility toward the new China and the Three-Self Reform. Ni Tuosheng had been arrested in April 1952 (the news was not made public) due to his opposition to the Three-Self Reform interfering with spiritual faction beliefs, which later the meeting places further solidified. Hence, the national Three-Self’s criticism of Ni’s work was fundamentally aimed at countering the theological discourse of Christians who opposed the Three-Self Movement.

In response to another key figure opposing the Three-Self Movement, Wang Mingdao (王明道), who rejected participation in the movement based on the rationale that "believers and non-believers cannot be unequally yoked," the national Three-Self decided to counter his argument. In February 1955, Tianfeng published an article signed by "Qin Mu" (秦牧), which is the pen name of Huang Peiyong (黃培永), an official within the National Youth Association. The article, titled "The Correct Meaning and Misinterpretation of 'You are not to be unequally yoked with unbelievers,'" refuted Wang Mingdao's supposed distortion of the scripture from a hermeneutical perspective, arguing that "the refusal to participate in the anti-imperialist patriotic movement lacks biblical foundation and stems instead from the poison of imperialist thought." Shortly thereafter, the Guangxue Association published a standalone work entitled "The Testimony of Faith," which cited 13 scripture passages and examined the meaning of "yoke." It argued that Paul's original intention was for Christians not to be unequally yoked with anyone bearing "the yoke of sin," nor with adherents of other religions or those without faith. However, interpreting this to mean that Christians should have no interaction with non-believers or that there should be opposition between belief and unbelief would be a "poisonous distortion" of the scriptures.

In 1956, Tianfeng organized two large discussions on the topics of "How Christians View the World" (February to May) and "The Relationship Between Christians and the World" (July to November), publishing opinions from seminary teachers, pastors, and believers on these issues. According to statistics, Tianfeng published a total of 32 articles discussing how Christians perceive the world (Is it of the devil? Is it of God?), whether humans are completely depraved or reflect God's image, the relationship between belief and unbelief, and the link between faith and moral behavior. These discussions were not merely about differing theological perspectives; more importantly, they applied to how Christians view the new China and their relationships with non-believers in Chinese society. Additionally, Zhang Bohuai (張伯懷), the director of the Guangxue Association's publishing department, published works such as "What Does 'Do Not Love the World' Mean?" and volumes of sermons including "Collection of Sermons, Volume One" (1954), "Collection of Sermons, Volume Two" (1955), and "The Vision from Heaven" (by Han Peter, 1955), all of which addressed related topics.

(6) We Are for Faith 1955 (Wang Mingdao (王明道) 1955; Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2010: 127-128; Harvey 2002)

At the end of May 1955, after rereading Wu Yaozong's (吳耀宗) work Darkness and Light, Wang Mingdao received what he called a "new guidance" and decided to write a piece reacting "from a different point of faith." This article, which was written from June 3 to June 9 and spans over 20,000 words, was titled We Are for Faith and was published in the June issue of Spiritual Food Quarterly. Wang placed great importance on this article, explaining it multiple times in study sessions and even publishing it as a separate booklet with a print run of 5,000 copies.

In the article, he first explained the contradiction between fundamentalism and modern theology, then used Wu Yaozong's writings to illustrate the modernist denial of "fundamental doctrines" (unbelief). Based on this, he asserted, "This is the faith of the modernists! This is the truth of the modernists! Should I label them 'the unbelieving faction'—is this a slander? Is it unjust to them?" Further addressing the "unbelieving faction," Wang Mingdao pointed out that the principle of "mutual respect" does not apply because "they do not believe in Jesus; they are not Christians; yet they masquerade as Christians, mingling in the church, spreading some plausible but fictitious doctrines to confuse believers and undermine their faith. These people are thieves in the church—wolves in sheep's clothing." To "prevent them from harming God's flock," it was necessary to oppose them, emphasizing that "there can be no talk of respect or unity with these individuals."

Next, he countered Cui Xianxiang's arguments, stating that the differences between the two factions are far from "minor." In response to Ding Guangxun's call for "unity," Wang's reply was that union should only occur with "those who genuinely have faith and are passionate in loving the Lord," while they must "separate from the unbelieving faction and evil forces." He clearly declared, "I am one who cannot unite due to certain significant differences in faith." Furthermore, he insisted on "fighting them in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ." He criticized Wang Weifan (汪維藩) for being part of the spiritual faction while also participating in the Three-Self Movement, reaffirming that the modernists and fundamentalists are far from being "similar."

Finally, Wang Mingdao reiterated, "There can be no association with this kind of 'unbelieving faction,' nor participation in any of their organizations, as this is the truth of the Bible. 'We are willing to pay any price and make any sacrifice; distortion and slander will not intimidate us."

The article We Are for Faith summarizes Wang Mingdao's stance and resolve, later becoming a classic document against the Three-Self Movement.

(7) The Self-Election and Self-Consecration of Chinese Catholic Bishops 1957-58 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2019: 344-346)

During the Chinese Catholic Representatives Conference held on July 15, 1957, a conflict arose between the Vatican's Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith and the Chinese Catholic Church regarding the situation of Bishop Zhang Shilong (張士琅) of Shanghai. Following the arrest of the original bishop of the Shanghai diocese, Gong Pinmei (龔品梅), for counter-revolutionary crimes, the diocese elected Zhang as the acting bishop in March 1956 and filed the election with the Vatican's Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith in accordance with canon law. In July 1957, the Congregation officially sent a letter to the Shanghai Diocese, vetoing this arrangement in the name of the Pope. Consequently, all representatives at the Chinese Catholic Representatives Conference issued a "serious protest" against the Vatican's actions. After the meeting, the Party group of the State Council's Religious Affairs Bureau made a decision stating, "In the future, after the election of a bishop (or acting bishop) in the Catholic Church, there should be no report to the Vatican for approval... The Chinese Catholic Church can gradually achieve independence from the control of the Vatican."

In December 1957, the Chengdu Diocese held a representative conference, electing Li Xiting (李熙亭) as bishop, along with Tang Jun (唐俊) and Li Daoqu (李道揆) as deputy bishops. While a report was also sent to the Vatican, no consecration ceremony was held. In March 1958, the Hankou and Wuchang dioceses elected Dong Guangqing (董光清) and Yuan Wenhua (袁文華) as bishops through a "vote." At that time, both dioceses telegraphed the Congregation seeking the Pope's permission. However, the Congregation responded that the power to appoint bishops belongs solely to the Pope and that elections cannot replace this authority. Those who consecrated a bishop without the Pope's permission, including the consecrator and the consecrated, would incur automatic excommunication. In April, respectively, the bishop consecration ceremonies were held in Wuhan and Hankou, formally initiating the precedent for "self-election and self-consecration."

By July 1958, 19 dioceses—including Nanjing, Jinan, Taiyuan, Kaifeng, Banzhou, and Kunming—conducted self-elections for bishops, some of which also held consecration ceremonies. Additionally, the self-elected bishops were required to file with the local religious affairs department to facilitate government management of the church.

Despite facing significant political repression and even arrests, many clergy were unwilling to participate in the Patriotic Association and maintained close ties with the Vatican. The practice of self-election and self-consecration marked the public rupture in the relationship between the Chinese church and the Vatican, becoming a crucial historical backdrop for the subsequent division of the Chinese Catholic Church into a patriotic church and an underground church. According to data from the Chinese Catholic Patriotic Association and the Bishops' Conference, from 1958 to 2000, the Chinese Catholic Church has self-elected and self-consecrated 179 bishops.

Anti-Christian Leaders

(1) Zhou Enlai (周恩來) (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2023: 106, 112; 2012: 24, 29-34, 39-40; MacInnis 1972: 24)

Zhou Enlai (周恩來) (1883-1976), originally from Zhejiang Shaoxing (浙江紹興), was born in Jiangsu Huai'an (江蘇淮安). In 1913, he studied at Tianjin Nankai Middle School (天津南開中學堂) and later attended Nankai University (南開大學). He briefly studied in Japan, France, and the United Kingdom, and joined a communist group while in France. During the first United Front between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party of China (CCP), he served as the secretary of the central bureau of the CCP in the Soviet Area. During the War of Resistance Against Japan, he was the head of the Eighth Route Army's office in Chongqing. After the establishment of the People's Republic of China in 1949, he held positions as the Premier of the State Council, secretary of the Party Group, chairman of the National Political Consultative Conference, and chairman of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China. He passed away in January 1976.

Zhou Enlai was a key figure in the religious work of the CCP during the early years of the new state. In June 1950, at the second Party Group meeting of the National Political Consultative Conference, Zhou categorized the religions in China into "ethnic religions" and those "connected with politics," where the former included Islam (回教) and Tibetan Buddhism (喇嘛教), and the latter referred to Christianity (基督教) and Catholicism (天主教) which he saw as being "related to imperialism." He emphasized that one must not underestimate the use of Catholicism and Christianity as tools of aggression by imperialists. Chinese religious believers and activities must "cut off relations with imperialism to thwart its conspiracies." However, this struggle primarily targeted activities involving reactionaries and counter-revolutionaries, while the majority of believers remained the focus of unity. When dealing with reactionaries in the religious community, one must not "involve general religious issues" and reaffirmed the "advocacy of respecting everyone's freedom of belief," with the government pledging to protect churches and temples from occupation and destruction.

In March 1950, the first National United Front Work Conference was held in Beijing to establish the fundamental principles for the work related to Catholicism and Christianity after the founding of the country. Zhou pointed out that the central policy was "to sever ties with imperialism," asserting that "religions in China should be managed by Chinese people." He insisted on opposing imperialism while avoiding "involving religious belief issues."

In May 1950, Zhou met with Christian leaders three times. In a second discussion on May 6, he addressed their concerns, especially regarding government policies on foreign missionaries and overseas donations, pointing out that "foreign missionaries can easily unwittingly become tools of imperialism," hence church groups should not hire more. However, the government did not require those missionaries currently in China to leave immediately. Regarding overseas donations, Zhou indicated that, in principle, "no more fundraising should be done overseas," but also noted that the government would not be "blindly exclusionary" as long as there were no conditionalities attached. He also highlighted that the primary obstacle for Christianity in China was its connection to imperialism. He stated plainly that the public's opposition to Christianity was inextricably linked to over a century of its association with imperialist aggression. He reaffirmed, "Today, American imperialism continues to attempt to use China's religious groups to undermine the People's Republic of China." Therefore, the government required religious groups to clear and sever ties with imperialism, establishing self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating churches.

Zhou Enlai fully understood that most church leaders would find it difficult to accept the accusations of Christianity's connection to imperialism. Thus, in a third meeting on May 13 lasting over four hours, he candidly stated that the government's acceptance of Christianity hinges on the possibility of political cooperation between the two sides. He clearly articulated that theists and atheists, idealists and materialists could also cooperate politically: “Eliminating imperialist influence within the religious community does not mean everyone in the religious community is an instrument of imperialism. Personally, individuals may not feel exploited; however, imperialism subjectively has demands; they use religious groups as tools, which is a fact. Many believers are sometimes inevitably exploited as well. This point must be made clear. Clarifying this issue will only benefit the church.” Zhou emphasized that the government does not seek to eradicate religion but rather aims to "cleanse the bad apples, a very small number of lackeys, and Judases from the religious community." He did not hide the government's bottom line: "Our united front must expand, with boundaries contingent on whether relationships with imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism have been severed." "If you stand firm politically, you will not face discrimination; it is the only path forward." Zhou Enlai's discourse facilitated the emergence of the Three-Self Reform Declaration by Wu Yaocong (吳耀宗) and subsequently the signature campaign.

In June 1950, Zhou Enlai (周恩來) again emphasized the need to help religious figures "sever ties with imperialism" during the Third Plenary Session of the Seventh Central Committee of the Communist Party of China (中共七屆三中全會), encouraging them to "manage religion themselves, self-perform and self-conduct, and not allow foreigners to propagate." In July, Zhou instructed the Cultural and Educational Committee under the State Council to establish a "Research Group on Religious Issues," led by the Deputy Director of the Cultural Committee, Lu Dingyi (陸定一), with the Deputy Secretary-General, Shao Quanlin (邵荃麟), as its head. The group's main task was to provide opinions to the State Council regarding the situations involving Catholicism, Christianity, and Buddhism.

On December 29, the 65th executive meeting of the State Council passed documents including the "Decision on the Guidelines for Dealing with Cultural and Educational Relief Agencies and Religious Groups Receiving American Subsidies" and the "Regulations on the Registration of Cultural and Educational Relief Agencies and Religious Groups Receiving Foreign Subsidies." During the meeting, Zhou pointed out: "In the past, we anticipated that it would take another three to four years to completely eliminate the remnants of American imperialism in China, but recently the U.S. announced the freezing of our assets within its territory, which presents us with a very favorable opportunity to hasten the removal of the remnants of American imperialism from our country. Now, by issuing this order, we are delivering a serious blow to American imperialism."

(2) Lu Dingyi (陸定一) (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 2023: 112-116; 2012: 79-85)

Lu Dingyi (陸定一) (1906-1996) was from Wuxi, Jiangsu (江蘇無錫). He enrolled at Shanghai Nanyang University (上海南洋大學) in 1922 and joined the Communist Party of China (中共) in 1925. After graduating, he engaged in revolutionary work, mainly in the Communist Youth League and in propaganda. In 1937, he became the Director of the Propaganda Department of the Political Department of the Eighth Route Army. In 1945, he served as the Director of the Propaganda Department of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China. After the founding of the People's Republic of China, he held positions as the Director of the Propaganda Department of the Central Committee (1949-1952; 1954-1965), Deputy Director of the Council of Cultural and Educational Affairs of the Central People's Government (1953-1954), and Secretary of the Party Group of the Cultural and Educational Committee of the State Council (1949-1952). During the Cultural Revolution, he faced criticism and was even imprisoned. He was exonerated in 1979 and served as an advisor to the Propaganda Department of the Central Committee in 1980. He passed away in May 1996.

The religious control system during the early years of the People's Republic of China was centered on the Religious Issues Committee, established in January 1951 (later renamed the Religious Work Committee), primarily led by the Propaganda Department with assistance from the United Front Work Department. In July 1950, the Cultural and Educational Committee under the State Council established the "Research Group on Religious Issues," which was headed by Lu Dingyi, responsible for matters related to Catholicism, Christianity, and Buddhism (Islam and Tibetan Buddhism were managed by the State Ethnic Affairs Commission). Lu Dingyi, as the Director of the Central Propaganda Department and Deputy Director of the Cultural and Educational Committee, was a key leader overseeing matters related to Christianity and Catholicism until 1954.

From January 16 to 25, 1951, the Second National United Front Work Conference convened in Beijing, focusing on united front work under the backdrop of the War to Resist U.S. Aggression and Aid Korea. Lu Dingyi spoke on the work related to Christianity and Catholicism, asserting the need to eliminate the cultural invasion influence of American imperialism in China, which he described as "the struggle against imperialism." To achieve a victory in the anti-imperialist struggle, he claimed, "In addition to promoting a general mass anti-imperialist movement, we also need to attract Catholics and Christians to our side." He candidly pointed out that these believers had been influenced by American imperialism for a long time, which made mobilizing them a challenging task. He specifically reminded officials to approach the issue from the perspective of "patriotism," advising against starting with accusations of the church being used by imperialism, as this would make them "uncomfortable and difficult to accept." Instead, he suggested first explaining the principles of patriotism and then addressing the issue of "bad elements" within the church, which would make it easier for them to accept. Lu emphasized that churches in China "will not be eliminated quickly," and even to "weaken" them posed significant difficulties, stating that "one cannot implement a dictatorship over religion." Therefore, the focus of current religious work should be to ensure that the church "politically and organizationally severs ties with imperialism." He stressed the conflict between idealism (religion) and materialism, and that the current emphasis was on demanding religious groups detach from imperialism, with the government needing to support the reform movements within Christianity to bring the bulk of the believers away from the influence of imperialism, aiming to achieve the goal of transforming the church.

From April 16 to 21, 1951, the Cultural and Educational Committee of the State Council held a "Meeting on Handling Christian Groups Receiving American Subsidies." Lu Dingyi (陸定一) pointed out that the meeting represented "a struggle," aiming to eliminate the cultural invasion of America on China. Chinese Christians must stand on common political ground of patriotism with the government. He repeatedly emphasized how American imperialism historically and currently uses Christianity for cultural invasion and particularly criticized the "ultra-political" stance of Christians, stating that they must assist the government in exposing counter-revolutionaries within the church. He hoped that Chinese believers would actively participate in the three major movements, eliminate the thoughts of fear, admiration, and adulation towards America, and foster feelings of hatred, disdain, and contempt for American imperialism. At the meeting, Lu clearly called for a movement to accuse the church.

From November 27 to December 16, 1953, the Second National Religious Work Conference was held. The meeting was chaired by Lu Dingyi, who was both the Minister of Propaganda of the Central Committee and Deputy Director of the Cultural and Educational Committee of the State Council. The conference summarized the work of national Christianity from 1950 to 1953 and formulated guidelines and tasks for future work.

(3) Zhang Zhiyi (張執一) (Shanghai Local Chronicle; Zhang Zhiyi 張執一 1957: 52-77; 1958a; 1958b; 1958c: 2102-2103; MacInnis 1972: 23)

Zhang Zhiyi (張執一) (1911-1983) was from Hanyang, Hubei (湖北漢陽). He joined the Communist Youth League of China (中國共產黨青年團) in 1927 and became a member of the Communist Party of China (中共) in 1929. After 1935, he worked in Shanghai and was involved in various national salvation organizations. In 1946, he organized and led mass protests for peace against the civil war in Shanghai. In 1947, when the Shanghai Bureau of the Central Committee of the Communist Party was established, he successively served as the Secretary of the Cultural and Industrial United Front Committee of the Shanghai Bureau, Secretary of the Outer County Working Committee, and Secretary of the Counter-Revolutionary Working Committee. After the founding of the People’s Republic of China, he held several positions including a member of the Standing Committee of the Central South Bureau of the Communist Party, Secretary-General of the Central South Military and Political Committee, and Deputy Director of the Central Ethnic Affairs Commission.

From August 1954 to January 1965, Zhang Zhiyi served as the Deputy Minister of the United Front Work Department. After the Anti-Rightist Movement, he used the Party’s line to interpret united front work. In his 1957 article "Class Contradictions and Internal Contradictions of the People," he pointed out that China was primarily dealing with "internal contradictions of the people," but also noted that "class contradictions have not been fully resolved, nor has the class struggle ended"; a considerable number of people opposed the Communist Party and even launched attacks against it. In the ideological domain, the "contradiction between proletarian and non-proletarian thought" remained a "prominent contradiction." If not properly managed, it could escalate into a confrontational struggle. Thus, it became necessary to encourage the centrist factions to "lean left," or even "make a leap to the left."

Shortly thereafter, in 1958, he attempted to dominate united front work through class struggle in his article "The People's Democratic United Front is a Special Form of Class Struggle," asserting that "the socialist revolution must use struggle methods to eliminate the bourgeoisie," and that "the People's Democratic United Front" was not only "a special method for engaging in class struggle" but also "a special weapon for carrying out the socialist revolutionary cause and consolidating the dictatorship of the proletariat." The united front combined unity and struggle, with the purpose "to continuously achieve transformation." Fundamentally, the united front emphasized "peaceful transformation," specifically through "buyout policies" (i.e., granting economic and political benefits) and educational methods to facilitate self-transformation, which involves both tension and relaxation, necessitating "a long-term struggle process." He firmly believed that a "new favorable situation" had already emerged, accelerating "the pace of socialist transformation," thereby realizing "a socialist society without class exploitation."

If class struggle has not yet ended, then the united front also becomes a "special form" of class struggle. How does the Communist Party of China (中共) handle religious issues? In 1958, he published an article titled "Atheists and Theists Can Cooperate Politically and Follow the Socialist Path" in the journal *Philosophical Research* (《哲學研究》). On the surface, the title suggests that atheists can cooperate with theists politically, but in reality, the article expressed his understanding of the relationship between religion and "class contradictions." It is evident that after the Anti-Rightist Movement, the Communist Party assessed the complexities of religious issues amidst the struggle between socialism and capitalism under the "two roads" framework: (1) Within internal contradictions of the people, "this does not mean that there are no class contradictions regarding religious issues; nor do enemy and friend contradictions entirely not exist." In passing through this socialist revolution, "the situation of the religious community remains very complex" amidst the struggle between the two roads; (2) This is reflected in the fact that the political awareness of believers regarding socialism has not yet reached a high level; there are still very few "progressive individuals (leftists) who genuinely uphold patriotism against imperialism, support the socialist path, and the leadership of the Communist Party." Many within the religious community, although they "may accept the leadership of the Communist Party and travel down the socialist path," are politically still in a neutral state, "harboring varying degrees of skepticism or opposition towards the Party and socialism." More seriously, some religious individuals "have not severed ties with imperialism and counter-revolutionaries, with some even openly stating their unwillingness to break away from the relationship with imperialism (especially the Vatican), opposing the new socialist China and the leadership of the Communist Party"; (3) In this regard, the contradictions exhibited are "antagonistic contradictions, i.e., enemy and friend contradictions." These "bourgeois reactionary rightists in the religious community" often don the guise of religion, being utilized by "imperialists, residual counter-revolutionaries, members of the landlord class, and wealthy peasants" to wage a struggle against the Chinese people on religious issues; (4) Catholicism and Christianity were originally tools of imperialist aggression, and currently, imperialism is still reluctant to accept its failure in China. The threat from imperialism to China persists; thus, their struggle against the Chinese people is long-term. "We must continue to work to completely detach religion from the influence and control of imperialist forces and hostile classes, converting the religious issue entirely into an internal contradiction of the people."

At the time, the socialist education movement in the religious community (i.e., the Anti-Rightist Movement) was in full swing. Zhang Zhiyi (張執一) raised the issue of "breaking" and "establishing," indicating that although this movement was "to break the capitalist political stance among religious figures and establish a socialist political stance," it did not "require religious believers to abandon their faith." He acknowledged that Marxist-Leninists could "firmly uphold a socialist political stance" without necessitating "that everyone who supports the socialist political system is a Marxist-Leninist," allowing them to retain different worldviews ideologically and to keep their respective religious beliefs. In this publicly published article, he responded to criticisms that the Communist Party's "religious policy is a scam" and that "Communists aim to eradicate religion," asserting the importance of the policy of religious freedom despite the article's strong flavor of "class struggle."

However, Zhang's remarks at internal meetings conveyed a different message. In May 1958, the Communist Party convened a professional meeting on religious work, where Zhang delivered a speech to numerous religious cadres, communicating the central government's basic positioning on religious work. He pointed out that religion, as a "superstructure from the old society," had lost its foundation after the elimination of the exploiting class, but it would not "immediately disappear," and its ideology might persist for a considerable period. However, "overall, religion cannot develop and will gradually weaken; we should consciously promote its gradual weakening." The term "consciously promote" refers to implementing reforms in the religious system.

So, does the government's leadership in reforming religion contradict the policy of religious freedom? In the current political context, how should the policy of religious freedom be understood? Zhang Zhiyi (張執一) particularly emphasized that religious freedom not only includes the freedom to promote religion but also the "freedom to propagate anti-religion" and "freedom of disbelief." His understanding of "protecting religious freedom" is that it does "not aid the development of religion, but rather aims to politically unite believers, encouraging them to develop productivity alongside the working people and make political progress." Zhang candidly pointed out that in order to achieve the "elimination" of religion, one must first facilitate its "weakening." "The protection of today is for the elimination of tomorrow. Protecting religious freedom is about creating conditions that encourage differentiation within the religious community, enabling them to transform themselves through self-modification, ultimately achieving the elimination of religion."

Anti-Christian Organizations

(1) Chinese Communist Party (Ying 2014: 884-901; MacInnis 1972; Yang 2012: 65-72)

The Chinese Communist Party (中國共產黨, Zhōngguó Gòngchǎndǎng) is a revolutionary party with a strong ideological orientation. Whether before or after the founding of the nation, it insists on transforming Chinese society according to revolutionary ideals and goals (communism), while also needing to adjust according to the realities of political demands. As a party that adheres to materialism and atheism, how to handle religious issues is an unavoidable topic during its revolutionary state-building process. From a worldview perspective, the CCP (中共, Zhōnggòng) maintains the opposition between materialism and idealism, viewing religion as the "opiate of the people" and its eventual extinction. However, due to practical political needs, it establishes a balance between unity and struggle regarding religion, as well as determining what means to use to achieve the extinction of religion. Consequently, religious work has always been an important aspect of the CCP's united front work, and it makes judgments on unity and struggle regarding religion according to the political lines and needs of different eras.

Reviewing the history of the CCP since its founding in 1921, religious struggle became one of the fronts of the youth movement, student movement, and mass movement. The newly formed CCP actively intervened in the anti-Christian movement in 1922 and infiltrated Christian Youth Associations. It can be said that Christianity was the first religion the CCP strategically engaged with and treated as an important narrative and weapon in its anti-imperialist struggle under the notion of "cultural invasion." During the 1930s and 40s, from the anti-Japanese resistance to the civil war between the Nationalists and Communists, the CCP responded to practical political needs while emphasizing the importance of uniting patriotic religious figures to expand the united front.

After the founding of New China, with the complex development of both internal and external situations, the CCP's assessment of the social revolutionary landscape, the balance of power, the ebb and flow of internal contradictions within the people, and the changes in power struggles within the CCP all influenced its religious policies. From the early focus on "New People's Democracy" to its involvement in the Korean War, where the movement to resist America and aid Korea fully unfolded, the CCP engaged in fierce struggles against imperialism (especially against American imperialism) and various mass political movements closely associated with it (land reform, ideological transformation, and the Three Antis and Five Antis campaigns). It can be said that during the years from 1949 to 1953, although Christianity became one of the five recognized religions, it also needed a transformation in the anti-imperialist struggle. Although the CCP proposed separating religion from politics (imperialism), during the process of "political overreach" and the radicalization of mass movements, it became easy to encounter issues regarding the broadening of targets when defining the "running dogs" and "toxins" of imperialism. However, in 1953, the CCP also began to realize the underlying problems and hence re-established the goal of "unity."

In 1953, the CCP announced the end of the New Democratic stage and gradually transitioned to the transformation into a socialist society. However, regarding the path to socialism and how to eliminate the exploiting classes, it still opposed "direct elimination" methods, instead advocating for "gradual transformation." Minister Li Weihan from the Central United Front Work Department emphasized that one should not attempt to overthrow religion with atheistic propaganda and pointed out that "the elimination of religion is only possible when humanity has eradicated classes and greatly developed its ability to control nature." Under these circumstances, the CCP understood "elimination of religion" as a natural consequence of achieving a socialist society (i.e., natural extinction), with religious work during the transitional period focusing mainly on unity and transformation, with struggle as a secondary element. The nationwide Christian conference in 1954 proposed "expanding unity," which can be seen as a concrete explanation of this direction.

However, when the "expansion of unity" faced resistance from spiritual factions, the CCP viewed this as a target for struggle, and through the anti-revolutionary movement of 1955, the issue of anti-Three Self religious groups was treated as a political problem of anti-revolution. As a result, in 1955 and 1956, the anti-revolutionary groups led by Wang Mingdao (王明道) and Ni Tuosheng (倪柝聲) in Christianity, as well as the anti-revolutionary group led by Gong Pinmei (龔品梅) in Catholicism, were successively uncovered. After the anti-revolutionary struggle, the CCP succeeded in forcing the anti-Three Self spiritual factions to join the patriotic Three Self camp.

In 1956, the political line of the CCP (中共, Zhōnggòng) underwent a relaxed adjustment, and Mao Zedong (毛澤東) declared that “the large-scale, tempestuous mass-class struggle has fundamentally ended,” though “class struggle has not completely ended.” Consequently, he proposed the direction of “correctly handling contradictions among the people.” In this context, religion was viewed as a contradiction among the people rather than a contradiction with hostile classes. At the same time, the CCP put forward the slogan “Let a hundred flowers bloom and a hundred schools of thought contend,” resulting in a relatively open environment for Chinese Christianity from 1956 to 1957. However, following the events of May and June in 1957, the CCP's assessment of the situation shifted towards the anti-rightist struggle, and the line of class struggle once again dominated the CCP’s religious policy, leading to a large-scale political reckoning with Chinese Christianity, where most of the originally united subjects were categorized as “rightist” elements.

In 1958, China continued along the path of the anti-rightist struggle’s enemy-versus-friend line while being dominated by an atmosphere of "leap forward" towards a communist society, resulting in further radicalization of religious work. A “promote weakening” line aimed at the “extinction of religion” emerged. Under this extreme "left" line, the Christian system faced unprecedented assaults, with numerous church venues being closed under the guise of "joint worship," and religious personnel being labeled as the "exploiting class." The “religion-free zone” experiment illustrated that religion had become the final target the CCP sought to "eliminate."

(2) Disintegration of the Jesus Family 1952 (Tao Feiya (陶飛亞) 2004; Luo Weihong (羅偉虹) 2014: 676-679; Lian 2010: 64-84; Wickeri 1990: 160-162)

The Jesus Family, founded by Jing Dianyong (敬奠瀛) in 1927, was a Chinese indigenous sect characterized by charismatic elements that emphasized a communal lifestyle (family). In 1949, there were a total of 104 "families" nationwide, with 12,000 believers. After the founding of the People's Republic of China, the Jesus Family actively supported the Three-Self Reform signature campaign, accounting for 381 of the 407 signatures from Shandong Province on the first batch of Three-Self Declaration (1,527 people). By April 1951, the number of signatories from the Jesus Family reached 1,747, making up one-fifth of the total in Shandong. Jing Dianyong was elected a member of the presidium at the "Handling Christian Organizations Receiving American Subsidies Conference" convened by the State Council in April 1951. During the Korean War, the Jesus Family organized a volunteer medical team to support the war effort, departing for the Korean battlefield on March 1, 1951, becoming the only Christian group to reach the front lines.

In June 1951, at a Three-Self Reform seminar in Taian, Jing served as a member of the presidium, but voices began to emerge accusing the Jesus Family, and Jing also made a self-accusation. Internal contradictions within the Jesus Family, particularly issues of patriarchal leadership and Jing's personal lifestyle, gradually surfaced. Soon after, an accusation meeting was held at the family’s original residence in Mazhuang, where a "Family Management Committee" was established, abolishing the patriarchal system. In December, Jing was summoned to Shanghai for training and to address various issues, during which he was required to reflect on the history of both the Jesus Family and himself.

In March 1952, the chairman of the Family Management Committee wrote to Wu Yaozong, chairman of the Three-Self Reform Preparatory Committee, reporting internal issues within the Jesus Family and requesting personnel to lead the reform. In April, a national Three-Self organization dispatched a working group led by Shen Derong (沈德溶), along with Zhu David (朱大衛) and Jin Qichang (金啟昌), to Taian, where they settled in Mazhuang with the assistance of Christian and government departments in Shandong Province. Once the working team arrived, they held discussion sessions, individual talks, established a study committee, and organized believers' study. Shen Derong and officials from the Taian United Front Work Department gave reports on "How Imperialism Uses Christianity to Invade China," "The Direction of the Three-Self Reform Movement," "The People's Government's Religious Policy," and "New Democratic Society and Its Future." In May, an exhibition was held to expose issues related to leaders like Jing Dianyong and Zuo Shunzheng (左順真). From May 27 to 29, a three-day accusation conference was held, during which Jing was accompanied by Cui Xianxiang back from Shanghai to Mazhuang to accept criticism from the masses. Of those present, over 120 registered requests to make accusations, with 60 ultimately airing their grievances on stage.

After the accusation meeting, it was decided to "split the Jesus Family," restoring 138 households related to blood relations and distributing them into agricultural households, industrial households, medical personnel households, teachers' households, and households returning to production, among others, in a reallocation of property. By October 1952, the "division of households" was completed, formally declaring the disintegration of the Jesus Family. The "Mazhuang Jesus Family" was renamed "Beixinzhuang Christian Church." The working group announced the "end of the domination and utilization of the Mazhuang Jesus Family by imperialism and reactionary feudal forces," and ultimately, Jing Dianyong was arrested by the government and imprisoned in Taian Prison.

With the disintegration of the "original household," the "small households" across the country also engaged in Three-Self Reform studies, underwent separation, and dissolved their existing organizations. The Jesus Family became the first Christian sect to be entirely "banned" after the establishment of the People's Republic of China.

(3) Christian Counter-Revolutionary Group Case 1955-1956 (Xing Fuzeng (邢福增) 2010: 97-147; 2005: 121-140; Wickeri 1990: 162-170)

In 1955, left-wing literary theorist Hu Feng (胡風) was criticized for his independent views and shortly thereafter was officially labeled by the Central Committee as a "struggle against the Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group." This nationwide anti-Hu Feng movement further evolved into a large-scale campaign to purge counter-revolutionaries. On July 1 and August 25, the Central Committee issued directives titled "Regarding the Launch of Struggles to Clear Hidden Counter-Revolutionaries" and "Regarding the Thorough Purge of Hidden Counter-Revolutionaries," respectively.

In the context of the campaign against counter-revolutionaries, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Office convened over 500 Christian figures on June 14 to hold a meeting to expose the "Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group," requiring the religious community to reveal counter-revolutionaries. Following this, several Christian individuals spoke out, emphasizing the need to be vigilant and guard against counter-revolutionaries hiding within Christianity. On June 20, both the national and Shanghai Three-Self Movement Committees held a discussion meeting to denounce the Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group.

On July 11, the journal Tianfeng published an editorial titled "Strengthen Unity, Distinguish Right from Wrong," which directly criticized Wang Mingdao (王明道) of the Beijing Christian Church for prominently opposing the Three-Self Movement. His errors included: (1) fundamentally denying that Chinese Christianity was used by imperialism, presenting the toxic ideology of imperialism as the "truth" of the Bible; (2) attempting to incite a conflict between the "Fundamentalists" and "Modernists" within Christianity regarding faith, thereby undermining and nullifying the foundation of the patriotic Three-Self Movement; (3) self-aggrandizing as a servant of God and labeling participating Christians in the Three-Self Movement as "spiritually degenerate" and "infidels." Therefore, in response to Wang Mingdao's "destructive activities under the guise of faith," the editorial called on patriotic Christians to clearly delineate their stance from Mr. Wang based on "the political principles of anti-imperialism and patriotism," and to actively engage in the struggle.

On July 4, 1955, the anti-counter-revolutionary struggle movement began in Beijing, where universities demanded that all students gathering at Christian churches report on and denounce Wang's issues. On August 5, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Office convened religious figures to study materials related to the "Hu Feng counter-revolutionary group." Director Luo Zhufeng (羅竹風) reiterated that religious believers must "eliminate counter-revolutionaries from within the church." He described the counter-revolutionaries within the religious community as primarily appearing "devout and zealous," but actually just "disguised in religious garb" to deceive the faithful. Luo's report marked the unveiling of the anti-revolutionary campaign in the religious sector.

In the early hours of August 8, Wang Mingdao and his wife were arrested at home, along with approximately 20 others arrested in Beijing, Xiangshan, Changchun, and Guangzhou. On August 15, Tianfeng published a short commentary titled "Expose Wang Mingdao's Reactionary Words and Actions," calling for the "decisive exposure of Wang Mingdao's reactionary words and actions, and an immediate clearing of the line with him." Following Wang's arrest, from August to November, Three-Self organizations and churches across the country launched denunciations against the "Wang Mingdao counter-revolutionary group," with Tianfeng continuously publishing critical articles about Wang. Subsequently, Lin Xiangao (林獻羔), a "Wang Mingdao element" in Guangzhou, was arrested, and a critique of the "Shanghai version of the Wang Mingdao counter-revolutionary rhetoric"—a publication titled "The Holy Anointment"—was initiated in Shanghai. In October and November, the Tianfeng Materials Room compiled "Expose the Counter-Revolutionary Elements of Wang Mingdao's Reactionary Statements" and "Accuse and Expose the Wang Mingdao Counter-Revolutionary Group," comprehensively criticizing Wang Mingdao and his associates.

After Wang Mingdao's arrest, he succumbed to pressure and threats, confessing to the government. On September 29, 1956, having agreed to repent and participate in the Three-Self Movement, he was "released for educational purposes." However, since he failed to fulfill his commitments, Wang and his wife were re-arrested by the government on April 29, 1958. In April 1961, the Beijing People's Procuratorate formally prosecuted them under the "Regulations on Punishing Counter-Revolutionaries." In July 1963, the Beijing Intermediate People's Court sentenced Wang Mingdao and Liu Jingwen (劉景文) to life imprisonment and 15 years in prison for counter-revolutionary crimes. Wang Mingdao appealed against the sentence, but in September, the Criminal Final Division of the Beijing High People's Court upheld the original verdict.

The "Wang Mingdao Counter-Revolutionary Group" case became a focal point of the anti-counter-revolutionary struggle within the Christian community, signaling the government’s targeting of those hardliners in churches who rejected participation in the Three-Self Movement based on differing beliefs. At that time, apart from Wang Mingdao, the Christian Assembly also represented the most organized and populous faction against the Three-Self Movement, undoubtedly becoming the government's next target.

Starting in mid-January 1956, leaders of Christian assemblies in Hunan (Changsha, Hengyang, Zhuzhou, Changde, Cili, Xiangtan, etc.), Guangdong (Guangzhou), Zhejiang (Hangzhou, Ningbo, Wenzhou, Pingyang, Xiaoshan, Ninghai, Ruian, Huangyan, Jinhua, Pan'an, Shaoxing, Jiaxing, Jiashan, etc.), and Fujian (Fuzhou, Xiamen, Quanzhou, Jinjiang, Putian, Fuqing, Minhou, Fuding, Gutian, Liancheng, Zhangpu, etc.) were successively arrested by public security authorities on charges of counter-revolution. These arrests were followed by widespread accusation meetings, calling for the Christian community to denounce the hidden counter-revolutionaries within the assemblies, as congregants everywhere began to step forward to participate in the accusations. Thus, a nationwide campaign to regulate the assemblies was launched.

As the national center of the assemblies, the Shanghai Assembly became the target of regulation on January 29, 1956. That evening, the Shanghai Public Security Bureau arrested Li Yuanru (李淵如), Wang Peizhen (汪佩真), Zhang Yuzhi (張愚之), Lan Zhi (藍志一), among others, officially declaring the dismantling of the "Ni Tuosheng Counter-Revolutionary Group" within the Shanghai Assembly. On January 30, the Shanghai Religious Affairs Office convened a meeting to announce the crimes of the Ni Tuosheng counter-revolutionary group, attended by over 2,500 Christians. From February 8 to 19, an exhibition showcasing evidence of the Ni Tuosheng counter-revolutionary group's crimes was held, drawing nearly 10,000 Shanghai believers. From February 21 to 24, the audience expanded to include congregants from other local assemblies. Multiple issues of Tianfeng starting in February extensively covered this topic and reprinted numerous denunciation articles. On February 22, a large meeting was held at the Nanyang Road Assembly, where over 3,000 assembly members accused the Ni Tuosheng counter-revolutionary group, including representatives of believers from Nanjing, Changsha, and Xuzhou.

On June 21, 1956, the Shanghai High People's Court heard the case against the Ni Tuosheng counter-revolutionaries. Ni confessed to the charges in court. Ultimately, he was sentenced to 15 years in prison, with political rights revoked for 5 years. In October 1958, the Shanghai High People's Court sentenced Li Yuanru and Wang Peizhen to 15 years in prison, while Zhang Yuzhi received a 12-year sentence. One of their charges included engaging in counter-revolutionary activities "in an increasingly aggravated manner" following Ni's arrest. Other arrested assembly members from various regions were also sentenced during 1958.

It is noteworthy that Ni Tuosheng had been arrested by the government as early as April 1952. The anti-Three-Self struggle during 1953-55 in various assemblies was not substantively related to Ni Tuosheng, and he could not lead the anti-struggle. However, in the 1956 counter-revolutionary group case of the Christian Assemblies, it continued to be associated with "Ni Tuosheng" due to his significant position in the assemblies, leading the government to view him as the head of the counter-revolutionary group. Additionally, the CCP attempted to use various evidence involving Ni, especially concerning his moral issues, as a critical weapon against the assemblies. The high-profile trial of Ni Tuosheng (in contrast to Wang Mingdao) and the public disclosure of his evidence indeed brought a tremendous, even fatal, impact on the assemblies.

(4) Wenzhou "No-Religion Experimental Zone" 1958 (Zhi Huaxin (支華欣) 2000: 41-42; Zhu Yujing (朱宇晶) 2011: 95-96; She He (舍禾) 2015: 483-495)

During the Great Leap Forward, national Three-Self organizations proposed in August 1958 to fundamentally change the semi-colonial nature of Christianity by implementing "united worship." Prior to this, Wenzhou in Zhejiang Province had already decided to implement integration starting in July during an expanded Three-Self conference held in May. The model of united worship quickly spread from Wenzhou to various parts of the country, with grassroots congregations uniting with other congregations in the same region, leading to a significant reduction in the number of grassroots congregations.

He suggested, "In terms of religion, could we establish a 'country free of religious activities' throughout China?" He Chengxiang then inspected Wenzhou, and Shen believed that the subsequent "leftist errors regarding religion" in Wenzhou were closely related to He’s visit. Shen Derong referred to Wenzhou's early implementation of united worship and the subsequent establishment of a "no-religion experimental zone."

The "no-religion experimental zone" in Wenzhou was reflected in the counties under its jurisdiction: Pingyang, Yongjia, Dongtou, Ruian, and Cangnan, where all religious activities were essentially halted during the Great Leap Forward. All Christian churches were closed instead of being merged. In the Wenzhou urban area, of the six churches that initially remained open for united worship, only Chengxi Church (城西堂) remained after further consolidations.

A summary report on religious work in Wenzhou in 1959 noted: "In the city, one Catholic church, one Protestant church, and one Buddhist temple will continue to be retained for legitimate religious activities, while all suburban churches have completely ceased services and will not resume activities for the time being. Regarding family gatherings, we will not publicly interfere or recognize them as lawful, but will seek to understand the situation, actively conduct persuasion education, and remain vigilant against rumor-mongers and saboteurs..."

The Wenzhou "no-religion experimental zone" reflected the CCP's policy of "eradicating" religion, which began to take shape during the Great Leap Forward. This policy would later be implemented nationwide during the Cultural Revolution.

(5) Striking Down the True Jesus Church 1958-59 (Inouye 2018: 208-210; Deng Zhaoming (鄧肇明) 1993: 2-19)

During the Great Leap Forward, the True Jesus Church became a key target for suppression and attack. The True Jesus Church is a Chinese indigenous Christian sect founded in 1917. In March 1951, the True Jesus Church held its 12th General Assembly, where it established a reform program expressing a commitment to strengthen political study and express a resolve to support Korea against America. In April, Wei Yisa (魏以撒), as president, attended the "Handling Christian Organizations Receiving American Subsidies Conference" convened by the State Council, co-signing a united declaration. In early 1952, Wei Yisa publicly reflected on his participation in the Three-Self Movement. In March 1953, the headquarters issued a notice to local congregations to study the Three-Self Reform Declaration, resulting in over 13,000 signatures by September. In April, the committee for the Three-Self Reform Movement was established at the True Jesus Church headquarters, which also relocated from Beijing to Wuhan, electing Jiang Yohan (蔣約翰) and Liu Jun'an (劉均安) as chairman and vice-chairman. During this period, Wei Yisa faced criticism.

In May 1958, Zhang Zhiyi (張執一), Vice-Minister of the United Front Work Department, mentioned the need to eliminate the True Jesus Church during a professional meeting on religious work. This suggestion was quickly disseminated to various provinces. On June 5, during a provincial religious work professional meeting in Sichuan, related issues were discussed, and various locations were instructed to "gather intelligence." In January 1959, Sichuan decided to start in Chongqing, an area with a concentrated population of believers, under the unified leadership of the party committee, forming a joint working group composed of United Front, public security, and religious affairs sectors, which then established collaboration groups at the county level to conduct coordinated attacks. By the end of May, it was reported that they had "essentially crushed and discredited the True Jesus Church, achieving the expected goals," and that "the struggle had reached a certain conclusion." For example, of the 135 key figures in the district association in Chongqing, 82 were dealt with (16 arrested, 34 controlled, 2 subjected to forced labor education, 10 engaged in struggle sessions, and 20 subjected to supervision in production and public self-criticism and debate).

The national Three-Self Movement also began its campaign against the True Jesus Church in May 1958. The journal Tianfeng published reports on the "illegal criminal activities" of the Hubei True Jesus Church and emphasized through a "editorial note" that these "criminal activities against the party, socialism, and the people" "must not be tolerated in any way." In June, Tianfeng targeted the True Jesus Church again, reporting on the illegal activities of the True Jesus Church in Hanyang, Hubei, and including a "short commentary" reprinting an article from the Hubei Christian Socialist Education Study Committee denouncing the True Jesus Church. In July, Tianfeng published submissions from readers of the Beijing True Jesus Church, calling for a denunciation of the reactionary group led by Jiang Yohan.

During the Great Leap Forward, the True Jesus Church was singled out and subjected to unprecedented political purges, likely due to its tightly organized national structure and strong charismatic traditions. At the same time, the True Jesus Church’s "exclusivity" in its ecclesiology, asserting itself as the only "true" church, caused it to be viewed as a key obstacle to unity despite its participation in the Three-Self Reform Movement, leading to its comprehensive purge. Following the implementation of united worship, various denominational organizations ceased operations, and congregations had to merge with other sects. However, some scholars pointed out that the True Jesus Church continued to develop in an underground state.
