China: Context (Pre-1900)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

  1. 總理衙門教案章程Tsungli Yamen Proposal 1871（Cohen 1963: 247-261；趙樹好2014：55-60；董叢林2016：126-132）

After the 1870 Tianjin Massacre, the Zongli Yamen (Office for the Management of the Affairs of All Foreign Countries) issued, in February of the following year, a document titled “Eight Articles of the Missionary Cases Regulations”. These were also communicated to the foreign ministries of Britain, France, Germany, the United States, Russia, Spain, Austria, Italy, Denmark, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Sweden, urging them to supervise their respective churches to strictly implement the regulations. The articles reflect the Qing government’s understanding and handling of missionary affairs at the time:

1.Proposed abolishment of foreign-run foundling homes, because many missionary incidents arose from rumors—such as gouging out eyes and cutting out hearts—linked to these institutions, which caused public fear. If such homes were to be established, they should accept only children of converts who had no guardians, and must report to and be registered with local authorities. Non-Christian infants were to be entrusted to Chinese provincial and local officials, who would assign local gentry to adopt them, so that “Chinese and foreigners may each do good according to their own customs, thereby avoiding suspicion.”

2.Chinese women were strictly prohibited from entering churches, and foreign nuns were not allowed to proselytize in China, citing traditional Chinese values of chastity, propriety, and the separation of men and women.

3.Missionaries were forbidden from interfering in lawsuits. Chinese Christian believers were to be treated the same as ordinary civilians in all legal matters. In cases involving disputes between Christians and non-Christians, local officials were to handle them impartially, and missionaries were to “follow Chinese laws and customs.”

4.No further indemnity claims were to be made under the pretext of missionary incidents.

5.Missionary activities were restricted to designated provinces as stated in their permits, and they were forbidden from secretly entering other provinces.

6.Caution was advised in accepting converts. Before admitting someone to the church, their background should be carefully investigated for any history of crime or misconduct. Their information should also be reported and recorded in the local registry for verification purposes.

7.When interacting with local officials, missionaries were to follow Chinese protocol, refrain from inappropriate use of official seals or authority, and behave with decorum and respect.

8.Missionaries were not allowed to unilaterally demand the return of church properties to avoid provoking conflict. If land or buildings needed to be purchased or leased, an application must be submitted to the local officials for investigation. If the site involved issues of geomancy (fengshui), it had to be resolved. Even with official approval, only when there was unanimous consent from the local community and no dissent, could the contract be finalized, clearly stating that the property belonged to the Chinese Christian community. It could not be falsely claimed or transferred to others, nor should missionaries trust deceitful locals who might manipulate property transactions in secret.

The reactions of various countries to the Zongli Yamen’s regulations were largely oppositional in nature.

•Russia stated that China ought to be “tolerant of all religions”, yet the regulations seemed to have “a tone of suppression and harshness.”

•The German government reaffirmed that, under international law, it had the duty to protect the lives, property, and rights of German missionaries in China.

•The American envoy claimed that the regulations mainly targeted Catholic missionaries, and had nothing to do with Protestant missionaries. Since Catholic affairs fell under French jurisdiction, the United States could not intervene.

•The British side remarked that there were no British-run foundling homes in China, so it would not engage in detailed argument on that issue. However, Britain clearly opposed the regulations banning Chinese women from entering churches and prohibiting female missionaries from proselytizing in China, arguing that these measures were incompatible with existing treaties. On legal matters, the British side also maintained that if a missionary was found to be shielding or resisting legal action involving a convert, the case should be reported through diplomatic channels to the relevant foreign envoy.

•Regarding the rejection of criminals and wrongdoers as converts, the British side countered by questioning why Chinese authorities failed to arrest and punish such individuals in the first place. Moreover, it claimed this proposal contradicted the missionary’s role to encourage moral reform.

•As for the issue of reclaiming church properties, Britain reiterated that this was primarily a Catholic concern.

•France expressed strong opposition to the regulations concerning foundling homes and women, arguing they were inconsistent with treaty obligations. It also denied that missionaries had interfered in Chinese administrative affairs, stating that when missionaries sought to intercede on behalf of Chinese Christians, it was simply a matter of compassion and humanity, and should not be condemned. Regarding criminals, France agreed that they should be identified by Chinese officials and referred to the French authorities for handling. China, it argued, should no longer place restrictions on religious belief, as doing so would violate the treaties. As for investigating the locations of former church properties, France considered that already covered by existing agreements.

  1. Imperial Edicts of the 21st Year of the Guangxu Reign (1895)

(Li Gangji 1986: Imperial Edicts, pp. 7–8)

In August 1895, in response to recent missionary incidents in Sichuan and Fujian, the Qing court issued a special imperial edict, stating:

“All generals, governors, and provincial administrators in the various provinces are to strictly instruct their subordinates: wherever there are churches, they must be vigorously protected. Local residents must be clearly instructed not to believe in baseless rumors or give rise to unwarranted suspicion. If anyone dares to use these matters as a pretext to stir up trouble, they shall be severely punished according to the law. Local officials who neglect their duties or act improperly shall also be heavily punished without leniency.”

In September, another edict was issued in reference to the church incident in the provincial capital of Sichuan, stating:

“All of this was due to local officials who failed to properly guide and instruct the people, allowing the situation to escalate. After disturbances broke out, they did not promptly impose punishment.” The edict specifically named Governor-General Liu Bingzhang, declaring that he had failed in his responsibilities, and that when the unrest began, he took no action, did not dispatch troops to suppress idle troublemakers, who then gathered in increasing numbers, resulting in a series of missionary incidents across the province. Consequently, he was dismissed from office and permanently barred from reappointment, as a warning to others.

In October, as missionary incidents once again erupted in Sichuan and Fujian, yet another edict was issued:

“There are many churches throughout the provinces, and it is the duty of local officials to constantly educate and guide the common people, and to strictly prevent even minor grievances or petty quarrels from suddenly escalating into conflicts. If there are malicious individuals who fabricate rumors and incite agitation, they must be closely investigated, arrested, interrogated, and punished, in order to eliminate trouble before it arises.”

The edict further warned that if local officials continued to be negligent and allowed disturbances to occur, they would be severely punished without exception.

  1. 光緒21年諭旨 1895（李剛己1986：諭旨7-8）

  2. Memorial from the Zongli Yamen on the Approved Procedures for Handling Missionary Cases (1896)

(Lian Lizhi and Wang Shouzhong, 1980: pp. 426–427)

In May 1896, the Zongli Yamen (Office for the Management of the Affairs of All Foreign Countries) submitted a memorial to the Qing court, stating:

“From now on, whenever incidents involving the demolition of churches or the killing of missionaries occur, if it is found that officials deliberately allowed such cases to escalate into major incidents, then this ministry shall evaluate the matter and submit it to the court for decisions on a case-by-case basis.

However, if the incident broke out suddenly and was beyond immediate control, the local officials shall be demoted by one rank but allowed to retain their posts, in accordance with the precedent for insufficient preventive measures.

If the failure lies in inadequate protection efforts, indicating ineffective handling, then officials should be demoted by two ranks while retaining their posts, in accordance with the precedent for lesser but still significant administrative faults. This is intended as a warning and deterrent.”

The Zongli Yamen also proposed that for future compensation of missionary cases, since local officials could not shirk responsibility for mishandling, in addition to assigning compensation to the relevant county or district, the governor-generals, governors, surveillance commissioners, circuit intendants, and prefectural and county magistrates should also repay compensation from public funds in installments over the years, according to their respective levels of responsibility, in order to serve as a lesson and cautionary measure.

On May 24, the Emperor Guangxu wrote in the imperial margin:

“Approved as proposed.”

  1. 總理衙門奏遵議教案處分摺 1896（廉立之、王守中1980：426-427）

  2. Regulations on the Reception of Missionaries by Local Officials (1899)

(Wang Tieya 1982: pp. 862–863; Tao Feiya & Li Qiang 2016; Li Yumin 2010: p. 213)

To reduce the pressure on the central Qing government caused by frequent missionary incidents (教案), in March 1899 the Qing court promulgated the “Five Articles on the Reception of Bishops and Missionaries by Local Officials.” These regulations stipulated that missionaries were to be received by government officials of corresponding rank, based on the missionaries’ ecclesiastical hierarchy.

The regulations were initiated to some extent by the Zongli Yamen and were not intended to bestow honorary titles on missionaries, but rather to require local officials to receive and handle missionaries according to a fixed protocol. The goal was to resolve missionary cases locally and prevent them from escalating to the national level, which often drew in foreign diplomatic pressure.

  1. 地方官府接待教士章程 1899（王鐵崖1982：862-863；陶飛亞、李強2016；李育民2010：213）

The “Regulations on the Reception of Missionaries by Local Officials” were jointly formulated by the Zongli Yamen and French Bishop Pierre Marie Alphonse Favier, approved by the French Minister to China, and ratified by imperial decree of the Qing Emperor. The key provisions were as follows:

Ecclesiastical and official ranks were aligned:

Bishops were to be treated on par with provincial governors and governor-generals (督撫);

Vicars and senior priests (acting vicars and grand vicars) were equivalent to circuit intendants (道);

Ordinary priests corresponded to prefectural and county officials (府、縣). All provincial officials—including governors, intendants, prefects, sub-prefects, and magistrates—were required to grant audiences to missionaries of equivalent rank and receive them with proper ceremonial respect.

Archbishops and bishops were to submit the names of the priests and details of the churches involved in any official matters to the governors or governor-generals for orderly reception and protocol compliance.

Archbishops or bishops residing outside provincial capitals were not required to travel long distances to meet with governors or governor-generals.

In cases where a new governor assumed office, or when a bishop or archbishop was newly appointed, or for New Year greetings and festivals, it was acceptable to send a letter or calling card as a form of courtesy.

The governors would reciprocate in like manner.

When new priests arrived to take office, they could only request to meet with local officials upon presenting a letter of introduction from their bishop.

In provinces where missionary cases (教案) occurred, the bishops or priests at the location of the incident could handle the matter through their foreign minister or consulate, either with the Zongli Yamen or with local officials. They were not required to negotiate directly with local authorities beforehand. Local officials were required to promptly cooperate and work out a settlement with them.

Local officials were to regularly instruct and restrain local residents, urging them to treat Christian converts equally and harmoniously, and not provoke conflict out of prejudice.

At the same time, bishops and priests were to encourage believers to cultivate virtue and good conduct, in order to preserve the reputation of the Church.

In cases where disputes arose between Christians and non-Christians, local officials were to handle them impartially, while missionaries were not to interfere or shield anyone, so as to promote peaceful coexistence between Christians and non-Christians.

However, in 1908, the Qing government abolished these regulations, citing widespread abuses by missionaries, who often overstepped their authority and acted as if they were high-ranking local officials, leading to numerous problems.

Social Hostility toward Christianity

  1. Anti-Christian Thought and Conflicts Between People and Missionaries (Missionary Cases)

(Xing Fuzeng, 2006: 241–257)

Since China’s full opening in 1860, anti-Christian sentiment became increasingly pervasive throughout the country, leading to the outbreak of nearly a thousand incidents of conflict between local populations and missionaries, commonly known as missionary cases (教案).

Why did the spread of Christianity in modern China provoke such intense resistance from the Chinese people? This question has long drawn significant interest from both Chinese and foreign scholars, giving rise to diverse debates and interpretations.

  1. 民間反教思想與民教衝突（教案）（邢福增2006：241-257）

Regarding the causes of the missionary cases (教案), the mainstream explanation in early mainland Chinese scholarship approached the issue by viewing Christianity as a tool of imperialist aggression against China. In other words, the essence of conflicts between the people and missionaries was seen as a thoroughly anti-foreign religious struggle.

According to the Chinese Communist revolutionary historical perspective, modern Chinese history is a history of imperialist invasion, with Western missionaries portrayed as the vanguard of imperialist aggression. The missionary enterprise, along with Chinese churches, was viewed as a mechanism of imperialist expansion into China.

Thus, in this view, the history of modern Chinese Christianity, like that of modern China more broadly, is defined by a central thread of invasion and resistance. The Chinese people’s opposition to foreign religions is therefore portrayed as a natural and justified expression of anti-imperialist struggle—a cause that was both legitimate and righteous.

Another perspective that has had an even broader impact than the “anti-foreign religion struggle” narrative is the view of traditional cultural anti-Christianism. American scholar Paul A. Cohen (柯保安), in his 1963 book China and Christianity: The Missionary Movement and the Growth of Chinese Antiforeignism, 1860–1870, pointed out that Christianity was rejected by Confucian tradition and regarded by many Chinese as heretical.

This is because traditional Chinese culture has long upheld a conceptual opposition between “orthodoxy” (正統) and “heresy” or “heterodoxy” (異端邪說). From the standpoint of Confucian orthodoxy, any belief system that deviates from its principles is labeled as “evil” or heretical. Therefore, as a non-orthodox belief system, Christianity inevitably encountered opposition from orthodox forces during its introduction and spread in China.

Cohen identifies the anti-Christian tradition that confronted Catholicism in the Ming and Qing periods and Protestant Christianity in the late Qing as a continuation and development of this anti-heterodoxy tradition.

Taiwanese scholar Lü Shiqiang, in his book The Reasons for Anti-Christian Sentiment Among Chinese Officials and Gentry (1860–1874), refutes Paul A. Cohen’s interpretation of an “anti-Christian tradition.” He argues that the content of intellectual and cultural traditions is highly abstract, and that to adequately explain Chinese resistance to Christianity, one cannot simply attribute it to cultural conflict. Instead, a deeper analysis is required to identify what exactly these so-called “conflicts” entailed.

According to Lü, the primary causes of conflict can be categorized into three main areas:

1.Mutual misunderstanding between Chinese society and Christian missionaries;

2.The missionary enterprise in China acquired traits of aggression;

3.Officials and gentry sought to protect their dignity and interests.

Lü emphasizes that the “aggressive traits” he refers to are not vague imperialist notions, but rather concrete issues such as:

•Disputes over the return of church properties,

•The behavior and attitudes of missionaries, and

•The use of military force by foreigners to protect missionaries.

Indeed, there are substantial differences and heterogeneities between Christian beliefs and Chinese culture in many respects. When Christianity spread in China, it inevitably encountered tensions with Confucian orthodoxy, often appearing incompatible. From the perspective of the Confucian scholarly elite (士大夫), it was natural to raise various intellectual objections and doubts about Christianity—what we call anti-Christian discourse—and this should be seen as a normal phenomenon in the cross-cultural transmission and exchange of religion.

The issue, however, is that anti-Christian sentiment does not necessarily or inevitably lead to conflict. The gap between resistance to Christianity and the actual outbreak of conflict reveals the limitations of the “cultural conflict” explanation.

Therefore, in seeking to understand clashes between local populations and missionaries, we must carefully examine:

•The specific conditions under which Christianity (especially missionaries and converts) existed,

•The forms of tension that emerged with local officials, gentry, and the populace,

•And how these tensions eventually led to missionary cases (教案).

It is not sufficient to reduce all conflicts to anti-imperialist struggles or differences in Eastern and Western cultural traditions in a generalized and abstract way.

In addition, some scholars studying the missionary cases emphasize that the outbreak of conflict was due to multiple causes, and that in many instances, “nonreligious factors”—such as debt disputes, robbery, and land conflicts—were often more significant than the so-called “religious factors” (i.e., the cultural differences between Christianity and Chinese tradition).

Practical and mundane tensions in the everyday lives of Christian and non-Christian locals often provide a more compelling explanation for the core of the missionary cases than broad appeals to the idea of “heresy.” Among these, the conflicts over the allocation of local levies and taxes, especially between Christian converts and other members of the community, were a particularly important cause of friction and a major source of local Christian–non-Christian conflict.

Moreover, the negative impact of the “treaty system” on the protection of missionary activities—along with the tensions that arose when some missionaries exercised these privileges in their interactions with local elites and the general population—also formed a critical backdrop to the outbreak of missionary cases.

In particular, the “politics of evangelism” that emerged from the missionary protection policies of Catholic countries (such as France) undoubtedly played a significant role in intensifying the conflicts and antagonism between Christians and non-Christians.

Taiwanese scholar Chen Yinkun, using a quantitative historical approach, analyzed over 800 missionary cases that occurred between 1860 and 1899, and categorized their causes into seven main types, with the following proportions:

1.Differences in values – 5.96%

2.National security issues – 7.68%

3.Social order issues – 39.19%

4.Corrupt habits of officials and gentry – 3.03%

5.Conflicts over material interests – 22.32%

6.Incidental or accidental events – 5.15%

7.Others – 16.67%

This data clearly shows that issues related to social order and material interests together accounted for more than 60% of the overall conflicts between local populations and Christians.

  1. Timothy Richard and John Wherry’s Proposal on Missionary Affairs in China (1895)

  2. 李提摩太、惠志道條陳中國教務 1895（王彥威1963：281-285）

(Wang Yanwei, 1963: 281–285)

In 1895, Timothy Richard of the British Baptist Missionary Society, along with John Wherry of the American Presbyterian Mission (North) and 18 other missionaries, submitted a memorial to the Qing court titled “Petition to Sustain Missionary Work”.

In it, they argued that, throughout the world, “government and religion arise together and reinforce each other”, and that if the court could establish appropriate regulations for the Church, there would be no cause for conflict among the populace.

However, since the Tianjin Missionary Case, similar incidents had erupted across the country. The missionaries attributed these disturbances to “lawless individuals who fabricate absurd and groundless rumors to incite trouble.”

Thus, they respectfully appealed to the emperor’s benevolence, requesting that the Zongli Yamen (Office for the Management of the Affairs of All Foreign Countries) be ordered to jointly consult with church representatives to swiftly draft regulations for the protection of the Church, thereby achieving peaceful coexistence between Christians and non-Christians.

In his memorial, Timothy Richard explicitly pointed out that recently published works such as the Jingshi Wen Xubian (《經世文續編》) and the Illustrated Gazetteer of the Maritime Countries (Haiguo Tuzhi《海國圖志》) contain language that slanders the Church and falsely accuses Christian converts, with page after page filled with such defamatory content. In contrast, the benevolent deeds performed by the Church are never mentioned; and even when they are, the tone is such that they are veiled with suspicion and insinuation about the sinister intentions of Westerners.

He noted that since these publications were being printed and distributed across the provinces, some mischief-makers have drawn on them to fabricate vulgar and inflammatory rumors, which in turn has aroused fear and suspicion among the people toward Christians, ultimately leading to the outbreak of missionary cases.

In the memorial, Richard reaffirmed that:

“The Church’s mission and rules are grounded in the New Testament, which records the teachings passed down by the Savior Jesus and his close disciples. Its core message is this: God reigns over all nations, and He loves all people as a father loves his children. Beneath His sheltering grace, all are spiritual siblings. The foundation of a moral life lies in cultivating loyalty and filial piety. Believers must strive to follow the example of the Savior Jesus and emulate God’s compassionate heart, which cannot bear to see humanity fall into sin and suffering. The Church aspires to transform conflict into humility, evil into good, ignorance into wisdom, poverty into wealth—so that people not only enjoy peace and order in this world but also receive the grace of eternal life.”

In addition:

“Every seven days, believers gather in one place to proclaim the heavenly Way, to guide people in trusting God’s blessings and in overcoming selfish desires. They treat all races—whether yellow, white, or black—as brothers. In terms of care, the Church engages in activities such as medicine, elderly care, and orphan care. In terms of education, there are academies and charitable schools. All endeavors that are beneficial to humanity are passed on in an orderly fashion, aiming to promote the good and reform the bad, not only for the benefit of one nation, but also for all nations, and especially to support weaker countries.”

Furthermore, the memorial reaffirmed the strictness and clarity of Church regulations, stating:

“All who are given to drunkenness, deceit, gambling, or licentious behavior are strictly prohibited from entering the Church. If immoral individuals infiltrate, their presence will eventually come to light. Once discovered, they will be expelled, and only upon sincere repentance will they be readmitted.”

It then went on to cite examples of how Christianity has transformed hearts and minds in other countries as evidence of its positive influence.

The memorial further emphasized that missionaries coming to China have:

•Translated Western classics, scientific works, and mechanical texts into Chinese,

•Rendered Chinese classics into Western languages,

•Established schools and academies to teach Chinese people useful Western knowledge, all while not neglecting the Four Books and Five Classics of Chinese tradition.

In ports and inland cities, missionaries have also:

•Founded hospitals, providing exceptionally skilled medical treatment,

•Provided relief during times of disaster and famine, sparing no effort in their charitable endeavors.

The memorial also stated:

“Since missionaries understand the causes of national prosperity and decline across the world, they have, during their years in China, offered repeated and earnest advice. Had their counsel been adopted earlier, China might have avoided the severe hardships it faces today. Even now, if China were to rise and take action, missionaries still possess methods that can bring relief.”

From this, it is clear that:

“Government and religion support one another. Wherever orthodox religion flourishes, that nation prospers; wherever true religion fails to take root, that nation declines.”

Finally, Timothy Richard and others put forward three suggestions:

1.All slanderous content against the Church found in publications such as the Illustrated Gazetteer of the Maritime Countries (《海國圖志》), the Supplement to Statecraft Writings (《經世文續篇》), and other defamatory books targeting Christianity, should be entirely banned.

2.It should be publicly declared that since missionaries are engaged in beneficial work for China, the Chinese people must not regard them as heretical or evil, and whether officials or commoners, anyone who wishes to convert should be free to do so, without coercion to violate Church teachings. All those who sincerely seek to embrace the faith should be allowed to do so without discrimination.

3.Since missionaries have come to China to promote its well-being, local officials and gentry should be instructed to study and adopt effective educational and philanthropic practices from various countries. Anything beneficial to the people, whether foreign or domestic, should be jointly discussed and implemented harmoniously.

If these three recommendations are enacted, then “missionary cases can be permanently put to rest” and “China’s afflictions will be forever removed.”

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

  1. The Opening of China

  2. 中國開門（郭廷以1983：37-73）

(Kuo Ting-yee, 1983: 37–73)

Since the early modern era, China upheld a Sinocentric worldview, positioning itself as the center of global order through the tributary system. During the Qianlong reign (1759), foreign trade was restricted to the single port of Guangzhou, and the Governor-General of Guangdong and Guangxi implemented the strict “Regulations for Guarding Against Foreign Barbarians”.

During the Qianlong and Jiaqing periods, Britain sent two diplomatic missions to China, requesting the relaxation of trade restrictions. However, due to the rigid imperial system and disputes over ritual protocols, such as the requirement to kowtow before the emperor, both missions failed.

Entering the 19th century, Britain became increasingly determined to change the unequal terms of Sino-British trade. In 1834, the British Parliament abolished the East India Company’s monopoly on China trade and established the office of Superintendent of Trade in China.

From that point on, Sino-British relations shifted from being conducted through the East India Company to being managed directly by the British government. While the East India Company had primarily pursued commercial interests, the new arrangement—under the British government and the trade superintendent—now also had to account for national status and diplomatic representation.

In effect, the superintendent functioned as a de facto diplomat, sent by the British government to China. In short, Sino-British relations had become political, evolving from a limited engagement to a broader and more comprehensive relationship.

The first Superintendent of Trade in China, William J. Napier, arrived in Macau in July 1834 and immediately entered into a dispute with Governor-General Lu Kun of Guangdong and Guangxi over matters of diplomatic protocol. Napier advocated for the use of military force, which brought Sino-British relations to an impasse. Although tensions later eased somewhat, the British determination to change the status quo stood in stark contrast to the Qing Empire’s continued reliance on its traditional system, revealing a deep structural gap between the two sides.

In 1836, Charles Elliot succeeded Napier as the new Superintendent. Relations between China and Britain further deteriorated over the issue of opium prohibition.

In reality, Britain primarily exported wool and cotton textiles to China, but these goods were not well received by the Chinese market. In contrast, China’s exports to Britain—tea, silk, and porcelain—were extremely popular, leading to a large inflow of silver into China.

However, as British opium traders began smuggling opium into China, the trade imbalance was reversed, causing a massive outflow of silver from China. Ten percent of the annual revenue of the British colonial government in India came from opium sales to China.

This outflow of silver seriously affected the value of copper coinage in China. While copper coins were the legal tender for everyday transactions, silver was the unit for state revenue and tax collection. As silver became more expensive, copper currency depreciated, increasing the tax burden on the population.

Thus, Lin Zexu’s anti-opium campaign directly disrupted the triangular trade between China, Britain, and British India, further intensifying the already strained Sino-British relations.

The Qing dynasty’s anti-opium measures not only directly damaged British economic interests, but were also perceived as a humiliation to British nationals in China. Many opium traders demanded that Britain protect its interests and citizens in China, and calls for military action grew increasingly strong in British public opinion.

British Foreign Secretary Lord Palmerston reported to Parliament the objectives of launching a military expedition against China, gaining the support of MPs who argued that:

The national flag had been insulted, British commercial interests had suffered, and the lives and property of British nationals were under threat—thus, firm retaliation was necessary.

However, some members of Parliament opposed going to war over the immoral opium trade. Nevertheless, in April 1840, the motion to authorize military action passed by a narrow margin—271 votes in favor, 262 against—granting the government the authority to go to war with China.

In June 1840, Britain mobilized warships, steamers, and both naval and land forces from the Cape of Good Hope and India to the coastal waters of Guangdong, where they blockaded the Pearl River estuary, occupied the Zhoushan Islands, and then advanced north toward Tianjin, threatening Dagu Fort.

Negotiations between China and Britain failed, and in January 1841, British forces attacked Humen (the Bocca Tigris), compelling Qishan, the Governor-General of Zhili, to sign the “Chuanbi Preliminary Agreement” with Charles Elliot.

However, as the Qing court was dissatisfied with the terms of the agreement, hostilities resumed and British forces launched another assault on Humen.

At this point, the British government replaced Elliot with Henry Pottinger as plenipotentiary. Pottinger led new offensives, capturing coastal cities such as Xiamen and Ningbo, and in August 1842, advanced along the Yangtze River toward Nanjing.

With British troops at the gates of Nanjing, the Qing government was forced to sue for peace.

On August 29, 1842, both sides signed the Treaty of Nanjing, officially ending the war. According to the treaty, China was required to:

•Open five treaty ports—Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai—to foreign trade, and permit British nationals to reside there.

•Cede Hong Kong Island to Britain.

•Pay an indemnity of 21 million taels of silver, including:

•6 million for opium,

•3 million for debts owed to British merchants, and

•12 million for military expenses.

In July 1843, China and Britain signed the Five-Port Commerce Regulations, and in October, followed up with supplementary provisions (known as the Treaty of the Bogue). These agreements granted:

•Most-favored-nation (MFN) status to Britain—stipulating that any new privileges granted by the Qing to other nations must also be extended to Britain;

•Consular jurisdiction (extraterritoriality);

•And fixed tariff arrangements.

In the past, China held complete control over relations with the West, but going forward, foreign powers would dominate every aspect. Although Britain claimed to be seeking equality, it ultimately imposed a system that was unequal and unfavorable to China.

  1. The Lifting of the Ban on Christianity Under the Treaty System

  2. 條約制度下基督教的弛禁（楊大春2004：12-19、24-27）

(Yang Dachun, 2004: 12–19, 24–27)

The Qing dynasty’s prohibition of Christianity originated with Kangxi’s 1729 edict, issued in response to the Rites Controversy, which declared:

“Henceforth, Westerners need not continue missionary work in China; it is sufficient to prohibit it.”

Subsequently, both the Yongzheng and Jiaqing emperors also issued orders to suppress Catholicism.

However, following the Opium War, neither the Sino-British Treaty of Nanjing nor the Treaty of the Bogue (Humen Treaty) explicitly addressed the issue of lifting the ban on missionary activities.

After the opening of the five treaty ports, missionaries—being British nationals—could legally enter China. Nevertheless, the imperial ban on Western missionary preaching technically remained in effect.

The first mention of Christianity in a Sino-foreign treaty appeared in the Sino-American Treaty of Wangxia, signed in July 1844. Article 17 stipulated that:

“Citizens of the United States, whether engaging in trade, residing long-term, or staying temporarily in the five treaty ports, shall be permitted to rent houses or lease land to construct buildings, including hospitals, places of worship, and burial grounds.”

However, this clause only granted foreigners the right to build churches in the five treaty ports. It did not explicitly authorize Chinese nationals to adopt or practice Western religions.

In 1844, French King Louis Philippe decided to send Théodose de Lagrené as a special plenipotentiary envoy to China to negotiate with the Qing government. In October, China and France signed the Treaty of Whampoa, in which Article 22 stipulated:

“French nationals shall likewise be permitted to build churches, hospitals, charitable relief centers, schools, and cemeteries. If any Chinese person damages or violates a French church or cemetery, local officials must promptly arrest and severely punish the offender.”

Compared to the Sino-American Treaty of Wangxia, the Sino-French treaty added explicit protections for places of worship, but still did not clearly authorize Chinese nationals to convert to Western religions.

Nevertheless, Lagrené continued negotiations with Qiying, the Governor-General of Guangdong and Guangxi, regarding Church rights. Finally, in December 1844, the Qing court issued an imperial edict lifting the ban on Catholicism, stating:

“The French envoy respectfully requests the lifting of the ban on Catholicism… Those who practice the religion for the sake of doing good shall be exempt from punishment, but those who stir up trouble or commit wrongdoing shall still be punished accordingly. As for foreigners preaching the faith, they remain prohibited from entering the interior without permission.”

This imperial edict marked the first official recognition of Chinese citizens’ legal right to practice Catholicism.

However, the imperial edict only addressed Catholicism and did not mention Protestantism (referred to at the time as Jesus Religion, 耶穌教). In response, William J. Boone of the American Episcopal Church and Walter H. Medhurst of the London Missionary Society approached Théodose de Lagrené, asking him to petition the Qing government to extend the lifting of the ban to include Protestantism as well.

In December, Lagrené conveyed this request to Qiying in Guangzhou, and the American consul also expressed support for Lagrené’s efforts.

That same month, Qiying replied to Lagrené in an official communication:

“This minister was previously unaware of the differences among the doctrines practiced by various nations. Now that I have come to understand more, I hereby declare again: Whether Catholics worship with or without the image of the cross, as long as the faith is practiced in the pursuit of goodness, there shall be no prohibition upon Chinese subjects.

As for the differences or similarities in religious practices, there is absolutely no justification for selective rejection.”

On December 22, the Qing court formally announced this communication, thereby granting Protestantism and Eastern Orthodoxy the same legal status and rights as Catholicism.

However, Lagrené still hoped that Catholic churches confiscated or occupied during the ban could be returned to the Church. Finally, in February 1846, the Qing court issued an imperial edict, stating:

“As for all Catholic churches built in various provinces during the Kangxi reign, those that have already been converted into temples or private residences need not be investigated. But for buildings that can be verified as original church properties, they are to be returned to the local Catholic communities.”

However, since foreign treaties at this stage only opened five ports, this lifting of the ban did not yet apply to the inland regions beyond those five treaty ports.

China’s full opening to the outside world did not occur until after the Second Opium War (Anglo-French Expedition) with the signing of the Treaty of Tientsin in 1858 and the Convention of Peking in 1860.

According to Article 8 of the Sino-British Treaty of Tientsin, signed in June 1858:

“The Christian religion, including Protestantism and Catholicism, is a doctrine of goodness that teaches mutual respect. From now on, all who preach or study the faith shall be equally protected. As long as they behave properly and commit no wrongdoing, Chinese officials must not oppress or obstruct them in any way.”

Similarly, Article 13 of the Sino-French Treaty of Tientsin states:

“Catholicism is fundamentally a religion that encourages moral conduct. All those who follow the faith shall be fully protected in their persons and property. Religious activities such as gathering for worship and reciting scripture shall be freely permitted.

Missionaries who hold properly sealed permits under Article 8 may enter the interior to preach, and local officials must treat them with respect and provide protection.

Any Chinese person who wishes to believe in and revere Catholicism, and who follows its rules and behaves properly, shall face no investigation or punishment.

All previous written or printed prohibitions against Catholicism, regardless of origin or location, shall be completely repealed.”

This clearly shows that provisions for the protection of missionary activity were officially established in the Treaty of Tientsin.

Article 6 of the Sino-French Convention of Peking, signed in October 1860, further stipulated:

“In accordance with the imperial edict of the 25th day of the first month of Daoguang 26 (February 15, 1846), a proclamation shall be issued to inform all people throughout the empire that civilians and military personnel in all regions are permitted to practice and preach Catholicism, assemble for worship, and build churches.

Those who illegally arrest or harass believers shall be punished accordingly.

In addition, churches, schools, cemeteries, farmland, and buildings that were confiscated during previous persecutions are to be returned, and handed over to the French imperial envoy stationed in Beijing, who will then transfer them to local Catholic communities.

French missionaries shall also be allowed to freely lease or purchase land in the provinces and construct buildings at their discretion.”

However, the clause “French missionaries shall also be allowed to freely lease or purchase land in the provinces and construct buildings at their discretion” did not appear in the original French version of the treaty. It was unilaterally added to the Chinese version by French missionary Joseph M. Mouly of the Missions Étrangères de Paris, who was involved in the Chinese translation.

  1. Western Power, Western Tide, Western Learning, and Western Religion
  1. 西力 、西潮、西學與西教（梁元生1978：1-7）

(Leung Yuen-sang, 1978: 1–7)

Since the mid-19th century, China faced tremendous impact and challenges from the West. Li Hongzhang described this as a “great transformation unprecedented in a thousand years.” Undoubtedly, this “great transformation” was closely linked to the westward expansion of Western power.

The Qing dynasty was unable to withstand the invasions marked by Western military superiority—‘strong ships and powerful cannons’— and, after its defeats in the two Opium Wars, was forced to open up to the outside world.

As China’s doors opened, a wave of Western culture flooded in, and Western religion (“Western teachings”) arrived alongside Western military power and cultural currents.

How to respond to Western power, Western learning, and Western religion became a central issue in 19th-century China, shaping political, social, economic, intellectual, cultural, and religious developments.

In response to the “great transformation,” some conservative officials and Confucian elites remained committed to traditional values, striving to defend the existing political system and social norms. However, some more perceptive individuals had already recognized that change was inevitable.

In the 1860s, the Qing court launched the Self-Strengthening Movement, with the goal of “strengthening China by adopting Western techniques.” This involved accepting Western military and technological advancements—“strong ships and powerful cannons”—at the technological level. The movement also promoted the idea of “Chinese learning as essence, Western learning for application” (zhongxue wei ti, xixue wei yong), seeking to learn from the West while upholding traditional Confucian values.

This division between essence and function revealed a continued belief that Chinese learning was fundamental, while Western knowledge was peripheral—useful, but not essential.

Yet, a minority of intellectuals had already perceived the superiority of the West at the institutional level. The crisis deepened after China’s defeat by Japan in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), which triggered a heightened sense of national peril. This led to the Hundred Days’ Reform of 1898, an ambitious attempt to comprehensively adopt Western institutions to save the country from collapse.

Unfortunately, due to strong resistance from conservative forces, the reform effort lasted only one hundred days and ended in failure.

For Western missionaries coming to China, they were deeply convinced that Christianity was the foundation of Western wealth and power, and that Western religion and Western learning were inseparable.

Most missionaries viewed China as a “pagan” or “heathen” nation, believing that only Christian civilization could bring salvation and transformation.

However, a smaller number of missionaries, witnessing the profound changes taking place in China, dedicated themselves to spreading Western learning, advocating for reform, and even promoting social transformation—hoping to facilitate the Westernization of China.

However, the spread of Christianity (“Western religion”) in Chinese society faced multiple challenges.

First, the treaty provisions protecting missionary activities gave Christianity in China an association with foreign aggression, embedding it with a perceived “invasive character.” This also led to the formation of a “protected Christian class” under missionary patronage, which, coupled with deep cultural and value differences between China and the West, resulted in the widespread circulation of anti-Christian sentiment in the 19th century and even sparked conflicts between local populations and Christians (known as missionary cases, or jiaoan).

Second, although Western learning—such as Western technology and political institutions—was increasingly accepted by officials and intellectuals, this did not necessarily translate into acceptance of Western religion.

Nevertheless, Christianity played an indispensable role in promoting Westernization, facilitating cultural exchange between China and the West, and contributing to China’s modernization process.

In the long run, as Chinese society became more open and increasingly Westernized, Christianity was able to develop and spread in a more tolerant and open environment.

  1. The Collapse of the Tributary System and the Partitioning of China by Foreign Powers

  2. 朝貢制度解體、列強瓜分勢力（郭廷以1983：217-227、239-252、265-298；Fairbank 1968）

(Kuo Ting-yee, 1983: 217–227, 239–252, 265–298; Fairbank, 1968)

Beginning in the 1860s, the Qing Empire was not only forced to enter the international system, but also faced growing ambitions from foreign powers to seize its neighboring tributary states, gradually realizing their goals of encroachment and annexation.

First, in 1871, Russia occupied Ili (Yili) under the pretext of suppressing the Muslim uprising in Xinjiang. After extensive negotiations between China and Russia, the two sides signed the Treaty of Ili in 1881, which redrew the border.

Following the Meiji Restoration, Japan refused to recognize the Ryukyu Kingdom as a Chinese tributary state. In 1875, it began the process of annexation, prohibited the kingdom from continuing its tributary missions to China, and in 1879, officially established Okinawa Prefecture.

In 1884, the Qing Empire, due to the Vietnam issue, went to war with France once again. As a result of its defeat in the Sino-French War, Vietnam was separated from China’s tributary system and became a French protectorate.

Soon after, in 1886, Britain declared Burma to be under British rule and began to advance into Tibet.

In the 1890s, Japan sought to seize Korea, a traditional tributary state of the Qing Empire. This ambition led to the First Sino-Japanese War in 1894, involving major naval and land battles.

The Self-Strengthening Movement, which had begun in 1860 and emphasized modernizing China’s military through shipbuilding and the manufacture of firearms and artillery, ultimately failed, culminating in defeat by Japan.

Under the Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895), China recognized Korea’s independence and ceded Taiwan to Japan. Although Japan originally intended to annex the Liaodong Peninsula, Russia, Germany, and France intervened diplomatically, and China was forced to pay an increased indemnity in order to recover the peninsula.

Following its defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895), the Qing Empire grew increasingly weak and unstable. Russia took advantage of this situation to expand its influence in Northeast China, pressuring China into signing an alliance treaty that granted Russia permission to build railways in Heilongjiang and Jilin, and to use those railways for military transport.

Russia’s actions accelerated the crisis of foreign powers carving up China.

In 1898, Britain demanded that the Yangtze River basin be recognized as within its sphere of influence, and leased the New Territories (adjacent to Hong Kong) and Weihaiwei.

Russia further extended its influence from the northeast into North China, obtaining leases for Port Arthur (Lüshun) and Dalian (then known as Talien or Daitō in Japanese and Dàtóng in Chinese sources).

France claimed the southwest region as its sphere of influence and leased Guangzhou Bay (Guangzhouwan).

Germany established dominance over Shandong Province, securing a lease for Jiaozhou Bay.

As foreign powers divided China into spheres of influence, Britain, in order to safeguard its own interests, proposed in 1898 to maintain China’s territorial integrity and ensure free trade. It also invited the United States to adopt a coordinated stance and to urge other powers to support the “Open Door” policy in China.

In September and November 1899, U.S. Secretary of State John Hay issued diplomatic notes to the major powers, proposing three key points:

1.No interference with commercial or investment activities within any country’s sphere of influence or leased territory in China.

2.The existing Chinese customs tariff rates should continue to apply uniformly to goods imported and exported through ports in all spheres, with the duties collected by the Chinese government.

3.In ports within each sphere of influence, no nation’s ships should be charged higher harbor dues than those of the controlling power; likewise, rail freight rates should be the same for all countries’ goods as for those of the host nation.

In March 1900, John Hay announced that all major powers had agreed to the Open Door Policy toward China.

However, this policy accepted the existence of spheres of influence and only called for equal trade opportunities and shared benefits. It did not prevent violations of China’s sovereignty or territorial integrity.

By the late 19th century, not only had the traditional “Chinese World Order”—which had once been established through the tributary system—neared complete collapse, but the Qing Empire itself had also fallen under the domination of foreign spheres of influence.

  1. The Formation of Modern Chinese Nationalism

  2. 中國近代民族主義之形成（王爾敏1977：209-230）

(Wang Erh-min, 1977: 209–230)

Traditional Chinese nationalism was characterized by a strong sense of cultural and ethnic identity. In contrast, modern Chinese nationalism emphasized the concept of sovereignty as a central tenet.

The emergence of sovereignty consciousness was primarily stimulated by the encroachment of Western powers. As early as 1864, Wang Tao proposed the goals of “securing economic rights” and “establishing national prestige.” By the 1890s, this had evolved into a demand for the recovery of “extraordinary rights”—that is, the special privileges granted to Western powers in China that infringed upon Chinese sovereignty.

Intellectuals such as Zheng Guanying, Huang Zunxian, Zeng Jize, and Ho Kai also responded to the unreasonable demands imposed on China by foreign powers, recognizing them as a violation of national dignity. This led to growing calls to defend sovereignty and reclaim economic and political rights.

Through this process, the traditional Sinocentric worldview of the “Celestial Empire” was gradually replaced by a modern, sovereign-state-based worldview, signaling the rise of modern Chinese nationalism rooted in the principle of national sovereignty.

The crisis consciousness and national awakening that emerged after China’s defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War in 1895 played a significant role in the rise of modern Chinese nationalism.

In the aftermath of the war, a wave of society and association formation swept across China. Organizations such as the Beijing Strengthening Society (Qiangxuehui), Shanghai Strengthening Society, and Changsha South Study Society promoted reform through lectures and newspaper publications. Their goals included not only the preservation and continuation of traditional ethnic and cultural identity, but also explicit calls to restore national sovereignty—as seen in groups like the National Rights Recovery Society, the Patriotic Society, and the Guangdong Railway Protection Society.

Among them, the Patriotic Society (Baoguo Hui), founded in Beijing in 1898, prominently advocated the goals of “protecting the nation, the race, and the religion.” Its mission statements also introduced key concepts such as “national territory,” “national sovereignty,” “citizenship,” “national religion,” and “national humiliation.”
