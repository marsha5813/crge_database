China: Agency (Pre-1900)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Mǎ Lǐsùn (Robert Morrison, 1782-1834) was the first modern Christian missionary to China, ordained as a minister in January 1807 in London. He was sent to China by the London Missionary Society and arrived in Macao and Guangzhou in September. Due to the Qing government’s enforcement of anti-religious policies at the time, along with the strict “anti-barbarian” regulations in the single-port trade system of Guangzhou, Mǎ Lǐsùn integrated into the British East India Company in 1809 as a translator to obtain legal residency. He indicated in 1825 that the greatest difficulty in working in China was how to nestle in a distant land with a hostile pagan government, hostile Catholic clergy, and hostile Christian commercial companies. Specifically, he referred to the Qing government’s closed-door policy and anti-religious stance, the hostility from the Catholic Church in Macao towards Christianity, and the East India Company’s reluctance to allow missionary work to interfere with its commercial interests.

During his 25 years in China, Mǎ Lǐsùn mainly stayed in the front lines of Guangzhou and Macao, engaging in the translation of the Chinese Bible and other literary work alongside the heavy workload at the East India Company. In 1810, he completed “The True Text of the Acts of the Apostles for the Savior Jesus” and “The Gospel of St. Luke.” In 1813, he published “The New Testament Epistles” and completed the translation of the Old Testament in 1823, naming it “The Sacred Book of Heaven.” He also dedicated himself to literary efforts, publishing evangelical materials: “A Treatise on the Redemption of God” (1811), “A Question and Answer on the Teachings of Jesus” (1812), “The Fragments of the Heavenly Way” (1819), “Encouragement to Read the Sacred Record” (1821), and “The Teachings of the Ancient Saints Revealed by Heaven” (1832-33), among others. Additionally, he published Volume 1 of “A Dictionary of the Chinese Language” in 1815, completed Volume 2 five years later, and finished Volume 3 in 1822. “A Dictionary of the Chinese Language” was the first English-Chinese and Chinese-English dictionary and became an important tool for future missionaries learning Chinese.

William Milne (米憐) from the London Missionary Society arrived in China in 1813 but was constrained by anti-religious policies, primarily working in Malacca (馬六甲) far from China. The Malacca mission was under the “Ultra-Ganges Mission” established by Mǎ Lǐsùn, focusing on the founding of the Anglo-Chinese College (英華書院) in 1818 and the compilation and printing of Chinese evangelical pamphlets. Under the restrictions of “China’s closed door,” Malacca became a logistics base for evangelism to China.

In 1816, Sir William Pitt Amherst (阿美士德爵士) led a diplomatic mission to China to discuss Sino-British relations on behalf of the British government. The mission arrived in Guangzhou in July, and Mǎ Lǐsùn was invited to serve as a Chinese secretary, accompanying the team north. However, he ultimately did not meet the Jiaqing Emperor and returned empty-handed. Acting as the mission’s Chinese secretary, Mǎ Lǐsùn recorded the journey and experiences, publishing a summary in London in 1820 titled “Memoir of the Principal Occurrences During an Embassy from the British Government to the Court of China in the Year of 1816.” In the book, he pointed out that the principles of equality and reciprocity are the fundamental tenets of economic and various exchanges between nations. Nevertheless, from the protocol issues encountered during this mission, especially the dispute over the Chinese officials requiring the British envoy to perform the “three kneels and nine bows” ceremony, he strongly felt China’s arrogant attitude towards other countries. He even denounced the Chinese government as “military despotism” and referred to the Chinese people as “Barbarians.”

In July 1814, he baptized Chua Cheng, the first Chinese Christian to be baptized. By 1832, a total of 10 Chinese individuals had been baptized. Reflecting on his 15 years of work in China, facing the question of “What have you truly accomplished in promoting Christianity in China over the past 15 years?” he reaffirmed the achievements of his work in learning Chinese, translating the Bible, establishing a base in Malacca, and printing Chinese missionary literature.In 1834, the East India Company’s monopoly on trade with China expired, and Britain dispatched a commercial supervisor to China. In July, the first commercial supervisor, Lǜ Láobēi (William J. Napier), arrived in Macao. Mǎ Lǐsùn was appointed as his Chinese secretary and translator. However, after taking office, Mǎ’s health deteriorated, and he passed away in Guangzhou on the evening of August 1, being buried in Macao on August 5.

  1. Lǐ Yǎgè (James Legge, 1815-1897) was a missionary of the London Missionary Society who arrived in Malacca in 1840 and served as the principal of the Anglo-Chinese College. After the signing of the Sino-British Treaty of Nanking in 1842, Hong Kong became a British colony. In 1843, Lǐ Yǎgè relocated the Anglo-Chinese College to Hong Kong. After arriving in Hong Kong, he assisted in the establishment of the Union Chapel, the first church serving British expatriates from non-established church backgrounds.

From 1845 to 1848, Lǐ Yǎgè returned to England for medical treatment and to give lectures. He returned to Hong Kong in 1848 and remained there until 1873, during which he began his project of translating Confucian classics into English. As a missionary, Lǐ Yǎgè advocated for a deep understanding of Chinese culture, especially Confucianism, leading to an extensive project of translating Chinese classics into English. In 1861, the first volume of “Chinese Classics” was published, followed by the translation of works such as “The Analects,” “The Great Learning,” “The Doctrine of the Mean,” “The Mencius,” “The Spring and Autumn Annals,” “The Book of Rites,” “The Book of Documents,” “The Book of Filial Piety,” “The I Ching,” “The Book of Songs,” “The Tao Te Ching,” and “The Zhuangzi” into a total of eight volumes over the next 25 years. Through his in-depth study of Chinese culture, Lǐ Yǎgè established himself not only as a missionary but also as a sinologist. Notably, during this period, he received significant assistance from Wáng Tào (Wang Tao), who arrived in Hong Kong from Shanghai in 1863. In 1867, Lǐ returned to England to recuperate but soon arranged for Wáng Tào to go to England as well; by 1870, both returned to Hong Kong together. Lǐ’s travels in England undoubtedly had a significant impact on Wáng Tào’s thoughts.

Moreover, he held the position of educator. After relocating to Hong Kong, the Anglo-Chinese College, while primarily focused on theological education, also functioned as a preparatory school for theological training. However, in 1856, the Anglo-Chinese College closed due to unsatisfactory enrollment. After 1860, he participated in educational reforms in the Hong Kong colony. At his suggestion, the government established a Central College and implemented English education, with government schools abandoning their missionary functions to focus on nurturing talent.

In 1873, Lǐ Yǎgè left Hong Kong, and in 1875, he was appointed the first professor of Sinology at Oxford University. In 1877, during a conference of missionaries in China, he presented a paper titled “Confucian in its Relation to Christianity” (read on his behalf by Wù Wélián [William Muirhead]). Due to differing perspectives on the concept of “God” in Confucianism and Christianity, this paper sparked controversy, leading the conference to decide to omit it from the published records. Lǐ later self-published this paper in Shanghai and London.

Lǐ Yǎgè’s project of translating ancient Chinese classics had an influence that extended far beyond his missionary scope, serving as a bridge for communication between Eastern and Western cultures. The transmission of Chinese Confucian classics to the West significantly impacted Western philosophical, ethical, and literary thought. In 1870, Aberdeen University in Scotland awarded him an honorary Doctor of Laws degree. During multiple visits to Paris, he engaged in scholarly exchanges with the renowned French sinologist Jū Lián (Stanislas A. Julien) and, in 1876, received the First International Stanislas Julien Prize for Chinese Literature from the French Academy. In 1884, Lǐ Yǎgè was again awarded an honorary Doctor of Laws degree by the University of Edinburgh.

On November 29, 1897, while lecturing at Oxford, Lǐ Yǎgè suddenly suffered a stroke and, despite attempts at resuscitation, passed away at the age of 82.(3) Hè Jìnshàn (Ho Tsun-sheen, 1817-1871), also known as Fútáng, was a native of Nanhai, Guangdong. His father moved to Southeast Asia, specifically Malacca, to make a living, working as a woodblock carver at the London Mission’s printing house. After arriving in Malacca, Hè joined the church with his father. Due to his older age, he was granted special status to study at the Anglo-Chinese College and began learning English. In 1838, he was baptized by John Evans from the Anglo-Chinese College. Later, he went to Calcutta to attend the Anglican training school (Bishop’s College) alongside Evans’ son.

After the arrival of Lǐ Yǎgè (James Legge) in Malacca in 1840, a close relationship developed between him and Hè Jìnshàn. In 1843, Hè followed Lǐ to Hong Kong, where he began teaching at the Anglo-Chinese College and served as a local missionary. At that time, Hè was already capable of reading the original Scriptures in Hebrew and Greek. Besides serving as a translator for Lǐ’s Sunday worship, he assisted with the Chinese gatherings at Union Chapel. Due to his commendable performance in various aspects, Hè Jìnshàn was ordained as a pastor in Hong Kong in 1846, becoming the first Chinese pastor to be ordained in Hong Kong.

Like Lǐ Yǎgè, Hè Jìnshān was dedicated to exploring the relationship between Confucianism and Christianity, identifying their commonalities and advocating for the use of “God” as the Chinese translation of the Holy Name. He authored works such as “The Interpretation of the Ten Commandments,” “Exegesis of the Bible,” “Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew” (1854), and “Commentary on the Gospel of Mark” (1856). L. Pfister described him as the first Chinese theologian within Protestant Christianity.

In addition to his work in Hong Kong, Hè also assisted the London Mission in missionary efforts in the inland regions of Guangdong, later becoming the pastor of the London Mission in Foshan. In 1870, the church in Foshan was completed, but later faced strong anti-Christian sentiments from the local populace, leading to its disruption, prompting Hè to retreat to Hong Kong. After returning, he suffered a stroke and eventually passed away in Guangzhou in 1871.

Hè Jìnshàn’s son, Hè Qǐ, was a polymath, embodying the roles of thinker, lawyer, physician, educator, philanthropist, and political reformer, and can be considered a pioneer of modernization in China.

  1. Dài Deshēng (James Hudson Taylor, 1832-1905) was born in Yorkshire, England, and from a young age had a desire to engage in overseas missionary work, inspired by reading W. H. Medhurst’s writings about China (China: Its State and Prospects, with Special Reference to the Spread of the Gospel). This inspired him to pursue medical studies as a means to serve as a medical missionary. In 1853, the Chinese Evangelization Society accepted his application, and he arrived in Shanghai in March 1854 to study Chinese. He initially conducted itinerant evangelism near Shanghai and later ventured multiple times into the interior. In 1856, he was in Ningbo for missionary work and married local missionary María Dyer. Due to long-term fatigue, he wrote during his recuperation: “If I had a thousand pounds sterling, China could have all of it; if I had a thousand lives, not one would I keep for myself, all should go to China. No, not China, but Christ. Is that too much for such a Savior as He?” In May 1857, he left the Chinese Evangelization Society.

In 1860, he returned to England for health reasons. During this time, he became a member of the Royal College of Surgeons and obtained a midwifery certificate. He also completed a collection of hymns in Ningbo dialect and translated and revised the Romanized New Testament in Ningbo dialect. By the end of 1864, Dài Deshēng finished “China: Its Spiritual Need and Claims,” indicating a decrease in the number of British missionary societies in China from 115 to 91 and noting that all were concentrated in five coastal treaty cities, with none in the interior. Consequently, he founded the China Inland Mission in 1865. In May 1866, Dài and his wife, with their four children and 16 other missionaries, set out for China, arriving in Shanghai in September.

From 1868 to 1871, Dài worked in missionary activities around Zhenjiang and Yangzhou. In 1868, due to rumors targeting a nursery, the Yangzhou Mission faced public unrest, an event referred to as the “Yangzhou Church Incident.”After the conflict, Dài Deshēng established a policy for the China Inland Mission not to seek compensation after church incidents, aiming to avoid escalating tensions between local communities and churches. In 1871, his three young children and his beloved wife María passed away. Dài returned to England, where he married another female missionary in November. He re-entered China in November 1872. From 1865 to 1875, the China Inland Mission had established bases in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, and Jiangxi.

In 1874, Dài returned to England again for health reasons and returned to China in September 1876. Subsequently, the China Inland Mission expanded deeper into the interior provinces and even established churches in Yunnan. In 1879, while recuperating in Yantai, Shandong, he purchased land to establish the “Zhifu School” to provide education for the children of the Mission’s missionaries. In March 1883, he returned to England and called 76 individuals to come to China for missionary work. By 1885, the Mission had entered nine inland provinces. In June 1886, Dài gathered co-workers in Shanghai to pray for the purchase of land to establish the headquarters of the China Inland Mission. In February 1890, the headquarters was completed. In January 1887, he returned to England and called 102 young people to engage in missionary work in China. Later, he traveled to the United States to establish a North American branch of the Mission. By 1893, this branch had sent a total of 39 missionaries to China. Subsequently, the Mission also recruited missionaries from Northern Europe and Australia. By 1895, the China Inland Mission had a total of 604 missionaries and 417 Chinese co-workers.

In 1895, Dài appointed the young Gù Zhèngdào (William Cooper) as the assistant director and, the following year, handed over all affairs to him before returning to England to continue spreading the Mission’s vision. He returned to China in January 1898. In September 1899, he traveled to Australia again. In April 1900, he led missionary gatherings in New York and Boston. In June 1900, he and his wife went to Switzerland for rest; not long after, news of the Boxer Rebellion reached him, revealing that over a hundred missionaries and their families from the China Inland Mission had been martyred, which caused him profound distress.

In January 1901, he appointed Hè Sīdé (Dixon E. Hoste) as the acting director, and in November 1902, he resigned as the director. Hè took up the position as the director of the China Inland Mission in January 1903. In February 1905, he returned to China, arriving in Shanghai in April and then visiting co-workers in Henan. On June 1, he arrived in Changsha, where he passed away two days later.

Dài Deshēng’s son, Dài Yǒngmiǎn (James II), grandson Dài Shàozēn (James II), and great-grandson Dài Jìzōng (James IV) all dedicated themselves to missionary work among the Chinese.

  1. Dīng Wéiliáng (W. A. P. Martin, 1827-1916) was from Indiana, USA, and his father was a Presbyterian missionary to Africa. After graduating from New Albany Theological Seminary in 1849, he was ordained as a pastor and sent to China. In November of the same year, he departed for China with his newlywed wife. In April 1850, they arrived in Hong Kong and then moved to Ningbo, where he began learning the Ningbo dialect using a phonetic approach. In January 1851, he collaborated with others to develop a phonetic system for writing in “Ningbo Hua” using the Latin alphabet. In February, he opened a boys’ school in Ningbo and began translating the Bible into the local dialect.

During the Taiping Rebellion, Dīng expressed sympathy and support for its Christian elements. In 1856 and 1857, he wrote three letters to U.S. Attorney General Caleb Cushing, urging the United States to recognize the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom, which were published in the North China Herald but did not receive a response. Being proficient in Chinese, he served as an interpreter for U.S. Minister William B. Reed during negotiations between China and the U.S. in 1858, drafting the Treaty of Tientsin.

During his leave back in the U.S. from 1860 to 1862, Dīng traveled and spoke extensively, calling for more people to pay attention to the mission work in China. In July 1861, he was awarded an honorary degree by Lafayette College. Reflecting on his ten years of work in Ningbo, he recognized the importance of education. Upon returning to China, he moved to Beijing and founded a day school (the predecessor of Chongshi School), serving as its principal.At the same time, feeling that the Qing court was unfamiliar with international law, Dīng Wéiliáng began translating “Elements of International Law” by American author Henry Wheaton. This initiative received encouragement from the U.S. Minister to China, Anson Burlingame, and the Chief Commissioner of Customs, Robert Hart, as well as support from the Qing government’s Board of Ceremonies, which sent personnel to assist in refining the Chinese text. After completion, it was published by the Board of Ceremonies.

In March 1865, based on the recommendations of Anson Burlingame and Thomas Wade from the British Embassy, Dīng Wéiliáng was appointed as an instructor at the Tongwen Guan (School of Combined Learning) in the capital, teaching English. In 1867, the school decided to hire Dīng to establish a course on international law. In November 1869, he was appointed head instructor of the Tongwen Guan. Founded by the Qing government during the Self-Strengthening Movement, the Tongwen Guan was a new-style school where all instructors were foreigners, specializing in training diplomats and translators. Dīng Wéiliáng formally began his appointment on November 26, 1869, and on December 1 of the same year, he officially submitted his resignation to the Presbyterian Mission. He believed it was a great opportunity to continue his missionary work as, at Tongwen Guan, he could influence more future Chinese leaders to reduce their resistance to Christianity and even not oppose it.

The Tongwen Guan also translated and published modern Western works, including “Elements of International Law” (translated by Dīng Wéiliáng), “Introduction to Natural Philosophy” (by Dīng Wéiliáng), “A Guide to Chemistry” (translated by Anatole Billequin), “French Legislation” (translated by Billequin), and “The Wealth of Nations” (translated by Wāng Fèngzǎo), among others. In 1885 (the 11th year of Guangxu), the Qing government awarded him the title of a third-rank official. Dīng held the position of head instructor for 25 years, and combined with his earlier roles teaching English and international law, he served a total of 30 years at the Tongwen Guan.

In May 1894, due to health reasons, Dīng Wéiliáng resigned as head instructor of the Tongwen Guan and returned to the U.S. for treatment. In January 1897, at the age of 70, he returned to Beijing. At that time, the nation was reeling from its defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War, with reformists urging the Qing government to implement changes. After staying in the U.S. for over a year, he returned to Beijing in September 1901. In 1902, the Qing government ordered the revival of Peking University, and Empress Dowager Cixi appointed Zhang Baixi as the concurrent president of the University, continuing to employ Dīng Wéiliáng as the head instructor. Later, Dīng assisted Zhang Zhidong in organizing a university in Wuchang and returned to the U.S. in 1905.

On his 80th birthday, he received a banner from U.S. President Woodrow Wilson and over 60 educational figures in the U.S., honoring his significant achievements in China.

In a voting event for the best Chinese work held by the Guangxue Society commemorating the centenary of Christianity in China in 1907, “The Source of Heavenly Principles” was chosen as the best Chinese work. Additionally, he authored “Introduction to Natural Philosophy,” “The Bright Star’s Guidance,” “A Synopsis of International Law,” “The Method of Enrichment,” “Investigations into Western Studies,” “Outlines of Sexual Studies,” and “The Harmony of Heavenly Principles,” among others. From 1872 to 1875, he edited “The Chronicles of East and West,” primarily publishing work on astronomy, geography, and natural philosophy. His English works mainly include “Recollections of a Cycle” (The Cycle of Cathay, 1896), “Essence of Sinology” (The Lore of Cathay, 1901), and “The Awakening of China” (The Awakening of China, 1907).

In 1870, New York University awarded him an honorary Doctor of Laws degree. In 1899, Princeton University also conferred upon Dīng Wéiliáng an honorary Doctor of Laws degree.   1. Medical Missionary Society in China, 1838 (Choa 1990; Cohen 1978: 574-576; Tiedemann 2010: 436-445; 雷祥麟 (Lei Xianglin) 2024: 78-79; 李傳斌 (Li Chuanbin) 2003: 34-38)

The Medical Missionary Society in China was founded in February 1838 by 伯駕 (Peter Parker, American Board), 郭雷樞 (Thomas Colledge, British East India Company), and 裨治文 (Elijah Bridgeman, American Board) in Guangzhou. This organization marked the beginning of modern medical missions in China, signifying the first formal introduction of Western medicine to the country and initiating the integration of the Christian missionary movement with modern medicine. The philosophy behind medical missions was to heal the body in order to save the soul, aiming to eliminate the Chinese people’s xenophobic mentality through healing and thus gain respect to facilitate the dissemination of the gospel; on the other hand, it sought to break superstitions and ignorance through scientific reasoning, ultimately transforming the worldview of the Chinese people and removing barriers to spreading the gospel. In 1845, the Medical Missionary Society split into two, with some members establishing the Hong Kong Medical Missionary Society.

Christianity introduced Western medicine to China, yet in the 19th century, the representation of modern medicine in China was predominantly by Western missionaries, especially those focusing on curative medicine. Among them, surgical procedures, which could produce dramatic effects quickly, were emphasized. The visual impact of surgical treatments could swiftly attract patients and persuade the Chinese of the superiority of Western medicine, thereby enhancing the reputation of the missionaries. Similarly, ophthalmic surgeries and tooth extractions also had comparable dramatic effects.

However, for medical missionaries who received formal training in Eastern medicine, there was a tension in balancing their dual roles of providing medical care and engaging in missionary work. Even though patients benefited from the medical services provided by the missionaries, the proportion of conversions to Christianity was not significant. Additionally, anti-missionary rumors in the 19th century frequently targeted the medical work of missionaries.

In 1887, an international medical conference was scheduled to be held in Washington, D.C., and several medical missionaries in China planned to attend. Since the conference required participants to be formal members of a medical society, 嘉約翰 (John G. Kerr) of the Guangzhou Boji Hospital and 文恆理 (Henry Williams Boone), the director of the Shanghai Tongren Hospital, proposed reorganizing the existing Medical Missionary Society. In 1886, the Medical Missionary Association of China was established in Shanghai, with 嘉約翰 (John G. Kerr) serving as president and 文恆理 (Henry Williams Boone) as vice president. This was the first national association of medical missionaries in China, with aims that included: (1) disseminating Western medicine in China and enhancing communication among medical missionaries; (2) advancing the development of missionary and medical work; (3) strengthening solidarity among members to promote the growth of Western medicine in China. It is evident that while the association had religious motivations, it placed greater emphasis on improving the medical standards in China.They further required that medical missionaries coming to China must have recognized formal medical training. The early members were either foreign or overseas Chinese physicians, and it wasn’t until 1903 that local Chinese physicians were gradually accepted into the association. They translated medical texts, standardized Chinese medical terminology, published medical journals, promoted medical education and public health education, and dedicated efforts to advance the modernization of Chinese medicine.

In 1890, the Chinese Missionary Conference discussed issues regarding medical missions. There were traditional views at the conference that emphasized medical work as a means of evangelism, but there were also clear acknowledgments of the undeniable contradictions and tensions between medical care and missionary work. The medical work within the missionary efforts continued to develop throughout the 19th century. Between 1887 and 1890, there were 46 medical missionaries who came to China. By the end of the 19th century, sizable church hospitals were established in various regions, and there began to be a focus on medical education, training Chinese medical practitioners.

In 1925, the name of the China Medical Association was changed from the China Medical Missionary Society. In 1932, the China Medical Association merged with the National Medical Association to form the 中華醫學會 (Chinese Medical Association).

  1. Establishment of the U.S. and British Bible Society’s Branch in China (麦金华 (Mai Jinhua) 2010: 10-19)

In 1804, the British and Foreign Bible Society was established in London, England, as a non-denominational Christian organization aimed at promoting the dissemination of the Bible. With the development of overseas missionary movements, it also sought to support Bible translation efforts. As early as 1812, the society provided funding for 马礼遜 (Robert Morrison) to translate the Bible, marking the beginning of its work in China. Subsequently, various Chinese translations received funding from the society. In 1863, the British and Foreign Bible Society officially established a branch in China, managed by 偉烈亞力 (Alexander Wylie).

With the assistance of the British and Foreign Bible Society, the first American Bible society, the Philadelphia Bible Society, was established in 1808. Since then, many other Bible societies in American cities were also founded with support from the British and Foreign Bible Society. However, the aforementioned Bible societies primarily served their local cities, and the call for a national Bible society began to gain attention. In 1816, the American Bible Society was founded in New York. In 1832, the American Bible Society first allocated funds for the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions’ missionary, 裨治文 (E. C. Bridgeman), to distribute Bibles, marking the commencement of its work in China. In 1875, the American Bible Society established a branch in China.

In 1809, the National Bible Society of Scotland was founded, prompting the British and Foreign Bible Society to cease its involvement in Scottish missions. In 1863, the Scottish Bible Society appointed 韋廉臣 (Alexander Williamson) as its first manager in China and established a branch in China in 1877. Since then, the publication and distribution of the Bible in China entered an era of “three societies vying for dominance.”

In terms of distribution of Chinese Bibles, between 1879 and 1889, the British and Foreign Bible Society averaged 180,000 copies per year. The American Bible Society distributed over 250,000 copies in 1887. The Scottish Bible Society distributed about 200,000 copies on average per year from 1884 to 1894.

  1. China Inland Mission, 1865 (蔡錦圖 (Cai Jintu) 1998; Austin 2007)

The China Inland Mission (CIM) was founded in 1865 by British missionary 戴德生 (James Hudson Taylor) with the aim of bringing the gospel to the un-evangelized regions of inland China.

After China opened up in the mid-19th century, missionary activities concentrated in coastal areas. 戴德生 (James Hudson Taylor) believed that hundreds of millions of people in the inland areas still had not encountered Christianity. He proposed a strategy to conduct missions deep into the interior. The mission operated under the belief of a “faith mission,” emphasizing “complete reliance on faith” and firmly believing that God would provide.The China Inland Mission operated in an interdenominational manner, attracting missionaries from Britain, Europe, North America, and Australia. 戴德生 (James Hudson Taylor) advocated for missionaries to wear Chinese clothing as a sign of respect for Chinese culture. In 1866, the first group of 16 missionaries led by 戴德生 (James Hudson Taylor) arrived in Shanghai, after which the mission gradually expanded into inland provinces such as 浙江 (Zhejiang), 安徽 (Anhui), and 湖南 (Hunan). By 1900, the China Inland Mission had established numerous churches, schools, and medical facilities across 18 provinces, with more than 800 missionaries, making it one of the largest missionary organizations of the time.

After the People’s Republic of China was established in 1949, the China Inland Mission remained until it withdrew from China in 1951, shifting its focus to Southeast Asia and other regions. In 1951, the organization was renamed the Overseas Missionary Fellowship (OMF) and continued its missionary activities throughout Asia.

  1. The Chinese Recorder, 1868-1941 (陶飛亞 (Tao Feiya) 2007: 243-262; Rawlinson 1990)

The Chinese Recorder and Missionary Journal 《教務雜誌》 is the longest-running English publication during the turbulent times in 19th-century China, published from 1868 until it ceased operations in 1941 due to the outbreak of the Pacific War. The publication was intended as a platform for missionaries in China to exchange information and discuss mission work, aiding mutual understanding of Chinese culture and implementing their efforts. It was initially launched as the Missionary Recorder monthly published by 福州 (Fuzhou) missionaries 佩來爾 (Lucian N. Wheeler) in 1867 with funding from the American Methodist Church and the Presbyterian Church of the North, but it ceased after 9 months when 佩氏 (Wheeler) was reassigned. Due to demand for its continuation, it was relaunched in May 1878 under the name The Chinese Recorder and Missionary Journal in Fuzhou, with 美以美會 (Methodist) missionary 保靈 (S. L. Baldwin) as the editor. After May 1869, 公理會 (American Congregational) missionary 盧公明 (Justus Doolittle) joined. However, it again ceased publication in 1872 due to lack of funding. In January 1874, it became a bimonthly published by 上海美華書局 (Shanghai Meihua Bookstore) (changing to monthly in March 1885), with 偉烈亞力 (A. Wylie) of the British and Foreign Bible Society as the editor, and after he returned home in 1878, 保靈 (Baldwin) resumed the role. Later, editors included 哈巴安德 (Happer A. Patton), L. H. Gulick from the American Congregational Church, 佩來爾 (Wheeler), and 費啟鴻 (G. F. Fitch) from the American Presbyterian Church. From 1912, Frank J. Rawlinson served as assistant editor before taking over as the longest-serving editor until he died in August 1937 during the Japanese attack on Shanghai. He was succeeded by 米立肯 (Frank R. Millican) from the American Presbyterian Church. After the outbreak of the Pacific War in December 1941, the publication ceased, making 《教務雜誌》 the longest-running missionary publication in China.

As a missionary publication, 《教務雜誌》 preserved a wealth of information on the missionary movement, particularly reflecting the various challenges faced by missionaries in China and showcasing diverse voices within the missionary community.

  1. The Society for the Diffusion of Christian and General Knowledge among the Chinese, 1887 (梁元生 (Liang Yuansheng) 1978: 89-113)

The Society for the Diffusion of Christian and General Knowledge among the Chinese was formed by a group of Western expatriates in China, including missionaries, merchants, and prominent figures. It was established in Shanghai in 1887, originally named the 同文書會 (Tongwen Book Society), and was renamed the 廣學會 (Society for the Diffusion of Knowledge) in 1892.The English name at its inception was The Society for the Diffusion of Christian and General Knowledge among the Chinese, which was changed to The Christian Literature Society in 1905.

In 1884, British Presbyterian missionary 韋廉臣 (Alexander Williamson) established the 同文書會 (The Chinese Book and Tract Society) in Scotland, initiating a fundraising campaign to acquire printing machinery and establish a printing press in Shanghai, aiming to publish religious texts and pamphlets in Chinese. After the Scottish society disbanded, the remaining funds, assets, and the Shanghai printing press were managed by 韋廉臣 (Williamson). In 1887, he contacted expatriates in China, such as customs chief 赫德 (Robert Hart) and 林樂知 (Young J. Allen), seeking to reorganize the society in Shanghai. In October 1887, the Chinese 同文書會 was officially established, with the English name as The Society for the Diffusion of Christian and General Knowledge among the Chinese. The English name indicates that in addition to publishing Christian literature, the society also aimed to introduce new knowledge (Western learning).

The actual work of the society was overseen by a secretary (later the superintendent), with 韋廉臣 (Williamson) serving as the first secretary until his death in 1890. After 李提摩太 (Timothy Richard) took over in October 1891, the Chinese name was changed to 廣學會 (The Society for the Diffusion of Knowledge) in 1892. The work included: (1) Translation and publication: During the 同文書會 period, the main focus was on publishing religious texts; however, after 1892, the number of books related to social reform, introducing new knowledge, and strengthening the nation gradually increased. After the First Sino-Japanese War in 1895, the latter exceeded the number of religious texts; (2) Free distribution and sale of books: For free distribution, books were primarily handed out outside the venues for the imperial examination; (3) Publishing and essay contests: After the resumption of the 万国公报 (Universal Daily) in 1889, it was designated as the organ newspaper of the 廣學會, changing its English name from Globe Magazine to Review of the Times. In addition to the 万国公报, they published the 中西教會報 (Missionary Review) starting in February 1891, the 大同報 (Datong Daily) in 1904, and the 女鐸報 (Women’s Herald) aimed at women in 1912. Essay contests were held as early as 1889 during 韋廉臣’s time, with topics such as “Are there differences between Western and Chinese sciences?” and “Which is more precise, Western or Chinese mathematics?”. Later, 李提摩太 (Timothy Richard) also frequently organized such contests.

The 廣學會 was well-received by reformist individuals for its publications introducing Western learning.

After the establishment of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, the 廣學會 moved its operations to Hong Kong and established the 基督教輔僑出版社 (Council on Christian Literature for Overseas Chinese, CCLOC; later renamed the Chinese Christian Literature Council). The 廣學會 continued limited operations under the Communist regime and merged with other publishers in 1956 to form the 基督教聯合書局 (Christian Union Press).

  1. Educational Association of China, 1890 (王樹槐 (Wang Shuhua) 1971: 395-396; Cohen 1978: 576-578)

After Christianity was introduced to China in the 19th century, missionary schools were established throughout the country to further educational efforts. At the first missionary conference in China in 1877, a comprehensive discussion on educational issues took place. During the meeting, 黎力基 (R. Lechler) argued that textbooks should remain focused on religious content, seeing education primarily as a tool for evangelism, while 狄考文 (C. W. Mateer) emphasized the importance of education as secondary to religion. The conference resolved to form a “School and Textbooks Series Committee” to oversee the compilation of educational materials. Members included 丁韋良 (W. A. P. Martin), 韋廉臣 (A. Williamson), 狄考文 (Mateer), and 林樂知 (Y. J. Allen).Allen), 傅蘭雅 (John Fryer), and 黎力基 (R. Lechler), among others. At the time, 韋廉臣 (A. Williamson) believed that textbooks should be edited from a Christian perspective, while 傅蘭雅 (Fryer) suggested separating religious and secular books.

At the second conference of missionaries in China in 1890, educational issues continued to receive attention. The conference decided to reorganize the “School and Textbooks Series Committee” into the 益智書會 (Educational Association of China), aiming to bring about coordination and unity in educational efforts and to accept Christians engaged in education as members. In 1890, the association had 35 members, increasing to 189 by 1899, and reaching 403 by 1907. For example, in 1907, membership was concentrated in coastal regions such as Shanghai (96), 浙江 (Zhejiang) (52), 江蘇 (Jiangsu) (46), 福建 (Fujian) (43), and 廣東 (Guangdong) (32).

Between 1890 and 1893, the 益智書會 published 12 titles, 6 of which were in the category of natural sciences. From 1890 to 1912, they published a total of 30 textbooks, many of which were adopted by church schools. However, in the 20th century, the association faced increasing difficulties in publishing textbooks. One challenge was that missionaries were only writing on a volunteer basis, making it hard for them to fulfill the editorial demands, and furthermore, Chinese textbooks required the assistance of local collaborators. Another challenge was the rise of local efforts to translate textbooks, such as those from 商務印書館 (Shangwu Yinshuguan) established in 1897 and the 學部編譯圖書局 (Ministry of Education’s Translation Bureau) established in 1908, which significantly outnumbered the efforts of the 益智書會.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. The Conference of Missionaries in China 1877 (Luo Weihong 罗伟虹 2014: 281-298)

From May 10 to 24, 1877, the first missionary conference was held in Shanghai by missionaries in China. The initiative for the meeting originated from a conference of the Shandong Presbyterian Church’s Zhifu district in August 1874, where attending missionaries believed that missionaries of different Presbyterians in China should gather to discuss missionary affairs. Additionally, missionaries from other societies in Zhifu for recuperation also had the opportunity to exchange ideas and discuss. The idea of holding a conference for all missionaries in China was conceived in Zhifu, leading to the establishment of a committee composed of J. L. Nevius (倪维思), A. Williamson (韦廉臣), and J. B. Hartwell (海雅西) for follow-up. The three then wrote to missionaries in China to inquire about their interest in this conference and the matters to be discussed. After two consultations, the results were published in the May-June 1875 issue of the Chinese Recorder (《教务杂志》), and a formal conference organizing committee was established, consisting of seven missionaries representing various regions (Hong Kong and Guangdong Province, Taiwan and Fujian Province, Zhejiang Province, Jiangsu Province, the Yangtze River Basin, Niuzhuang and Shandong Province, and Zhili Province).

A total of 126 missionaries from 29 different societies attended the 1877 missionary conference. Thirty papers were presented and widely discussed afterward. Topics addressed included: (1) Spiritual preparation for missionary work; (2) Challenges faced by missionaries in China: Christianity versus Chinese religions, ancestor worship in China, Chinese customs surrounding marriage and death, foot binding, the opium issue, and protective missionary clauses; (3) Establishment of the Chinese Church: self-support issues, local preacher training, standards for accepting baptism; (4) Missionary methods and ventures: circuit preaching, education, work with women, literacy work, principles of Chinese translation, etc.; (5) Cooperation among different societies.

The conference also issued an open letter to Western churches urging them to provide more manpower and resources for the vast missionary field in China: “The dark side of China pains us; beneath the dazzling façade of Chinese society lie brutal and tragic customs. A brief reading of the ‘Peking Gazette’ published daily by the government can confirm that from the Emperor down to commoners, every stratum is deeply entangled in superstition. … In this environment, millions of souls perish each year, is it not because we have failed to deliver the gospel to them?”

After the conference, the Records of the General Conference of the Protestant Missionaries of China, held at Shanghai, May 1—24, 1877, was published in 1878 by the American Presbyterian Mission Press in Shanghai, spanning nearly 500 pages.

  1. The Conference of Missionaries in China 1890 (Luo Weihong 罗伟虹 2014: 298-306)

From May 7 to 20, 1890, the second missionary conference was held in Shanghai by missionaries in China. According to the decisions made in the first missionary conference in 1877, the second conference was originally planned to be held ten years later, but after consulting the opinions of missionaries, it was confirmed to take place in 1890. A total of 445 missionaries from 35 different societies and organizations participated in the conference. Notably, for the first time, Chinese representatives attended this conference, specifically Pastor Y. K. Yen (顏永京) and his wife from the American Protestant Episcopal Mission. Fifty-eight articles were presented at the conference, divided into 13 discussion sessions. The topics discussed were quite broad: (1) Evaluation of the current situation in China; (2) Various issues related to missionary work in China: such as evangelism methods, secret societies in Shandong, work with women, conditions for allowing Chinese to be baptized, training of local preachers for self-support; (3) Missionary methods and ventures: medical work, education, literacy work, charitable work; (4) Bible translation; (5) Cooperation among societies; (6) Indigenous missions; (7) The opium problem, etc.

To follow up on various tasks, the conference established several committees, including three related to Bible translation (one in literary style, one in local dialects, and one in Mandarin). Other committees addressed issues of society unification, women’s work, the opium issue, literacy work, work with blind and deaf individuals, school textbooks, relations with the Chinese government, urging mother churches to send missionaries to China, and fellowship work.

To increase missionary resources, the conference passed a resolution to send an open letter to the mother churches of each society, urging them to dispatch 1,000 missionaries to China within five years.After the conference, the Records of the General Conference of the Protestant Missionaries of China, held at Shanghai, May 7—20, 1890, was published in 1890 by the American Presbyterian Mission Press in Shanghai, spanning over 700 pages.

  1. New Age Novel Contest, 1895 (Kuang Ciliang 鄺智良 2017; Li Zipeng 黎子鵬 2015: xvi-xl)

After the First Sino-Japanese War in 1895, British missionary John Fryer (傅蘭雅) at the Jiangnan Arsenal translated Western scholarly works, and capitalizing on the strong public sentiment, he published a notice in Shen Bao (《申報》), the International Review (《萬國公報》), and Chinese Recorder (《教務雜誌》) to hold a novel competition soliciting new novels that critically addressed the “three evils” of opium, contemporary issues, and foot binding, aimed at providing remedies for the ailments of Chinese society to achieve national strength. In the English notice in the Chinese Recorder, he urged participants to write with a “Christian tone” and hoped for involvement from students, teachers, and pastors within the church community. He also cited the success of the American novel Uncle Tom’s Cabin (《黑奴籲天錄》) in awakening American citizens to oppose slavery, arguing that quality novels could enlighten public opinion and that China likewise needed novels to reform societal ills.

The competition ended in September 1895, and Fryer received 162 entries from across the country. In March 1896, Fryer listed 20 winners in the International Review, but he expressed dissatisfaction, pointing out that the submissions focused too heavily on the opium issue while neglecting contemporary problems. He also requested the use of “simple literary style,” but many submissions were described as “vulgar.” Additionally, numerous works were not in the novel format, but were essays, letters, plays, and lyrics.

Among the 162 works, 124 explicitly noted their regional background, with the majority hailing from Shandong (38), Guangdong (23), Hubei (18), Fujian (13), Jiangsu (9), Zhejiang (5), Hebei (5), Anhui (4), Shanxi (3), Shanghai (2), Shaanxi (2), and Jiangxi (2). The backgrounds indicate a distinct coastal influence (Guangdong, Fujian, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shandong). Sixty-seven participants clearly identified their affiliated churches, while many others did not specify but included Christian elements in their content.

The New Age novels reflect the interpretations and applications of the Bible by late Qing Chinese Christians, notably their concerns for national salvation, prompting them to perceive Christianity from the perspective of social reform and national rejuvenation. From the perspective of Chinese literary history, the New Age novels undoubtedly serve as prototypes for the development of new Chinese novels, predating Liang Qichao’s (梁啟超) 1902 launch of the New Novel (《新小說》) by seven years.

  1. John R. Mott’s First Visit to China 1896 (Hopkins 1979: 175–186; Zhang Zhiwei 張志偉 2010: 14)

John R. Mott (穆德), the General Secretary of the World Student Christian Federation (W.S.C.F.), long-time leader of the Young Men’s Christian Association (Y.M.C.A.), and the Student Volunteer Movement, visited China for three and a half months in July 1896, covering Guangzhou, Shanghai, Zhifu, Dengzhou, Tianjin, and Beijing. During his trip, he inspected churches and schools, promoting the establishment of 22 school youth associations and convening meetings with missionaries from various areas. From November 3 to 5, he attended the national conference of youth associations held in Shanghai, during which the national organization “China Student Christian Association” was officially established to foster connections and provide guidance for local school youth associations.

Mott subsequently made several more visits to China: in 1901, 1907, 1913, 1922, 1926, 1929, and 1949.

  1. Missionaries and the Hundred Days’ Reform 1898 (Wang Shuhua 王樹槐 1965; Huang Zhaohong 黃昭弘 1993: 125-148; Liang Yuansheng 梁元生 1978: 137-143; Cohen 1978: 585-590)

On June 11, 1898, Emperor Guangxu issued an edict for reform, initiating a modernization movement in education, economy, military, and politics, driven by Kang Youwei (康有為) and Liang Qichao (梁啟超).However, due to opposition from conservative forces, on September 21, Empress Dowager Cixi initiated a coup, placing the Guangxu Emperor under house arrest, abolishing the new reforms, and arresting the reformers. The reform movement lasted only 103 days, known as the “Hundred Days’ Reform.”

Although the Hundred Days’ Reform was brief, reformist ideas had already gained recognition among intellectuals of the Reform Movement. Western missionaries played a significant role in advocating reform ideas and rescuing reformers after the coup. First, all those advocating reform were influenced by the West, with their understanding of Western scholarship largely derived from interacting with Westerners in China and reading translated works. These Westerners primarily included missionaries from the Guangxuehui (Broad Learning Society). The International Review (《萬國公報》) published by Guangxuehui and its translated works served as crucial channels for introducing Western scholarship and governance. It is worth noting that during the late Qing Dynasty’s Self-Strengthening Movement, the acceptance of Western knowledge was limited to material aspects under the banner of “strengthening through powerful ships and cannons.” However, the translations from Guangxuehui and the articles in the International Review transcended the framework of “Chinese learning as the essence, Western learning for practical use,” addressing political and educational systems, as well as ideological aspects of “Western learning.” Kang Youwei famously stated, “Every publication of the Shanghai Guangxuehui should be acquired as much as possible.” Liang Qichao included 22 works from Guangxuehui in his Bibliography of Western Learning. It can be said that missionaries were the sowers of Western knowledge and new governance, playing a crucial role in disseminating reformist ideas. Moreover, reformers like Kang and Liang had personal interactions with figures such as T. Richard (李提摩太) and Gilbert Reid (李佳白), and Liang Qichao even served as a secretary for T. Richard. Kang and Liang established the Qiangxuehui (Strong Learning Society) in Beijing, in which T. Richard also participated.

In addition to interactions with reformist individuals, the relations and interactions between missionaries and officials also contributed to the promotion of new governance. For instance, after T. Richard was sent to Beijing by Guangxuehui in 1895, he extensively mingled with officials and visited prominent figures like Weng Tonghe, Li Hongzhang, Sun Jiadun, Zhang Yinhuan, and Prince Gong, advocating for the new reform policies. T. Richard composed an article titled “New Policies” based on his discussions with Weng regarding the direction of reforms, proposing nine urgent matters for China to address, which Weng then submitted to the government. During the Hundred Days’ Reform, Guangxu even approved Kang Youwei’s request to hire T. Richard as a personal advisor to the Emperor.

After the coup of 1898, reformists such as Tan Sitong, Yang Shenxiu, Liu Guangdi, Yang Rui, Lin Xu, and Kang Guangren were executed on September 28, an event referred to as the “Six Gentlemen of the Wuxu Reform.” In fact, rumors had spread in Beijing prior to the coup that Cixi would depose Guangxu on September 19. On that day, Kang Youwei contacted T. Richard in the hope that he could rescue Guangxu. That very day, Kang was ordered by Guangxu to leave Beijing for Shanghai. When Kang arrived in Shanghai, the coup had already taken place, and orders were issued to apprehend him. T. Richard then telegraphed the British Consul in Shanghai, R. Brenan, to seek assistance in the rescue, ultimately allowing Kang to escape to Hong Kong.

As for Liang Qichao, he was discussing matters with Tan Sitong on September 21 when he learned of the coup, after which he immediately visited T. Richard. The three agreed that Rong Hong would meet with the American Minister, Liang would meet with the Japanese Minister, and T. Richard would go to see the British Minister. However, both the American and British ministers were out of the capital and could not be met. Liang only managed to meet with the Japanese Chargé d’Affaires, Lin Quanzhu. Lin later informed Ito Hirobumi, who decided to attempt a rescue, arranging for Liang to escape China by boarding a Japanese ship in Tianjin.

Key Theological Issues

  1. The Theological War of Opening China (Miller 1974: 249-257; Ye Renchang 葉仁昌 1994: 183-186; Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1997a: 29-34)

In 1834, when the news broke that British Superintendent of Trade in China William J. Napier (律勞卑) faced yet another setback in his attempts to improve Sino-British relations, disappointment was felt not only among British merchants in China but also among a group of early missionaries who came to China. They firmly believed that the first prerequisite for changing China’s pagan soil and backwardness was for its people to accept the truth of Christ. The foundation for evangelizing China hinged on whether the Chinese were willing to establish equal diplomatic relations with Western (Christian) countries and lift the prohibitions imposed by the Kangxi 康熙 Emperor. Hence, they were greatly dismayed by the current state of Sino-foreign relations. For instance, American Congregationalist missionary E. C. Bridgman (裨治文) repeatedly described the present predicament using terms like “evils” and “grievous,” emphasizing that Britain could no longer afford to be lenient and permissive toward China; otherwise, China would only grow prouder and more arrogant. In a publication edited by missionaries called the Chinese Repository (《中華叢報》), Bridgman even likened China’s isolationist policies to that of a “pirate.” Bridgman undoubtedly touched upon the crux of Sino-foreign relations at the time; he was acutely aware that if the Qing government was unwilling to change its isolationist policies, China could not benefit from interactions with the West. Therefore, he vehemently opposed the British government’s policy of silence towards China, asserting that only by curbing China’s arrogance could it be made docile. In other words, the use of strong military force was rational and justified. He stated: “Force is a necessary means to maintain justice; only force can improve the Qing Empire’s attitude toward the West.” “If possible, of course, peaceably, but if necessary, force is unavoidable” (Peaceably if we may, but forcibly if we must).

In 1839, war broke out between China and Britain over Lin Zexu’s (林則徐) opium ban, historically known as the “Opium War.” The British Parliament, in deciding to deploy troops, claimed this was a “just war,” aimed at protecting the lives, freedom, and property of the British people. For Bridgman, this was undoubtedly the moment he had long awaited. In January 1840, he noted that China was preparing to take a desperate gamble. Although war was an evil and full of suffering, it was difficult to avoid conflict in the name of “past justice” and “future security.” In July, he commented again, emphasizing that the horrific war aimed to awaken ancient, arrogant, prejudiced, and ignorant China from its slumber. As messengers of God’s love and peace, it was clearly a challenging task for them to affirm war. However, when they contemplated that the gates of pagan China would open after the war, allowing the Gospel to spread across the land, they felt compelled to accept war, firmly believing that God would have His special way, even using the sinful act of war as a means!

Yet, what placed the missionaries in a dilemma was their profound understanding of the role opium played in this war. The British Empire waged war for opium because opium trade had compensated the British government with large amounts of silver, reversing the significant trade deficit in Sino-British trade. British Parliament members, when voting to send troops, were also swayed by numerous opium merchants, making this war a veritable “Opium War.” How could they ignore the harm caused by opium? The only opportunity to open China’s gates was tied to immoral opium trade, and they believed the British government must bear responsibility for the opium trade, yet the allure of opening the stubborn doors of China was too great.

The missionaries found themselves in turmoil and contradiction. Ultimately, they had to interpret everything as God’s wondrous and unfathomable deeds, enabling them to extricate themselves from their predicaments. For instance, Bridgman believed that God would manifest Himself by any means, thus viewing the opium trade, although violating His law, as still serving Him. Another missionary, S. W. Williams (衛三畏), frankly pointed out that opium made the war “unjust,” yet the result of this injustice coincided with God’s purpose and plan. Under the overarching premise of evangelizing China, all unjust means (war, opium) could be rationalized. They believed that once China opened up, both countries would benefit mutually from future communications, the opium trade would cease, and more importantly, the Gospel could flow unimpeded into China, fulfilling the grand vision of China returning to the Lord in the near future.Ye Renchang (葉仁昌) once described the “theology of war” as a way for missionaries in China to justify the invasion of China from a religious perspective.

When the gates of China finally opened, the missionaries placed their hopes on war as a vehicle for the Gospel, naively believing that once political barriers were forcefully removed, missionary work would proceed smoothly. However, they did not anticipate that their rhetoric would later serve as compelling evidence for accusations that Christianity was an instrument of imperialist aggression in China. The gates of China were undoubtedly opened, but their words and actions, combined with missionary terms included in unequal treaties, set an unshakeable burden for future evangelistic work that could never be erased.

  1. The Term Question (Li Chichang 李熾昌 2008: 1-20)

Since the introduction of Christianity to China, missionaries have debated how to translate “Elohim” from the Hebrew Bible and “Theos” from the Greek Bible into Chinese. This debate mainly divided into two camps: one, led by American missionaries, argued for the translation as “神” (shen), while the other, led by British missionaries, advocated for “上帝” (Shangdi). Both sides developed their arguments, leading to a lively theological dispute. Due to irreconcilable differences, two distinct Chinese translations of the Bible emerged: the “Shangdi version” published by the British and the “Shen version” published by the Americans. The advocates of “神” believed that Chinese people had long been superstitious about ancestral gods, and the deities they worshipped, including Tian (heaven), Di (emperor), and Shangdi (God), were merely polytheistic idols, incompatible with the Christian belief in a single Sovereign. Thus, a pressing missionary task was to awaken the Chinese from polytheistic superstition and idol worship, making it inappropriate to use indigenous names for the divine in translations, as this would blur the lines with existing idol worship. Furthermore, “神” is a generic term that refers to all beings of worship and does not specify a particular deity. Additionally, “神” carries the connotation of being sacred and transcendent, which aligns well with biblical terms. Conversely, the Shangdi camp argued that Elohim and Theos are not merely generic terms, and within the Chinese context, “神” fails to clearly distinguish itself from polytheistic beliefs and idol worship. Therefore, it was essential to find an appropriate indigenous term, and “上帝” (Shangdi) was deemed suitable as it is the term by which the Chinese recognize the highest Sovereign.

The debate over 上帝 (Shangdi) versus 神 (shen) reflects differing missionary strategies and attitudes. Those in favor of “神” held a completely dismissive and exclusionary attitude towards Chinese tradition, believing that the aim of missionary work was to replace existing errors with Christian truth, rescuing pagans from superstition. In contrast, those supporting “上帝” displayed a more sympathetic and tolerant attitude toward local traditions, acknowledging that the local tradition also contained insight into the highest truth.

It was not only Western missionaries who voiced opinions on the “term question”; Chinese believers also expressed their perspectives. This can be seen in the “Term Question” debate that unfolded in the “World’s Paper” (《萬國公報》) between 1877 and 1878. The debate was initially sparked by the article “On the Term” by Shanghai missionary Huang Pinsan (黃品三). J. S. Roberts (陸佩), the editor of the publication and an American Presbyterian missionary, responded to the article and called for readers to express their opinions. In total, over 60 articles were published, mostly written by local Chinese pastors or believers. However, according to Li Chichang’s (李熾昌) analysis, the local voices participating in the discourse still could not escape the role of representing foreign missionaries; the debate largely served as a microcosm of divisions among Western missionary factions.

  1. Christianity and Confucianism (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1995: 117-118, 142-143)

In the 19th century, missionaries in China had to reflect on the relationship between Christianity and Confucian traditions within “Confucian China” (儒教中國). However, there were notable divergences among missionaries regarding their attitudes toward Confucian traditions, leading to debates between “Confucius plus Christ” (孔子加基督) and “Christ or Confucius” (基督或孔子). Missionaries who advocated for harmonizing with Confucian traditions included W. A. P. Martin (丁韋良) from the Northern Presbyterian Church, T. Richard (李提摩太) from the British Baptist Church, and Young J. Lin (林樂知).Allen, Joseph Edkins (艾約瑟, London Mission), Ernst Faber (花知安, Tungshan Association), James Legge (理雅各, London Mission), and others pointed out that the Confucian tradition and Christianity not only share common ground but that the latter can also perfect and supplement the former. For instance, Young J. Lin (林樂知) used the five relationships, five constants, and the three admonitions of Confucianism to draw parallels with Christianity, highlighting their similarities. W. A. P. Martin (丁韋良) argued for “Confucius plus Christ”: a Confucian could accept Christ without abandoning their beliefs. For the Chinese, the relationship between Confucius and Jesus should be one of mutual assistance and compatibility, rather than an either-or choice.

Conversely, missionaries opposing Confucianism included John Macgowan (麥嘉湖, London Mission), Griffith John (楊格非, London Mission), Hudson Taylor (戴德生, Inland Mission), Alexander Wylie (偉烈亞力, London Mission), D. N. Lyon (賴恩, American Presbyterian), and C. W. Mateer (狄考文, American North Presbyterian), among others. They were convinced that Confucianism was the greatest obstacle to Christianity’s spread in China, arguing that Christians should be “new men in Christ, not holy men of Confucianism” (The New men in Christ is not the Holy men of Confucianism). Lyon passionately proclaimed, “Confucius and Christ—who is greater? Should we first explain the merits of Confucius and his teachings and then point out how Jesus supplements them? No! Absolutely not! Jesus is the King of Kings, and Lord of Lords (the great king of kings, and Lord of Lords).” Macgowan reiterated that one should choose between Christ and Confucius. Mateer expressed deep concern over the “Confucianizing tendency” within the Chinese church. These arguments were clearly aimed at the positions taken by certain missionaries (and Chinese Christians) advocating for “harmonizing with Confucianism” and “supplementing Confucianism,” issuing stern reproaches. They feared that the process of harmonizing with Confucian traditions might lead to the transformation or even distortion of the Gospel message.

  1. Christianity and the Ancestor Worship Issue (Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1997b: 11-19)

The issue of ancestor worship is an unavoidable topic in the historical encounter of Christianity with China. Whether in Ming and Qing Catholicism or late Qing Christianity, both within missionary circles and among Chinese believers, intense debates and discussions have emerged regarding this issue. Numerous publications written by early 19th-century missionaries focused on ancestor worship, reflecting their significant concern about the topic. For example, John L. Nevius’s (倪維思) “On the Errors of Ancestor Worship” (1859), John Chalmers’s (湛約翰) “On the Secular and Clear Ancestral Offerings,” Justus Doolittle’s (盧公明) “On Cold Food and Clear Brightness” (1855), Thomas H. Hudson’s (胡德邁) “On Tomb Sweeping During Qingming” (1848), Walter H. Medhurst’s (麥都思) “On Tomb Sweeping During Qingming” (1826), and “Records of Difficult Questions by the Wild Guests” (1854). These works predominantly held a negative stance toward ancestor worship, emphasizing that only the one true God is worthy of receiving “sacrificial rites”; parents and ancestors “cannot be equated with God.” True filial piety consists of “respecting the living and mourning the dead,” which aligns with biblical teaching on filial devotion.

Long concerned with the Chinese practice of ancestor worship, Matthew T. Yates (晏馬太, Southern Baptist) emphasized in his 1877 report to the missionary conference that ancestor worship was an act of idolatry. He pointed out that while ancestor worship undoubtedly manifested filial piety in ancient Chinese classics, it could not represent a system that has existed for thousands of years. He observed that what he saw was not the filial devotion of Chinese sons but rebellion and obstinacy. The so-called “filial piety” of these individuals only manifests after parents’ deaths; could this truly be considered filial? Moreover, ancestor worship had essentially become a fundamental religion for the Chinese, where they hoped to avoid disasters and ensure peace. This had long surpassed the essence of filial devotion, transforming into idol worship. Such practices resulted in the expenditure of vast sums of money for the Chinese each year, turning the living into slaves of the dead, with ancestor worship becoming a source of all social evils.Yates (晏氏) firmly believed that only Jesus Christ could save the Chinese people from superstition and idol worship. Therefore, any concessions made concerning ancestor worship to accommodate Chinese people were merely expressions of surrender.

Among the more than 20 missionaries who spoke that day, only A. E. Moule (慕雅德), Joseph Edkins (艾約瑟, London Mission), and W. S. Holt (何特, American Presbyterian) supported a more moderate and compromising approach to the ancestor worship issue. Moule, considering practical realities, inquired whether Chinese believers could still inherit ancestral property without participating in ancestor worship. He was familiar with two individuals: one could not become a Christian because they owned ancestral property, while the other gave up their inheritance upon conversion but lost their means of support and was on the brink of poverty. The remaining attendees attacked ancestor worship and opposed any form of compromise or tolerance. Indeed, many missionaries recognized that this stance would greatly hinder the conversion of Chinese people, prompting some to advocate for strengthening Christian rituals while removing superstitious elements to replace ancestor worship.

In 1890, during the second joint conference of missionaries in China, the issue of ancestor worship once again became a focal point for discussion. At the meeting, some missionaries openly challenged the long-standing prohibition against ancestor worship, arguing for coordination and tolerance. They insisted that ancestor worship was not entirely synonymous with idol worship. For instance, W. A. P. Martin (丁韋良) acknowledged that while ancestor worship undoubtedly mixed elements of idol worship and superstition, it also had its merits. Therefore, he proposed that ancestor worship be altered based on the principle of “removing the dross and preserving the essence,” allowing it to coexist harmoniously with Christian faith. Martin further argued from three aspects—“posture,” “invocation,” and “offering”—to assert that ancestor worship did not equate to idol worship. He pointed out that the “bowing” in ancestor worship is an expression of respect rather than an act of idol worship, as “kneeling” is an essential part of daily life for Chinese people, whether showing reverence to parents, the emperor, or officials. Regarding “invocation,” Martin believed that whether ancestor worship constituted idolatry depended entirely on whether the worshippers sought blessings from the deceased; if so, it was superstitious; otherwise, it was merely a gesture of respect. He noted that Chinese people pay respect to the deceased during ceremonies such as weddings, coming-of-age rituals, and funerals, without seeking protection. As for “offering,” he contended that the determination of idolatry should be based on the object of worship, not the items used in the ritual. Thus, Western practices of using flowers to honor ancestors were fundamentally equivalent in meaning to the Chinese practice of offering meat and vegetables. Martin concluded by suggesting that missionaries should not interfere with Chinese customs, allowing the divine truth (The Divine Truth) to take root in people’s hearts before it brings about natural influence and reform.

Alongside Martin, other speakers including F. Ohlinger (武林吉, United Methodist), H. V. Noyes (那夏禮, American Presbyterian), and H. Blodget (伯羅樂, American Congregationalist) opposed any form of compromise on ancestor worship. Consequently, the missionaries engaged in a protracted and heated discussion on the issue, with many targeting Martin’s report for rebuttal. For example, Lyon strongly objected to Martin’s assertion that filial piety was central to ancestor worship, deeming it a misplacement of priorities. Missionaries supporting Martin’s perspective constituted a minority, including Edkins, Gilbert Reid (李佳白, American Presbyterian), and T. Richard (李提摩太). Hudson Taylor (戴德生, China Inland Mission) ultimately concluded the matter, stating, “The issue of ancestor worship is, from beginning to end, and in every related aspect, an act of idol worship.”

  1. Folk Sects and Christianity (Bays 1985: 121-134; Bays 1994: 124-143; Zhu Xiaobai 2023: 190-196; Li Nan 2018: 201-216)

At the 1890 missionary conference in China, British Baptist missionary F. H. James (秀耀春) presented an article titled “Secret Sects of Shandong,” indicating that a significant number of members from folk sects in Shandong were joining Christianity.After his report, James (秀耀春) listed the names of ten sects (such as Taixiangjiao (太陽教), Chaoguangjiao (朝光教), Bailianjiao (白蓮教), Sichuanjiao (四川教), Wuweijiao (無為教), Baiyunjiao (白雲教), Shengxianjiao (聖賢教), Baguajiao (八掛教), Zimu Jiao (子母教), and Falujiao (法路教)), noting that some possessed political characteristics while many were purely religious sects. He pointed out that local officials were suspicious of them, yet he praised these Christians as sincerely seeking God and hoping to rid themselves of sinful habits to bless their souls and eternal lives. Therefore, he called on missionaries to take these sects seriously, as he personally collected substantial texts from various sects and found commendable value within them. He believed that the existence of these sects demonstrated that Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism could not fulfill the needs of the people. Consequently, their existence proved that people yearn for truth, and Christianity should avoid harshly criticizing their errors with a lack of compassion, but instead help them reform by developing their merits and supplementing them with higher and better knowledge.

As early as the 1880s, the Chinese Recorder (《教務雜誌》) contained accounts from missionaries documenting interactions between folk sects and Christianity. American Congregationalist Arthur H. Smith (明恩溥) indicated that some teachings of folk sects exhibited similarities to Christianity, with the first believers in the northwestern region of Shandong being adherents of the “Zhongyangmen” sect. Henry Porter (麥博恆理), also of the American Congregationalist mission, mentioned that the leader of the Baguajiao sect in Shandong led a group of followers to collectively convert to Christianity, praising one of them as a “seeker after Truth.” Joseph Edkins (艾約瑟, London Mission) pointed out that many believers from the Zhili (直隸) region came from the Hongyang sect.

However, some missionaries were skeptical about the motives of folk sect members joining Christianity, suggesting that it was a means of escaping government repression, with the hope of obtaining protection through Christianity. Yet, other missionaries acknowledged that due to the extensive network of folk sects in Shandong, it was nearly impossible to avoid association with them. If church congregations were to refuse them entry, it would be difficult for the church to establish itself locally. Therefore, the most important requirement was that they sincerely repent and abandon past rituals and behaviors contrary to Christian teachings.

Daniel H. Bays’s (裴士丹) research indicated that some doctrines of folk sects indeed aligned with Christianity, providing possibilities for sect members to convert to Christianity. Consequently, for Chinese officials, “in the 19th century, Christianity should, to some extent, be regarded as heresy in China, rather than merely a ‘foreign religion’.” He even posed a hypothesis regarding whether the popularity of the indigenous Pentecostal True Jesus Church in Shandong in the 20th century could stem from the similarities between Pentecostal beliefs and those of folk sects.

  1. Divergence in Missionary Approaches (Cohen 1957; Cohen 1978: 557-559; Xing Fuzeng 邢福增 1995: 32-33)

Historian A. Cohen (柯文) compared the “Hudson Taylor model” and the “Timothy Richard model” in 1957, asserting that the former represented a strongly salvation-oriented missionary route that “conserved” efforts, contrasted with Timothy Richard and Young J. Allen (林樂知), who actively advocated for and participated in the Westernization and reform of China. Cohen remarked that these divergences in missionary approaches primarily stemmed from differing theological perspectives held by the missionaries.

However, the missionary work in the 19th century was fueled by the Great Awakening in Europe and America (The Great Awakening), and the overwhelming emphasis on soul salvation within Pietism deeply influenced every missionary coming to China. Thus, almost all missionaries were engaged in direct evangelism. Many missions provided various degrees of education and medical services, ultimately aimed at enabling Chinese people to convert to the Gospel through these services. When examining those labeled as “liberal” missionaries, it is apparent that they all initially devoted themselves to direct evangelism. Any subsequent changes in their missionary methods were likely responses to the challenges they faced rather than merely influenced by theological viewpoints; thus, to attribute these changes solely to theological reasoning would be disconnected from the reality. Initially, Young J. Allen also focused on direct evangelism upon arriving in China, with his eventual shift likely reflecting a response to the difficulties encountered in the country rather than theological influences. Similarly, Timothy Richard’s top-down missionary strategy emerged from the lessons learned from missionary work in China.

The divisions in late Qing missionary approaches largely arose from the various practical challenges faced by missionaries in China.Some missionaries, responding to the lack of success in direct evangelism at the time, adjusted their strategies, hoping to further assist China in reforming through education and the establishment of publications, thereby eliminating paganism and believing that this approach would be greatly beneficial to missionary work in the long run. However, another group of missionaries disagreed with such changes, arguing that they would lead the missionary endeavor away from its original objectives. For these missionaries, often referred to as “fundamentalists,” the provision of medical and educational services by missions was seen as a means rather than an end. This sharply contrasted with the later emphasis placed by “liberal” missionaries like Young J. Allen on the purpose and effectiveness of education itself. The divergence in missionary approaches sparked much debate among missionaries in China.

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. Gu Hongming (辜鴻銘) (Ku Hung-ming, 1857-1928), originally from Xiamen (廈門), Fujian, was born in the British colony of Penang (檳榔嶼) (Malaysia). He lived and studied in Scotland and Germany, mastering multiple languages including English, German, and French. He graduated from the University of Edinburgh in 1877 and subsequently pursued further studies at Leipzig University (德國萊比鍚大學) and the University of Paris (法國巴黎大學). After returning to Penang in 1880, he dedicated himself to researching Chinese culture. He returned to China in 1885 to assist Governor Zhang Zhidong (張之洞) of Hubei and Hunan in the Self-Strengthening Movement. While Gu promoted modernization, he was culturally conservative and staunchly defended traditional Confucianism, embodying a cultural conservatism that integrated Eastern and Western thought.

On July 21, 1891, he published an article titled “defensio populi ad populos or The Modern Missionaries Considered in Relation to the Recent Riots” (為吾國吾民爭辯：現代傳教士與最近騷亂關係論) in the North China Herald (字林西報), criticizing the actions and words of missionaries in China while defending the anti-missionary sentiments among the Chinese populace. Gu expressed deep skepticism towards the missionary objectives emphasized by Western missionaries, which he categorized into three aspects: (1) the improvement of moral standards. He posited that if missionaries could genuinely prove that their work achieved this goal, the protection under treaties they enjoyed would be well-deserved. However, he countered with a question: “It is a well-known secret that only the worst, weakest, ignorant, and impoverished denizens among the Chinese have been converted to Christianity. Who else, apart from these individuals, have the missionaries recruited?” In Gu’s view, these converts embraced foreign religions solely for monetary gain, consequently abandoning the faith of their ancestors and becoming a populace that disregards their own historical traditions while obeying foreign diktats. Missionaries must introspect whether the spread of Christianity has truly elevated the moral standards of the Chinese people.

  1. The second aspect is enlightening the populace. Gu Hongming believed that if the work of missionaries genuinely enlightened and illuminated obscure areas, then they would naturally earn the support of the Chinese people. However, he questioned whether what missionaries brought was truly the “scientific and educational content” they claimed, or merely “intellectual tricks” that were anti-scientific in nature. Based on his understanding of modern Western ideologies and the conditions of the medieval Church, he bluntly stated that missionaries stood in opposition to scientific principles. Missionaries proclaimed that the Bible was a divine book dictated by an omniscient and omnipotent creator; was this not a blatant contradiction to science? “Those who burned and persecuted scientists in Europe somehow present themselves as champions of science and enlightenment here in China. How strange and absurd is that?”

  2. The third aspect is charitable work. It is indubitable that missionaries invested massive resources in charitable activities in China. However, Gu pointed out that this commendable effort must be justified as worthwhile expenditure. In fact, if the money spent by foreign governments to protect missionary work were directly applied to medical and relief efforts, it would surely be more cost-effective. Moreover, of the millions donated by the people of Europe and America, how much actually alleviated the suffering of the Chinese populace? How much was spent on supporting missionaries and their families, or even on building beautiful residences and other miscellaneous and administrative expenses?

In Gu Hongming’s eyes, if the objective of missionaries was to improve moral standards and enlighten the populace, then this was merely a form of deceit and illusion. As for charitable efforts, they were, at best, a vast charity scheme designed for the unemployment of missionaries themselves. In other words, missionary work in China was a complete failure, fundamentally opposed to its lofty goals. Furthermore, with the support of foreign powers’ cannons, missionaries operated freely with a group of Chinese apostates (教民) across Chinese territory, “insulting and harming everything, apart from their pure clerical duties. This fact starkly highlights the roots of the Chinese people’s aversion towards foreigners.”

Gu Hongming’s critique of missionary work reflects a key factor influencing the evaluation of Christianity among Chinese intellectuals, primarily based on the conduct of missionaries and the behavior of Chinese converts (whom he labeled as “apostates”).He firmly believed that the incidents involving missionaries were actually the eruption of public anger triggered by years of accumulated insults and injuries; as for the rumors of missionaries killing infants for blood rituals, these were merely the ignition point for an already volatile situation. Therefore, he argued that missionaries should introspect and hold themselves accountable for their behavior, which had incited dissatisfaction among intellectuals and the populace.

  1. Yu Dongchen (余棠臣) (1851-), from Dazhu County (大足縣), Sichuan, was poor in his youth and lacked education, making a living by peddling coal. Between 1886 and 1890, several conflicts between local believers and missionaries erupted in Longshui Town (龍水鎮), Dazhu County, during which churches were destroyed. In 1888, Yu and his brothers participated; later, he played a leadership role in the conflicts of 1890, leading over 100 workers from coal mines and paper mills to kill 12 local believers and destroy more than 200 homes belonging to them. He issued proclamations stating, “Foreigners possess the nature of dogs and sheep, yet act with the ferocity of tigers and wolves…使司鐸又鉆衙門，顛倒是否…” (the latter part appears to be incomplete in the original text). Soon after, due to pressure from French diplomats to the Qing government, General Qi Yuan (岐元) of Chengdu and Governor Liu Bingzhang (劉秉璋) of Sichuan sent troops to suppress the unrest. Yu Dongchen retreated to Xishan (西山), which became known as the “Dazhu Church Incident” (大足教案).

In 1892, Yu descended from the mountains again, setting local believers’ churches ablaze, prompting government troops to quell the disturbances and capturing 37 of Yu’s followers, forcing him to flee once more. In 1898, Yu was arrested and taken to the Rongchang County Prison (榮昌縣監獄). Shortly thereafter, some of Yu’s followers rebelled and attacked Rongchang County, pressuring the county magistrate to release Yu Dongchen. After his release, he returned to Longshui Town and declared, “The destruction of the church in the 16th year of Guangxu (光緒) (1890) was not a private vendetta by Dongchen, but the public anger of the people…” The conflict led the consul and bishop stationed in Chongqing to urge local authorities to respond swiftly. Given the fervent anti-missionary sentiment among the population, officials feared that swift arrests would provoke even greater opposition. Therefore, they resorted to hiring assassins to secretly eliminate Yu Dongchen. Upon learning of this plot, Yu led his followers to Hebao (河包場) in Rongchang County in May, where they burned a Catholic church, killed two believers, and captured the missionary Pere Fleury (華芳濟) and a Chinese priest named Lei (雷某). Local officials responded, allowing Lei to escape.

At this time, General Gongshou (恭壽) of Chengdu and Governor Ren Xifen (任錫汾) of Sichuan had differing views on how to pacify the situation. Gongshou hoped to send someone to negotiate, while Ren advocated for a combination of suppression and conciliation. Yu demanded that all anti-missionary cases remain unprosecuted and that outsiders should not exploit the situation for leverage. However, both the French minister and the Eastern Sichuan bishop remained resolute in their stance. Yu felt that the government lacked sincerity, leading him to take preemptive action by issuing a proclamation outlining the longstanding foreign threats, the barbarism of foreigners, and the atrocities committed by missionaries, claiming they were rising in arms for the sake of the nation and the people, to defend their hometown and eliminate harm. In August, he led over 2,000 followers to re-enter the city, intending to burn churches and capture priests. Believers from various regions began to flee, and foreign residents in Chongqing retreated to boats in preparation for potential conflict. Due to the severity of the situation, the authorities decided to mobilize all available forces for a crackdown. Ultimately, Yu Dongchen was besieged, captured, and confined, while 3,000 of his followers surrendered their weapons and dispersed. The anti-missionary incident involving Yu Dongchen lasted for ten months, with his followers peaking at around ten thousand, impacting more than a dozen prefectures. Over 20 churches were destroyed, with casualties among clergy and believers. In the aftermath, the consul in Hankou demanded compensation of 25,000 taels for the foreign clergy and 400,000 taels for the church damages, which after negotiations was settled for just over 50,000 taels.

  1. Zhou Han (周漢) (1843-1911), styled Tiezhen (鐵真), was from Ningxiang (寧鄉), Hunan. He joined the military as a scholar in 1860. From 1882, he assisted in military affairs under the special commissioner Liu Jintang (劉錦棠) in Xinjiang. In 1884, he took leave to return to Hunan, residing in Changsha. At that time, Liu Jintang had plans to print philanthropic books to influence public sentiment, which Zhou assisted with, but fundraising difficulties led to its cessation around 1888.

During his time in Changsha, Zhou witnessed the increasing presence of foreign missionaries in Hunan and began writing anti-missionary articles for publication. Between 1899 and 1898, he authored and published over a hundred anti-missionary documents, most concentrated from 1890 to 1892. His anti-missionary writings circulated widely in Hunan, Hubei, and the Yangtze River Basin, significantly raising public awareness and facilitating anti-missionary actions. These documents appeared under Zhou’s name but also under those of historical figures, renowned officials, and various sectors, along with affiliations to Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism. Regarding content, Zhou’s anti-missionary literature primarily criticized Christianity from a Confucian perspective, addressing the aggressive nature of missionary activities in China, as well as the discrepancies between Eastern and Western customs.However, his anti-missionary methods broadly utilized vernacular language and spread anti-missionary ideas to the general public through lyrics, illustrations, and other mediums, such as “Gui Jiao Gai Si” (鬼叫該死) and “BiXie Quan Tu” (辟邪全圖).

Due to the substantial number of anti-missionary documents associated with Zhou Han, the British consul in Hankou requested that Hunan Governor Chen Baozhen (陳寶箴) issue an order in 1898 for the local magistrate of Ningxiang to apprehend Zhou Han. After Zhou was captured, he refused to confess, drawing significant discontent from Hunan’s local gentry. Ultimately, Chen Baozhen charged him with “persistent madness and incitement of public sentiment,” classifying him as insane and committing him to prison. In 1910, due to severe illness, he was released to his family’s home and passed away in 1911. Throughout his 12 years in prison, his anti-missionary stance remained unchanged.

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. Qing Dynasty Gentry Class (Lü Shiqiang 呂實強 1966; Su Ping 蘇萍 2001; Zhang Zhongli 張仲禮 1991; Ho 何炳棣 1964; Cohen 1978: 564-566)

In the 19th century, the opposition to Christianity was not an organized movement but stemmed from the gentry class of Chinese society. Studies by Ping-ti Ho (何炳棣) and Zhang Zhongli (張仲禮) indicate that the gentry was the backbone of Chinese society, serving political, economic, and cultural functions. They were not only the pillars of the bureaucratic system but also acted as a bridge within local communities, connecting the state and grassroots society. The formation of the gentry class was closely related to the imperial examination system; many ascended to official positions through these exams (xué ér yōu zé shì 學而優則仕), while those unable to enter the narrow doors of the bureaucracy could still wield considerable influence in local affairs and education due to their knowledge and literary skills. Locally, the gentry reinforced their social status through public welfare projects (such as building schools, constructing embankments, and repairing temples) and played a leading role in local governance.

Lü Shiqiang (呂實強) notes in his work “The Causes of Anti-Christian Sentiment in Late Qing China (1860-1874)” (《中國官紳反教的原因（1860-1874）》) that the participants in the civil-religious conflicts (教案) during the late Qing seemingly represented the common people; however, the true instigators and planners were often local gentry and intellectuals. The public’s expulsion, attack, or killing of missionaries and converts, and the destruction or burning of churches and residential homes of Christians, largely stemmed from the influence or incitement of the gentry. The anti-Christian pamphlets and posters that circulated widely at the time effectively stirred up general public hatred and anger towards Christianity, and these were also largely produced by them. It can be said that the gentry class, including intellectuals (士大夫), was the main driving force against Christianity in late Qing China. Lü also pointed out that many local officials, due to their responsibilities, had to execute protective clauses for missionaries and found themselves in opposition to the gentry, yet inwardly, they remained anti-Christian.

Why did the late Qing gentry oppose Christianity? Lü indicated that this was mainly due to misunderstandings and several other factors. Another reason for the anti-Christian sentiment among the populace was that Christian missionary work in China was intertwined with an aggressive nature, particularly as missionary protection was included in the unequal treaties, inevitably arousing national resentment. Specific issues arising from this aggressive nature included disputes over church properties, the peculiar status of missionaries, and the overstepping of attitudes and behaviors by missionaries, all of which led to profound discomfort among the populace. Moreover, the differences between Chinese social customs and Western practices resulted in misunderstandings and numerous rumors, contributing to the civil-religious conflicts. Finally, missionaries, through extraterritorial rights and protective clauses, transformed believers into a new class distinct from ordinary citizens in Chinese society, challenging the dignity and interests of the local gentry.

Su Ping (蘇萍) pointed out in her work “Rumors and Modern Civil-Religious Conflicts” (《謠言與近代教案》) that rumors were an essential tool in the anti-Christian movement during the late Qing, with the gentry and officials being the creators and disseminators of these anti-Christian rumors. Under the instigation of these rumors, the general populace directly participated in various civil-religious conflicts.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Tianjin Incident (Tianjin Jiaoin) 1870 (Tang Ruiyu (唐瑞裕) 1993; Zhao Shuhǎo (趙樹好) 2014: 21-26)

After Tianjin (天津) opened its port in 1860, the French Catholic Mission established a nursery in Tianjin to receive infants. Between 1868 and 1869, the Benevolent Church (仁慈堂) baptized 2,000 critically ill infants. The deceased infants were buried in a nearby cemetery at Wanghailou (望海樓), but when this cemetery became full, they were moved to a mass grave in Hedong (河東). This action raised suspicions among the local gentry, leading to rumors that the church was “kidnapping” children and “harvesting eyes and hearts for medicine.”

In April and May 1870, several incidents of child abduction occurred in Tianjin. Captured abductors confessed to using sedatives to kidnap young children. Later, rumors about child abduction spread rapidly, creating widespread panic. That summer, a severe drought hit Tianjin, and infectious diseases spread, causing a series of deaths among the children at the Benevolent Church. Sometimes two or three corpses shared a coffin. In June, wild dogs dug up the graves of two children buried by the Benevolent Church in Hedong. Witnesses reported that the bodies had decomposed from the inside out, with the chest and abdomen disintegrated and intestines exposed, leading to rampant rumors that foreign priests were using the nursery as a cover to abduct and murder children for medicinal use. Shortly after, one abductor was captured and, to evade punishment, claimed that the sedatives were supplied by the church, further inflaming public sentiment against it.

Local officials brought the abductor to the church for questioning, but as the abductor’s testimony was unreliable, the matter should have ended there. However, large crowds gathered outside the church demanding punishment. The French consul in Tianjin, Henri Victor Fontanier (豐大業), intervened, requesting that the Qing Minister of Foreign Affairs, Sheng Xuanhuai (崇厚), dispatch troops to suppress the unrest. Fontanier and Sheng disagreed on handling the situation, leading to a heated argument, during which Fontanier even pulled out a firearm but did not shoot. Later, Fontanier quarreled with the county magistrate, Liu Jie (劉傑), and shot at Liu’s entourage, injuring a follower, inciting public fury. Fontanier’s secretary, Simon, fired warning shots, and a brawl broke out between the crowds and the two men, resulting in Fontanier and Simon being killed by the mob, their bodies discarded in the river. In their rage, the crowd attacked the church, killing 10 nuns, 2 priests, 2 French consulate staff, 2 French expatriates, 3 Russian expatriates, and 40 Chinese believers, burning down Wanghailou Church, the Benevolent Church, the nearby French consulate, and four Christian churches operated by American and British missionaries, along with a Spanish Catholic church and a Russian Orthodox church.

The Tianjin Incident involved a significant number of casualties and church destructions, making it the largest church-related incident since the Opium War. It not only shocked the Chinese government and society but also prompted intervention from foreign nations. The envoys of Russia, Spain, the United States, Prussia, Belgium, France, and Britain jointly issued a note to the Qing Court, demanding that the Qing government handle the situation properly. At this time, British and French forces sent dozens of warships to the Tanggu coast. Later, the French envoy sent a note to the Qing Ministry of Foreign Affairs, demanding severe punishment for the local officials and perpetrators, as well as compensation of 210,000 taels, threatening war against China otherwise.

To quell the unrest, the Qing Court appointed Zeng Guofan (曾國藩), the governor of Zhili (直隸), to handle the matter. After an investigation, it was found that over 150 boys and girls at the Benevolent Church were sent by their families for nurturing and were not abducted. Furthermore, there were no reports of missing children in Tianjin at the time. The corpses buried by the church were all identified, and the allegations of priests abducting children and harvesting organs were deemed malicious rumors. Zeng Guofan defended against the false rumors and clamped down on the perpetrators, strictly prohibiting further disturbances, leading to the removal of the local governor and magistrate. Consequently, both the government and society perceived him as biased in favor of foreigners. Later, the court transferred Zeng Guofan to the governor of Liangjiang (兩江), appointing Li Hongzhang (李鴻章) as the new governor of Zhili to continue the investigation. Finally, the Qing Court compensated the families of the deceased with 460,000 taels, dispatched Sheng Xuanhuai to France to apologize, exiled local officials, and punished dozens of Tianjin residents involved in the incident. Thus, the Tianjin Incident came to an end. Although it concluded peacefully, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs realized the frequency and seriousness of such incidents, leading to the formulation of eight articles in the “Regulations on Religious Affairs” to restrain missionaries and ease conflicts between the faithful and the citizens. However, Britain, America, and France all disagreed, and church-related incidents continued to occur, escalating further, culminating in the outbreak of a widespread anti-foreign movement led by the Boxers (義和團).
