China: Agency (1900-1910)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

John Mott 穆德(1865-1946) was raised in a Methodist family in Iowa and got his BA from Cornell University. At Dwight L. Moody’s summer conference at Mout Hermon, Massachusetts, he was among the first hundred to sign the Student Volunteer Movement declaration. Mott led in the formation of the World’s Student Christian Federation in 1895 and became its general secretary (1895-1920) and chairman (1920-1928). He was also the general secretary of YMCA (1915-1931). As early as 1896, Mott visited China as the general secretary of the World’s Student Christian Federation. Later, he travelled broadly and lectured in China frequently together with Geroge Sherwood Eddy under the umbrella of YMCA. In 1910, Mott led the planning of the World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh and served as the chairman. After the conference, he headed the continuation committee. He came to China in 1913, and organized a few conferences which led to the China Continuation Committee. As a leading figure of world Christian movement and ecumenical movement, Mott had a large influence in China, especially through his travels and lectures. Mott also played executive roles in the foundation of World Council of Churches and was named its honorary president at the inaugural assembly in 1948. Mott was well-known for his bestseller title, The Evangelization of the World in This Generation. He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1946 due to his endeavor for international Christian organizations. (Hopkins 1981: 70-73)

George Sherwood Eddy艾迪 (1871-1963) was born in Kansas and graduated Yale College in engineering in 1891. Later, he went to Union Theological Seminary and Princeton Theological Seminary for theological education. In 1889, at the Northfield conference by Dwight L. Moody, he had a religious experience and was enlisted in the Student Volunteer Movement (SVM). He went to India in 1896 as a secretary for the SVM and YMCA. He helped found the National Missionary Society of India in 1905, and was the only non-Indian at the founding conference. In 1911, he was appointed as YMCA’s chief evangelist for Asia. Eddy visited China in 1911, 1913, 1914, and 1918. It was said that a large majority of Chinese Christian leaders were influenced by him. In 1931, Eddy finished his term with YMCA, and got connected with the socialist Christian group organized by Reinhold Niebuhr. In particular, he founded Providence Cooperative Farms in Mississippi. (Stanley 2000; Bays 2012:104)

Cheng Jingyi 诚静怡 (C.Y. Cheng, 1881-1939) was a leading figure of Christian union and indigenization in early 20th century. He was born in a Christian family in Beijing, and his father was a pastor of London Missionary Society (LMS). He got educated in LMS schools, firstly in Beijing and later for theology in Tianjin. During the Boxer Movement of 1900, Cheng’s family had a terrible experience, besieged in the British Legation for two months. In 1903, he went to England to help with the translation work of the union version of the Bible. Then, he studied in the Bible Training Institute in Glasgow from 1906 to 1908. After return, he began to work at the Mishi Hutong church 米市胡同教堂, which was associated with LMS and had an independent status. Cheng was involved in the campaign for religious freedom from late Qing Dynasty to early Republican period, and gradually won his fame as a Christian leader. In 1910, Cheng attended the International Missionary Conference at Edinburgh and made two speeches. During the conference, he highlighted that “the independence of the Chinese Church” is an urgent problem, and “a united Christian church” should be the end of mission work in China. Cheng made a strong appeal to the conference, to push a transformation from the “China Mission” to the “Chinese Church,” as well as from the “Church in China” to the “Church of China.” After the conference, Cheng became a secretary of the China Continuation Committee, together with Edwin Carlyle Lobenstine. He traveled across the country to promote interdenominational cooperation, and five regional conferences were held due to his efforts. When the National Christian Council was founded in 1922, Cheng was appointed as the General Secretary. In 1927, with the establishment of the Church of Christ in China, Cheng became the chairman. Cheng made difference between the “indigenous church” and “indigenous Christianity”: the former meant independence from mission societies and the latter implied a synthesis between Christianity and Chinese culture. In 1928 and 1938, he led the Chinese delegation to attend the international missionary conferences in Jerusalem and in Madras. He was the only Chinese who attended three consecutive conferences, and was elected as a vice chairman in the 1928 conference. Cheng became a Christian leader, due to his contribution to the indigenization and union movement in China as well as the most international prominence. Cheng died in 1939 at an early age due to illness. (Ng 2012: 133-142; Liu 2005)

Ding Limei 丁立美(1871-1936) was a well-known evangelist in early 20th-century China. He was born in a Christian family with his father as a pastor. From 1883 to 1893, he was educated at Tengchow College 登州文会馆, an institution founded by Presbyterian missionaries. He started theological study in 1896 in part-time way, and was ordained in 1898. During the Boxer Movement, he was put in jail for 40 days, and even got 200 rod-blows. In 1910, the Chinese Student Volunteer Movement for the Ministry 中华学生立志传道团was founded under the YMCA with Ding as the general secretary (1910-1923). They shared the motto: the evangelization of our mother country and the world in our generation. In the following decade, Ding travelled broadly across 18 provinces and had more than 200 lectures. Many were attracted to Christianity due to his preaching, such as the historian Jian Youwen 简又文 and the theologian Xie Fuya 谢扶雅. Ding thus won the tile: Mott of China. In 1918, he helped found the China Inland Evangelical Society 中华国内布道团, and went as far as Yunnan for missionary work. In 1923, Ding started to teach at the North China Theological Seminary for 8 years. He also helped found a Bible College in Tianjin. He died in 1936 at the age of 64. (Cha 1980)

Sun Yat-sen 孙中山(1866-1925) was born in Guangdong province, and later got educated in Hawaii and Hong Kong. He got interested in Christianity when studying in Hawaii, and was baptized in Hong Kong by a missionary from the Congregationalist church. Sun was an idol of the Republican revolution, starting with the Revive China Society兴中会 in 1894 to the United League 同盟会in 1905. He proposed the slogan of expelling the barbarians and restoring the Chinese regime. His vision was to build a modern Republic, with the principles of nationalism, democracy and socialism. Sun was elected as the provisional president after the success of the revolution. He had a lot of public lectures, to praise the contribution of Christians and to promote religious freedom. In the Republican government, there was also a large group of Christians or people who had studied in mission schools. Later, he transferred the power to Yuan Shikai 袁世凯, but rose another revolution when founding the latter having betrayed the revolutionary ideas. Sun also initiated the first cooperation between the nationalists and the communists under Russian influence. He died in 1925 with his mission unfulfilled. There was a debate about Sun’s funeral when some family members proposed a Christian one. The late Madame Sun, Song Qingling 宋庆龄, came from a well-known Christian family and got educated at the Wesleyan College in the United States. (Schiffrin 2020)

Xu Qian 徐谦 (1871-1940): Xu was born in Anhui province. He had traditional Confucian education, and passed the highest level of civil service examinations with a title of Hanlin. Xu had an expertise on law. He joined the Republican government, but broke with Yuan Shikai 袁世凯 due to the latter’s anti-Republican rule. He got connected with the Anglican missionaries and was impressed by their moralities, but he was not baptized until Yuan’s death. Then he was elected as the president of the newly founded Society for Religious Freedom 信教自由会. But he was more well known for the idea of Christian national salvation. Xu served in the Guangdong government of Sun Yat-Sen 孙中山, and became a very pious Christian. In 1918, he spoke loudly in the national meetings of missionaries and Chinese Christians in Shanghai and Guling, and got support from the second meeting. A committee for Christian National Salvation was established, with Xu Qian, Cheng Jingyi诚静怡 and Yu Rizhang余日章 as executive members. But it was delayed for the formal inauguration of the society, which also led to Xu’s complaints against Cheng and Yu. He had to take the sole role of organization. In 1919, in the context of May-Fourth Movement, a Christian Salvation Society was founded in Guangzhou. General Niu Yongjian was the main organizer, but Xu was elected as the president. A similar organization was also founded in Beijing, with Xu’s brother as a vice president. These twin organizations pushed Xu to work further in Shanghai. Finally, on January 1st, 1920, the Shanghai Christian National Salvation Society was inaugurated in the Moore Memorial Church. Due to Xu’s promotion work, it nearly became the national headquarters of the society. Xu proposed a tripartite idea of salvation – the person, the nation, and the world. For him, personality reform and political reform were both important; only Christianity could save the country. The idea of Christian national salvation faded in the 1920s against the background of nationalist revolution. Xu was criticized by both the nationalists and the communists while he tried to keep a balance between the two parties. (Ying 1994)

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

The China Continuation Committee 中华续行委办会 could be traced to the Continuation Committee after the Edinburgh Conference in 1910, to promote interdenominational cooperation in the mission fields, with John R. Mott as its chairman. Mott came to China in 1913 and organized five regional conferences to collect relevant information. During the national conference in Shanghai, the China Continuation Committee was formally established. Different from the former ones, there was an obvious Chinese participation in these conferences, with percentage from one fourth to one third. The organization did not want to start something totally new, but to push forward cooperation based on the existing work. The American Presbyterian missionary E.C. Lobenstine and Cheng Jingyi were appointed as secretaries. In 1914, A. L. Warnshuis of the American (Dutch) Reformed Mission was called to the position of National Evangelical Secretary. In 1919, the Committee launched the “China for Christ Movement中华归主,” with the Chinese Yu Rizhang余日章 (David Z.T. Yui) as the chairman. The program was to deepen the life of Christians and to win non-Christians to faith in general, and to strengthen religious education and to train Christian leadership in particular. In 1922, a survey with the same title was published by the Committee, with detailed information of mission work, education, social service and other fields, which became a comprehensive reference for the period of 1900-1920. The Committee also published a yearbook both in English and Chinese, entitled China Mission Year Book or 中华基督教会年鉴. In 1922, when the National Christian Council was founded, the China Continuation Committee finished its historical mission, and its work was merged into the new organization. (Latourette 2009: 669-671, 776-777)

Society for Religious Freedom 信教自由会: It was founded in November 1916 in Beijing, which continued the campaign for religious freedom in 1910 and the united petition for religious freedom in 1913-1914. Its aim was to retain the constitutional rights of religious freedom. A former justice minister and also a Christian, Xu Qian 徐谦, was elected as the president. Their basic approach was to lobby to the President and assembly members. In addition to the headquarters, one Catholic and one Protestant division was established, with Ma Xiangbo 马相伯 and Cheng Jingyi诚静怡 as the respective leaders. The Protestant division proved to be active in particular. They sent representatives to local provinces, and also invited local organizations to send representatives to Beijing. A newsletter was also edited to collect related information. Four basic ideas were proposed by the Society: religious freedom was a guarantee to end religious conflicts and wars; religious freedom was benefactory to the harmonious relations of five races of the Republic; the Confucian teaching was not proper to equal education of five races; religious freedom was a basic right for citizens of the Republic. It was due to their hard work that the article of religious freedom was kept in the newly drafted constitution, while the article of state religion was deleted. This became one of the few successful cases of Chinese Christians struggled for their rights. And it was mainly a Chinese Christian movement, while the missionaries just helped as advisors. (Liu 2012)

China Medical Missionary Association 博医会: The body firstly met in 1890. Subsequent meetings were held in 1905, 1907, 1910, and 1913. It was the pioneer national organization of modern physicians in China. With The China Medical Missionary Journal as a platform, it laid plans for the promotion of medical education and of public health, the preparation of medical literature, the fixing of uniform terminology, and the conduct of research. In 1914, the Rockefeller Foundation sent a commission to China to investigate the medical situation. The China Medical Board 中华医学会was organized as a subsidiary organization to which the funds were entrusted. The general plan was to train a medical profession and the Chinese personnel, with high technical and education standards. A new Peking Union Medical College was created under its sponsorship. Assistance was also given to the Hunan-Yale Medical School in Changsha and the St. John’s-Pennsylvania medical school in Shanghai. In 1925, under the request of the China Medical Missionary Association, it was merged into the China Medical Board. (Latourette 2009: 655, 744-745, 789; Tao and Wang 2014)

Christian National Salvation Society 基督教救国会: Christian national salvation was an idea proposed and promoted by Xu Qian 徐谦 (1871-1940). He had traditional Confucian education, and passed the highest level of civil service examinations with a title of Hanlin. Xu had an expertise on law. He joined the Republican government, but broke with Yuan Shikai 袁世凯 due to the latter’s anti-Republican rule. He got connected with the Anglican missionaries and was impressed by their moralities, but he was not baptized until Yuan’s death together with the latter’s emperor dream. Xu served in the Guangdong government of Sun Yat-Sen 孙中山, and became a very pious Christian. In 1918, he spoke loudly in the national meetings of missionaries and Chinese Christians in Shanghai and Guling, and got support from the second meeting. A committee for Christian National Salvation was established, with Xu Qian, Cheng Jingyi诚静怡 and Yu Rizhang余日章 as executive members. But it was delayed for the formal inauguration of the society, which also led to Xu’s complaints against Cheng and Yu. He had to take the sole role of organization. In 1919, in the context of May-Fourth Movement, a Christian Salvation Society was founded in Guangzhou. General Niu Yongjian was the main organizer, but Xu was elected as the president. A similar organization was also founded in Beijing, with Xu’s brother as a vice president. These twin organizations pushed Xu to work further in Shanghai. Finally, on January 1st, 1920, the Shanghai Christian National Salvation Society was inaugurated in the Moore Memorial Church. Due to Xu’s promotion work, it nearly became the national headquarters of the society. Xu proposed a tripartite idea of salvation – the person, the nation, and the world. For him, personality reform and political reform were both important; only Christianity could save the country. The idea of Christian national salvation faded in the 1920s against the background of nationalist revolution. Xu was criticized by both the nationalists and the communists while he tried to keep a balance between the two parties. (Ying 1994)

True Jesus Church 真耶稣教会: It was founded by Paul Wei 魏恩波 (1876-1919) in 1917, and was one of the largest indigenous churches in modern China, with strong Pentecostal characteristics. Wei was baptized in a church associated with the London Missionary Society, and had participated in the Beijing branch of the Church of Christ in China. He also contributed to the YMCA, the Salvation Army and the Methodist church. In 1916, due to an experience of divine healing, he got in touch with the Apostolic Fatih Mission of Bernt Berntsen. Wei received the baptism of the Holy Spirit, together with wife and son. In 1917, under special revelation, he began to write the True Testimonies of the Holy Spirit 圣灵真见证书. Together with some fellows, he had the whole-body baptism in river. It means the start of the True Jesus Church. Since 1919, he started the periodical Universal Reformed Church Times 万国更正教报. Two important figures joined the church as co-founders. One was Zhang Lingsheng张灵生(1863-1935), who experienced speaking in tongues in the Apostolic Faith Mission and got ordained by Bernt Berntsen. He joined Paul Wei in 1918 as a co-worker. Another is Barnabas Zhang张巴拿巴 (1882-1961), who got saved through Zhang Lingsheng and had the experience of speaking in tongues. In particular, Zhang took the information of True Jesus Church to South China, to make it a national movement. With a mix of “unitarian” Pentecostalism, Seventh-day worship, and intense millenarianism, its worship and behavior based on the gifts of the Holy Spirit, including tongues, and also on the believers’ receiving divine revelations during trance, the True Jesus Church is “very Pentecostal”. (Bays, 1996: 311-312) There was a division between the south and the north in 1924. They soon united again in 1931. At the foundation of the People’s Republic of China in 1950, the True Jesus Church claimed to have 1,200 churches and 125,000 members. Its leader later joined the Three-self Patriotic Committee, and it existed for a long time as a distinctive group. A headquarters for the True Jesus Church was established in Taiwan in 1975. (Inouye 2018)

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The campaign for religious freedom争取信教自由运动: It could be traced to 1906, when Chinese Christians in the United States proposed the right of religious freedom, to the Qing officials who went to investigate political system. In 1910, during the constitutional movement, Cheng Jingyi 诚静怡 petitioned in Beijing to add the article of religious freedom. A new campaign emerged in the early Republican period, due to the foundation of the Confucian Society 孔教会 and its proposal of making Confucianism the state religion. The Christians in Beijing rose up again and united the other religious groups to protest. Their main argument was that Confucianism was not suitable with the new Republic, and state religion was incompatible with religious freedom. There was a hot debate in the constitutional draft committee. And it was also connected with Yuan Shikai 袁世凯’s ambition to be the new emperor, with Confucianism as a tool. When Yuan died with his dream unfulfilled, the Society for Religious Freedom 信教自由会 was organized as a permanent organization in Beijing, with Xu Qian 徐谦 as the president, and Cheng Jingyi and Ma Xiangbo 马相伯 as vice presidents to represent Protestants and Catholics respectively. They lobbied to the president, senior officials, and the congressmen in particular. The Protestants proved to be most active under the leadership of Cheng Jingyi. After several rounds of discussion, the Christians made a great success that religious freedom was guaranteed in the constitution, while the article of state religion was deleted. It was one of the few successful stories of Chinese Christians struggling for their rights. And it also became part of the independence movement. (Liu 2012)

The China for Christ Movement 中华归主运动: It was launched in 1919 by the China Continuation Committee, with Yu Rizhang 余日章 (David Z.T. Yui) as the chairman. The general program was to deepen the Christian life of church members and to win non-Christians to the faith, to strengthen religious education in home, church and school, and to train Christian leadership. City-wide campaigns were held. The Movement was a call to advance in all phases of church activity. (Latourette, 2009: 776-777) According to the survey of China Continuation Committee, 66% of the missionary body, 34% of the Chinese employed force, and 24% of the church members, resided in the large cities with a population of 50,000 or above. The cities with the largest missionary communities were Shanghai, Beijing, Guangzhou, Wuhan, Nanjing, Fuzhou, Chengdu and Changsha. And the cities with the largest number of organized churches were Beijing, Guangzhou, Fuzhou and Shanghai. As regards the number of church communicants, the chief cities ranked as follows: 8,400 in Guangzhou, 7,800 in Shanghai, 7,702 in Beijing, 3,084 in Fuzhou, 2,236 in Changsha, 2,114 in Tianjin, and 2,061 in Nanjing. (Stauffer 1922: 385-386)

Christian National Salvation 基督教救国主义: It was an idea proposed and promoted by Xu Qian 徐谦 (1871-1940). He had traditional Confucian education, and passed the highest level of civil service examinations with a title of Hanlin. Xu had an expertise on law. He joined the Republican government, but broke with Yuan Shikai 袁世凯 due to the latter’s anti-Republican rule. He got connected with the Anglican missionaries and was impressed by their moralities, but he was not baptized until Yuan’s death. Xu served in the Guangdong government of Sun Yat-Sen 孙中山, and became a very pious Christian. In 1918, he spoke loudly in the national meetings of missionaries and Chinese Christians in Shanghai and Guling, and got support from the second meeting. A committee for Christian National Salvation was established, with Xu Qian, Cheng Jingyi诚静怡 and Yu Rizhang余日章 as executive members. But it was delayed for the formal inauguration of the society, which also led to Xu’s complaints against Cheng and Yu. He had to take the sole role of organization. In 1919, in the context of May-Fourth Movement, a Christian Salvation Society was founded in Guangzhou. General Niu Yongjian was the main organizer, but Xu was elected as the president. A similar organization was also founded in Beijing, with Xu’s brother as a vice president. These twin organizations pushed Xu to work further in Shanghai. Finally, on January 1st, 1920, the Shanghai Christian National Salvation Society was inaugurated in the Moore Memorial Church. Due to Xu’s promotion work, it nearly became the national headquarters of the society. Xu proposed a tripartite idea of salvation – the person, the nation, and the world. For him, personality reform and political reform were both important; only Christianity could save the country. The idea of Christian national salvation faded in the 1920s against the background of nationalist revolution. Xu was criticized by both the nationalists and the communists while he tried to keep a balance between the two parties. (Ying 1994)

Translation of the Bible into Mandarin Chinese 和合本圣经的翻译：The Protestant missionaries started the Chinese translation of the Bible in early 19th century. Robert Morrison in China and Joshua Marshman in India were two pioneers, with their translations published in 1822 and 1823 respectively. There was an early cooperation between British, German and American missionaries, including Walter Henry Medhurst, Karl Gutzlaff, and Elijah Coleman Bridgman. Their translation of the New Testament was completed in 1835. In 1843, at a missionary conference in Hong Kong, it was decided to appoint delegates from different regions for a joint translation of the Bible. Later known as the Delegates’ Version 委办译本, it was helped by Chinese scholars such as Wang Tao 王韬. But due to the different opinions regarding the Chinese translation of “God,” only a united version of the New Testament was published with the approval of all delegates. At the same time, there were many translations into local vernaculars, including Cantonese, Shanghai dialect, and Hakka, some of them just using Romanized phonetics. In 1865, a committee was appointed to translate the New Testament into Beijing Mandarin, including William A. P. Martin, the head of Imperial College of Foreign Languages同文馆, and Samuel I. J. Schereschewsky, a Jewish American missionary who had deep understanding of Hebrew. The translation of the Union Version of the Bible 和合本圣经was proposed in the missionary conference of 1890 in Shanghai. Three versions were originally planned, including two classical Chinese versions (High and Easy Wenli 文理) and one vernacular Mandarin Chinese version. The work was featured by collaboration and diversity. The different opinions regarding the Chinese translation of “God” were kept, with two versions of Shen 神 and Shangdi 上帝. Chinese scholars such as Wang Xuanchen 王宣忱 (aka Wang Yuande 王元德) and Cheng Jingyi诚静怡 also contributed a lot. Most importantly, under the influence of New Culture Movement and May-Fourth Movement, the vernacular version won broad popularity. Till now, after a few revisions, the Union Version keeps to the be the most widely used Chinese translation of the Bible. (Zetzsche 1999; Tong 2023)

Key Theological Issues

Precedence of liberal-fundamentalist conflict: Scientism was a label of the New Culture and May-Fourth Movement. Among missionaries and Chinese Christians, there was also the spread of higher criticism, social gospel and liberal theology, represented by Sherwood Eddy and the YMCA. This led to the countback from conservative groups, which preceded the liberal-fundamentalist conflict in the 1920s. (Yao 2003:36-48)

Anti-Christian Leaders

Chen Huanzhang 陈焕章(1880-1933) was well-known for the Confucian Movement in early Republican China, while Christians were the main opposite. He was born in Gaoyao country of Guangdong province. After passing the first level of civil service exam, he went to Guangzhou to study under Kang Youwei 康有为, a leading intellectual of the 1898 political reform and a promoter of making Confucianism as a modern religion. Chen won a title at the last national civil service examination in 1904, and then went to study in the United States, firstly at Cook Academy in New York and later at Columbia University. In 1911, he got his Ph.D from Columbia with a two-volume published dissertation, entitled The Economic Principles of Confucius and His School, which won appreciation of the Columbia professors, Friedrich Hirth and Henry R. Seager, as well as praise from top academic journal such as The American Historical Review. (Peach 2024) In 1912, Chen went back to China. He founded the Confucian Society 孔教会 in Shanghai, and petitioned together with other leading intellectuals to the new Republican government to make Confucianism the state religion, which got hard critics and protests from the Christians. The major reason was that the state religion was incompatible with religious freedom, which had been confirmed by the Provisional Constitution and the founder of Republican China Sun Yat-sen. However, when Yuan Shikai 袁世凯 wanted to be a new emperor, Chen retired to his hometown just as the traditional scholar-officials. In 1919, he established the headquarters of Confucian Society in Beijing, and in 1923, he founded a Confucian College 孔教大学. In 1927, he attended the World Conference on Religion and Peace in Geneva and was elected as a vice chairman. He also traveled broadly in Southeast Asia to promote Confucianism. In 1929, he moved to Hong Kong and founded the Confucian Academy 孔教学院, which later became one of the six official religions in Hong Kong and one major institution to promote Confucianism in mainland China with the symbolic act of donating statues of Confucius. (Han 2007)

Chen Duxiu 陈独秀 (1879-1942) was a leading intellectual of the New Culture and May-Fourth Movement, as well as a founder of Communist Party of China (CPC). He was born in Anqing of Anhui province and got traditional Confucian education. In early 20th century, he joined the group of oversea students to study in Japan, and got connected with some progressive intellectuals. Chen had also been an education officer in the local Republican government. In 1915, he founded the New Youth Magazine 新青年in Shanghai which became a flag publication during the New Culture and May-Fourth Movement. In 1917, he was appointed as the Head of Faculty of Arts of Peking University, around whom there were a group of progressive intellectuals such as Li Dazhao 李大钊 and Hu Shi 胡适. In 1920, he organized the Shanghai Communist Group, the pioneer of the Communist Party of China. At the first congress of CPC, he was elected as the secretary of the central bureau even though he could not make up at the meeting. His leadership continued to 1927 when the first nationalist-communist cooperation bankrupted. Later, Chen became a Trotskyist and a harsh critic of Stalinism, which led to a deteriorated relationship between him and the CPC leadership. He was even expelled from the CPC membership. In 1932, he was jailed by the Nationalist government and stayed in prison till 1937. However, he was accused by his comrades as a traitor to the Japanese 汉奸. Chen died in 1942 at the age of 63. As a leading figure of New Culture and May-Fourth Movement, Chen criticized religions in general from a scientific perspective, and proposed taking place of religion by science 以科学代宗教. He also criticized Confucianism as a symbol of the traditional regime. In early age, Chen admired the personality of Jesus, but his ideas shifted quickly to critics of Christian churches, due to his scientific and later Marxist thought. (Feigon 1983; Zhang 2013)

Cai Yuanpei 蔡元培(1868-1940) was a leading figure during the New Culture and May-Fourth Movement due to his position as the Chancellor of Peking University. He was also well known for the idea of “taking place of religion by aesthetic” 以美育代宗教. Cai was born in Shaoxing of Zhejiang province. He got traditional education and passed the highest level of civil service examination with a title of Hanlin 翰林. After the failed political reform in 1898, he left Beijing to promote new-style education in Zhejiang and Shanghai. Cai was involved in the Republican revolution, as the founder of Guangfuhui光复会 and the local leader of Tongmenghui同盟会 in Shanghai. In 1907, he went to Germany. After managing German language, he studied in University of Leipzig for three years, in the fields of philosophy, psychology, and art history. He returned to China after the Republican Revolution of 1911, and became the Minister of Education in the newly founded Republican government. From 1916 to 1927, he acted as the Chancellor of Peking University. During his term, he made a lot of reforms, well known for freedom and openness. And when the police jailed students during the May-Fourth demonstrations, he protested to the government and even threatened to resign. When the first Nationalist-Communist cooperation broke up in 1927, he stood with the Nationalists and had harsh critics of the Communists. But later, he co-founded the China League for Civil Rights to protest against Chiang Kai-shek’s dictatorship. In 1928, he helped found the Academia Sinica and became its first president. At the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese War in 1937, Cai went to Hong Kong due to his health problem an died in 1940. Cai had a particular concern of aesthetic education. He helped found the National College of Music (now the Shanghai Conservatory of Music) and the National College of Fine Arts (now the China Academy of Art, Hangzhou). Cai did not have a direct comment on Christianity. However, his slogan of “taking place of religion by aesthetic” had a large, enduring influence among Chinese intellectuals and students due to his prominent position and fame in the education field. (Liang and Wang 1995; Cai 2020)

Hu Shi 胡适 (1891-1962) was nearly the most prominent scholar in 20th-century China, with his leading role during the New Culture and May-Fourth Movement in particular. He was born in a prosperous family originally from Anhui and got educated in Shanghai. In 1910, he went to study at Cornell University and later transferred to Columbia University under the instruction of John Dewy. In 1917, he won big fame with an article on literal reform published in New Youth Magazine 新青年. Then, he was appointed as a professor at Peking University and acted as department and faculty heads for some time. During 1938-1942, he was the Chinese Ambassador to the United States of America. In 1957, he became the President of Academia Sinica, which moved to Taiwan after the failure of the Nationalist government. From an early age, Hu claimed himself to be an atheist. He also interpreted traditional Chinese religions from a humanistic perspective. During his study in U.S., Hu had some personal contacts with Christian students. He was touched for some moment, partly due to loneliness when he just arrived in U.S., and partly due to his admiration of the personality of Jesus. He soon shifted to suspicion and critics of Christianity from a scientific and pragmatistic perspectives. In particular, he criticized the transcendental and supernatural dimension of religions. Later, Hu also criticized Christianity due to its connection with imperialism. During the Anti-Christian movement of 1920s, Hu insisted that education should be separated from missionary work, and religious education should not be included in the curriculum. But different from other progressive or radical intellectuals, Hu insisted that Chinese should keep an attitude of toleration and understanding toward Christianity, and everyone has the freedom of religious beliefs. (Grieder 1970; Liu 2003; Zhang 2015)

Yuan Shikai袁世凯 (1859-1916) rose from the army and had been the supreme advisor to the Korean government. In 1898, he cooperated with Empress Dowager Cixi to put down the political reform movement led by Emperor Guangxu. He then was promoted to Viceroy of Zhili and also led the Beiyang Army. At the outbreak of the Republican revolution in 1911, he forced to the emperor to abdicate and won the presidency from Sun Yat-Sen. Later, Yuan was notorious for his emperor dream and the secret treaty with Japan. At the beginning, Yuan had a friendly relationship with the Christians groups. He had an open mind during the Boxer Movement, and protected the missionaries. He received the delegates of the China Medical Missionary Association at their triennial meeting and Sherwood Eddy during the latter’s meetings in 1914. Yuan was also a contributor to the YMCA. But due to his emperor ambition, Yuan supported the Confucian initiatives and restored some traditional ceremonies. The Christians protested against the Confucian movement, as well as Yuan’s break-up with the Republican government. (Tang 2004)

Anti-Christian Organizations

The Confucian Society 孔教会: It was founded by Chen Huanzhang 陈焕章 in 1912, a disciple of the iconic intellectual Kang Youwei 康有为 and a graduate from Columbia University with a Ph.D. Its main aim was to promote Confucianism and to save society. Local branches were established, together with organizations with similar name and function. In 1923, the Society petitioned to the congress to make Confucianism the state religion, due to its historical tradition in China. The movement coincided with Yuan Shikai 袁世凯’s ambition to be the new emperor, and thus got Yuan’s support. But it arose the fierce protests from Christians who struggled for religious freedom. Through several rounds of debate in 1917, the Christians won the competition. Chen continued his efforts of promoting Confucianism in Republican China. In 1919, he established the headquarters of Confucian Society in Beijing, and in 1923, he founded a Confucian College 孔教大学. In 1927, he attended the World Conference on Religion and Peace in Geneva and was elected as a vice chairman. He also traveled broadly in Southeast Asia to promote Confucianism. In 1929, he moved to Hong Kong and founded the Confucian Academy 孔教学院, which later became one of the six official religions in Hong Kong and one major institution to promote Confucianism in mainland China with the symbolic act of donating statues of Confucius. (Han 2007)

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

The New Culture and May-Fourth Movement五四新文化运动: It is a landmark movement of modern intellectual reform and Chinese nationalism. Broadly, it could be put between 1915 and the 1920s, with the May-Fourth demonstration in 1919 as a highlight. It started with an intellectual movement regarding literature reform and vernacular languages. Chen Duxiu 陈独秀’s New Youth Magazine 新青年became a main platform, with star figures such as Hu Shi 胡适 who graduated from Columbia University. When Cai Yuanpei 蔡元培 was appointed as the chancellor of Peking University, he led a reform of openness and freedom. A large group of progressive scholars gathered there, including the future founder of the Communist Party of China Li Dazhao 李大钊, in addition to Chen Duxiu and Hu Shi. In 1919, when China got unfair treatment during the Paris conference after World War I, a big demonstration happened in Beijing with the Peking University students at the front, and it soon developed as a nationwide movement. It marked the rise of nationalism in modern China. With the slogan of democracy and science, the New Culture and May-Fourth Movement had a long-term legacy in the political and intellectual fields of modern China. In the later stage, the leading figures divided between the liberalists such as Hu Shi and the more radical Marxists such as Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao. The movement itself did not touch religion or Christianity in particular. But, with the spirit of scientism and nationalism, it prepared resources for the religious discussion in 1920 within the Youth China group 少年中国, as well as the anti-Christian movement from 1922 to 1927. (Chow 1980)
