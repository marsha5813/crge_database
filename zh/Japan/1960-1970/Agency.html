Japan: Agency (1960-1970)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Summarize the key contributions/influences of each leader in the decade (500-1,000 words).

  1. Kitamori, Kazo (北森嘉蔵, 1916.2.1-1998.9.29)

  • Birthplace: 熊本県熊本市

  • Deadplace:

  • Education: Graduate from Japan Lutheran College(1938), Kyoto Imperial University(1941)

  • Kazuo Kitamori was a Japanese theologian, educator, and pastor of the United Church of Christ in Japan.

Kitamori was born in Kumamoto Prefecture, and baptized in the Lutheran Church in high school. Then he went on to study at Japan Lutheran College(日本ルーテル神学専門学校). After graduating, he became a pastor at the Lutheran Ashiya Church. In 1941, Kitamori graduated from the Department of Philosophy at Kyoto Imperial University and became a professor at Tokyo Theological Seminary in 1949. Concurrently he serves as pastor at the Chitose-Funabashi Church of the Japan Christian Church. In 1946, he published, “Theology of the Pain of God,” and it was widely read both Christian and non-Christian. After the war, even after the Japan Evangelical Lutheran Church separated from the United Church of Christ in Japan, Kitamori stayed in the church and worked on the reconstruction of the United Church of Christ in Japan, including the formulation of a statement of faith.

In 1946, Kitamori published “Theology of the Pain of God,,” which was regarded as a groundbreaking work of original systematic theology by a Japanese author (Yagi, 129). Kitamori arrived at the concept of “God's Pain” while grappling with questions about the essence of the Gospel and striving to grasp it with strict precision. This was the reason he became the first original theologian in Japan (Yagi 129).

Regarding “Theology of the God's Pain,” this part was already described in the section on Theology in the 1940s, so it is skipped here. However, this logic did not have a significant impact in the 1940s, when Barthian theology was highly influential. It was not until the 1960s, when “Theology of the Pain of God” began to be discussed in European theological circles, that Kitamori gained attention within this trend.

  1. Kensaku Iwai (岩井健作, 1933.8.1-2025.3.31）

  • Birthplace: Kamonomura, Kamogun, Gifu prefecture/ 岐阜県加茂郡賀茂野村出身

  • Education: Graduate from Doshisha University Graduate school of theology （神学修士）

  • Kenji Iwai was a pastor of the United Church of Christ in Japan and was involved in the anti-nuclear weapons movement and anti-war activities in Vietnam.

Iwai was born in Gifu Prefecture and was baptized in 1946. After graduating from Doshisha University, he went on to graduate school. While at university, he was actively involved in student movements. Later he became an pastor in the United Church of Christ in Japan and served as a pastor at Hiroshima Nagaregawa Church, Kure Yamate Church, Iwakuni Church, and Kobe Church in Hiroshima Prefecture.

One of the notable aspects of Iwai's work was the establishment of the Kure Christian Peace Association(呉キリスト者平和の会) after he was appointed to the Kure Christian Church of the United Church of Christ in Japan in 1960. In the statement announcing its establishment, he advocated opposition to the production and testing of hydrogen bombs, disarmament, opposition to the stationing of foreign troops, and adherence to the peace constitution (Isshiki, 133). Furthermore, this peace society became a regular venue for citizens to engage in study, addressing not only security issues but also labor issues, the imperial system, human rights, freedom in today's society (Isshiki, 134).

In 1966, Iwai was moved to the Iwakuni Church and organized the “Phantom Opposition Four-Party Alliance(ファントム反対4者共闘),” which brought together Christians in Iwakuni with the Social Democratic Party, the Communist Party, the New Progressive Party, and labor unions. This “Four-Party Alliance” continued until around 1968, but Christians later withdrew and moved closer to the citizens' movement (Isshiki, 135). Furthermore, Iwai and “Iwakuni Christian Peace Association” also formed an alliance with the “Be Heiren("The Citizen's League for Peace in Vietnam")).”
Iwai's peace movement was consistently viewed as part of the church's missionary activities (Okura, 124). He believed that the formation of the church community and the peace movement were inseparable. And, he identified the challenge of missionary work related to the church's significance in postwar society within the postwar peace movement that began in the wake of World War II, and actively participated in it (Okura, 124).
Therefore, Iwai can be regarded as one of the representative pastors of postwar Protestant missionary work who sought to redefine the relationship between the peace movement and the church within the postwar Japanese Christian Church, and endeavored to re-establish the church's missionary work as a practical endeavor grounded in historical and social reflection (Okura, 124).

  1. 土井辰雄（1892-1970)

Birth Place: Sendai, Miyagi-ken(宮城県仙台市）

Dead Place: Tokyo

Education: Graduate from Pontificia Università Urbaniana in Vatican

The First Japanese Archbishop of the Catholic Archdiocese of Tokyo (Archbishop),

later to become Japan’s first Cardinal. As a representative of the Catholic Church, he guided the Church through the difficult period of World War II and the postwar years.

From an early age, he was exposed to Catholic culture, and around 1901, he lived at the Sendai Seminary, where he deepened his experience of Catholic life. His father, Doi Bunjiro, was known as Japan’s first Catholic priest, and Tatsuo was baptized in 1902.

He assisted in religious ceremonies such as the Mass, which gradually nurtured his growing interest in becoming a Catholic priest (Sasaki, 136).

カトリック東京大司教区の最初の邦人教区長（大司教）

日に日本初の枢機卿となる。カトリック教会の代表者として、第二次世界大中及び戦後の困難な時期を乗り越えた。

幼少期からカトリックの文化に接し、1901年頃からは仙台神学校内に居住し、カトリックの文化に接してた。彼の父は日本初のカトリック司祭として知られる土井文治郎であり、辰雄は1902年に洗礼を受けた。

ミサなどの宗教儀礼の手伝いをつとめ、カトリックの司祭になることへの関心を募らせて行った（Sasaki, 136)

After graduating from high school in 1914, he studied abroad at the Pontifical Urban University (Propaganda Fide) in Rome. In 1920, after completing his third year of studies in philosophy and theology, he returned to Japan for his military conscription examination. In May 1921, he was ordained a priest in Sendai and was appointed to serve at Yotsuya Church in Morioka (Sasaki, 136). In 1934, he was appointed secretary to the Apostolic Delegate to Japan, Archbishop Paolo Marella.

In 1937, as the Archdiocese of Tokyo—previously administered by the Paris Foreign Missions Society—was reorganized as an independent Japanese-led diocese, Doi was consecrated as its bishop in February 1938. At the time, Japanese priests and seminarians were being drafted one after another, foreign missionaries from enemy countries were being interned, and it was becoming increasingly difficult to maintain church operations. In addition, the military requested the dispatch of clergy to provide pastoral care for Catholic believers in Japanese-occupied territories overseas, making this an extremely difficult period (Sasaki, 136). In these circumstances, Doi took on the role of unifying and representing the Catholic Church in Japan during the war.

In 1960, Tatsuo Doi was appointed Japan’s first Cardinal. During the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), he served on the Central Preparatory Commission, the Commission on Church Governance, and the Commission for the Revision of Canon Law. At the Council, he spoke about the situation and mission of priests in countries like Japan, where Christians were a small minority, and requested that these realities be given greater attention in the Council’s decrees. He also called for greater efforts toward Christian unity and cooperation with non-Christians, and emphasized the importance of traditional religious and ethical systems that had long influenced societies—such as Buddhism and Confucianism in Japan (Sasaki, 137).

In 1965, when Pope Paul VI attended the United Nations General Assembly to make an appeal for world peace, Doi participated as a representative of the six continents. In 1968, in response to the newly designated “World Day of Prayer for Peace,” he organized a joint peace prayer service at Tokyo Cathedral with representatives of Buddhism, Shinto, new religious movements, and Protestant churches (Sasaki, 137).

Yuko Sasaki, “Tatsuo Doi,” in Junko Inoue (ed.), 101 Religious Figures of Modern Japan, Shinshokan, 2007.

1914年高等学校を卒業後、ローマのプロパガンダ（ウルバノ）大学に留学した。1920年に哲学と神学の第三学年を収めた後、徴兵検査を受けるために帰国。1921年5月、仙台で司祭叙会され、盛岡の四ツ家教会の叙任司祭となる（Sasaki, 136)。1934年に駐日教皇庁使節パウロ・マレラ大司教の秘書に任命される。1937年、それまでパリ外国宣教会によって司牧されていた東京大司教区が邦人教区として独立するのに伴い、1938年2月土井はその教区長として叙階された。当時、日本人司祭や神学生も次々と戦場に召集され、敵国出身の外国人宣教師たちは収容され、教会の維持にも支障をきたす状態が進行していた。また軍からは海外における日本の占領地内のカトリック信者の司牧のための聖職者の派遣を要請されるなど、困難な時代を迎えていた（Sasaki, 136) このような状況で、土井はカトリック教会の代表として、戦時下のカトリック教会をまとめる役目も担った。

1960年、土井辰雄は日本で最初の枢機卿として信任される。1962-65年に開催された第二バチカン公会議においては、中央準備委員会、教会行政委員会、教会法改正員会の委員を務めた。また、バチカン公会議では、日本のようなキリスト教徒の少ない国における司祭の状況や使命について発言し、それらがバチカン公会議の教令の中でいっそう配慮されるように要請した。またキリスト教各派の一致や非キリスト者との協力への一層の努力を訴えるとともに、何世紀にもわたって大きな影響を及びした伝統的な宗教や倫理体系、日本では仏教や儒教を重んじるよう、発言した（Sasaki, 137) 　1965年、教皇パウロ6世が世界平和アピールのため国連総会に出席した際には、6大州の代表としてこれに参加した。1968年に「世界平和祈願の日」に呼応して、東京カテドラルで仏教、神道、新宗教各派、プロテスタントとの合同世界平和祈願を行った（Sasaki, 137)

  1. 日本キリスト教海外医療協力会Japan Overseas Christian Medial Cooperative Service, JOCS

  2. 日本キリスト教協議会（National Chrisitian Council in Japan, NCC)

  1. Summarize the key contributions/influences of each organization in this decade (500-1,000 words).☐

  1. The Japan Overseas Christian Medical Cooperative Service (日本キリスト教海外医療協力会, JOCS) is a Japanese NGO established in 1960. It is a Christian organization primarily engaged in medical activities in developing Asian countries. It is one of the oldest international cooperation NGOs founded in Japan. However, although it is a Christian organization operating overseas, its direct mission is not overseas missionary work.

This organization was founded in 1939 as the Japan Christian Medical Association (日本キリスト者医療連盟, JCMA) by Christians involved in medical work, based on medical activities that began in 1938 toward the Chinese people as compensation for the crimes committed by the Japanese military.

Following this, in March 1960, the Japan Christian Medical Association was established as the parent organization, and the Japan Christian Medical Association for Overseas Cooperation was officially launched. The first president was Morizo Ishidate (石館守三, 1901-1996). Based on the biblical truth that "it is more blessed to give than to receive (" the organization aimed to alleviate the suffering of neighboring countries and peoples, particularly those in Asia who had endured great sacrifices during Japan's wartime aggression. JOSC primarily dispatched Christian medical personnel to overseas countries (Nakamura, 222). During the 1960s, particular efforts were made to send medical personnel to Indonesia, Taiwan, and Nepal.

(1) Indonesia:
JOCS activities began in Indonesia. In 1961, Takeshi Umeyama(梅山猛) was sent to Bandung, Indonesia, where they expanded medical activities. In response to Ueyama’s call, Tamura Hisaya(田村久弥） also worked as an obstetrician and gynecologist in Java, Indonesia, from 1963 to 1966. From 1973 to 1978, he conducted medical activities at a hospital in North Sulawesi (224). JOCS did not provide one-sided support but emphasized the autonomy of Indonesia's Christian community and placed importance on “Cooperative Service”. As a result, there were instances where they could not meet the expectations of local Christian organizations that had hoped for medical supplies or financial assistance (Nakamura, 224). From 1978 to 1985, nutritionists were dispatched and achieved good results in improving children's nutrition.

(2) Taiwan:

JOSC's activities in Taiwan focused on medical cooperation for Hansen's disease patients. In 1962, Kazuo Saikawa(犀川一夫, 1918-200) was dispatched from the Overseas Mission Committee of the United Church of Christ in Japan to Taiwan to treat Hansen's disease patients. Saikawa worked at Nagashima Aiseien from 1944 to 1960, where he gained experience treating Hansen's disease.

(3) Nepal:

Medical activities in Nepal were carried out in collaboration with the United Nepal Mission (UCM). Notable figures include the activities of Mr. and Mrs. Iwamura (岩村昇, 1927-2005), who dedicated 18 years from 1962 to address issues related to disease in Nepal and poverty in Asia (Nakamura, 228). In particular, they established a regional healthcare system focused on tuberculosis control and other infectious disease prevention measures. Their activities were carried out in collaboration with the Nepal Joint Mission (UCM), to which Iwamura belonged at the time. UCM is an interdenominational international Christian organization. Initially, Christianity was banned in Nepal, and the Nepalese government disliked the introduction of Christianity into the country, requesting that the word "Christianity" be removed from the mission's name. The mission accepted this condition and proceeded with its activities (Nakamura, 228). However, while the mission's operations were conducted within the constraints imposed by the Nepalese government, the hospitals they operated were based on Christian principles. Nevertheless, direct proselytizing remained strictly restricted (Nakamura, 228).

中村敏『日本プロテスタント海外宣教史 垂松雅休からげんざいまで』新教出版社、2011.

  1. The National Christian Council in Japan(日本キリスト教協議会, NCC) is a major Christian organization that has existed continuously since the 1950s. The organization's establishment was written in the 1950s and 1960s, so it has been omitted here.

In the 1960s, the NCC's activities included peace movements opposing nuclear testing and the Vietnam War, and efforts to apologize to Asian countries and build peaceful relations. The NCC began exchanges with the Korean NCC, and delegations were sent back and forth between the two organizations. In September 1961, NCC issued a statement opposing the resumption of nuclear testing by the United States and the Soviet Union. Furthermore, in November 1962, four representatives from the NCC participated in the First East Asian Women's Conference held in Bangkok, Thailand.

One of the key activities that the NCC has focused on is fostering exchanges with churches in South Korea. In 1962, the NCC sent a goodwill delegation to the Korean Christian Church to promote peace between the two nations. Furthermore, in 1965, during a visit to South Korea by the Chairman of the United Church of Christ in Japan, Isamu Omura(大村勇, 1901-1991), who was also the Vice-Chairman of the NCC, the NCC entrusted him with a greeting message. Ohmura stated, “The Japanese Church deeply repents and sincerely apologizes for the numerous political and human rights violations committed by the Japanese government and people.” In 1966, the NCC established the “Japan-Korea Relations Committee(日韓関係委員会)” based on the resolution of the “Japan-Korea Lay Representatives Conference(日韓信徒代表協議会)” held in 1964.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

The 1960s were a time when the Christian community actively participated in social movements, including opposition to the revision of the Japan-U.S. Security Treaty and efforts to establish Yasukuni Shrine as a state religion. It was also a year when Japan resumed exchanges with countries in Asia.

In January 1960, a meeting of Christians protesting the security treaty revision was held in Tokyo, and after the gathering, approximately 1,200 people sang hymns and participated in a street demonstration (Suzuki, 451). The defeat of the 1960 security treaty struggle also impacted Christian churches, forming the Christian Peace Association, Japan Fellowship of Reconciliation(JFOR), the Baptist Church, and the Non-Church Movement. This conference was formed under the influence of the World Christian Peace Conference held in Prague in 1961, which was attended by Christian peace activists worldwide (Suzuki, 451).

Peace movements

Furthermore, in July, the First World Conference on Religions for Peace was held in Kyoto, with participants including the Christian Peace Conference, the Japan Fellowship of Reconciliation, and the Christian Women's Temperance Union. In September, the National Council of Churches in Japan issued a statement opposing U.S. nuclear testing. The Council of Religious Leaders for Peace in Japan (Shūkyōsha Heiwa Kyōgikai ) was formed with representatives from Buddhism, Christianity, Shinto, Tenrikyo, and other new religions in 1962. They sent a statement opposing the Christmas Island nuclear tests to the U.S. president. It reflects the interfaith dialogue that characterized the 1960s, with Christianity transcending religious boundaries to engage in such activities.　

Following 1962, various Christian organizations for peace were established, and meetings were held in several places in Japan. The All Japan Christian Peace Conference(Zennihon Kirisutosha heiwa kaigi ) was held in 1962, and in April 1964, the Japan Christian Peace Association(Nihon kirisutosha heiwa no kai) was formed. In July, the Second World Conference of Religions for Peace was held in Tokyo. In May 1965, the Emergency Conference of Christians for Peace in Vietnam was established.

Anti- Yasukuni movement

In May and June 1960, Christians held rallies opposing the ratification of the new security treaty, but right-wing groups and gangsters sometimes attacked them. In March 1963, the NCC issued a statement opposing the national protection of Yasukuni Shrine, and in March 1969, the Christian Bereaved Families Association issued a statement opposing the Yasukuni Shrine bill. The Japan-U.S. security alliance was strengthened, and Japan's rearmament was promoted. In 1965, the Central Council for Education of the Ministry of Education published an interim draft of "The Ideal Human Being," stating that "loving one's homeland, Japan, is the same as loving the Emperor." It was strongly opposed by the Japan Teachers' Union, the Socialist Party, and the Christian community, who argued that it had "strong parliamentary overtones" (Nakamura, 311)

Amidst such social circumstances, there was a growing movement to place Yasukuni Shrine under state control, and in 1969, the Yasukuni Shrine Bill was submitted to the Diet. This Japanese billstipulated that Yasukuni Shrine would be placed under state control, that the government would conduct ceremonies and events, that the state would be involved in appointing officials, and that the state would bear part of the expenses and provide subsidies. In response to this move, opposition arguments were put forward, mainly by religious and civic groups, claiming that it was a move to restore the prewar status quo(Nakamura, 311). Other religious groups, including Christianity, opposed the move, arguing that it would give Yasukuni Shrine special status.

Key Theological Issues

The 1960s, influenced by the Second Vatican Council held in 1962, saw the establishment of an ecumenical dialogue between the Catholic Church in Japan and the Japan Christian Council in 1966. This period can thus be regarded as an era in which ecumenical movements between Catholics and Protestants were promoted from a theological perspective.

The Japan Christian Council and the Christian Encyclopedia Tourism Committee published the Christian Encyclopedia(『キリスト教大辞典』) in 1963 from Kyobunkan. This dictionary was created to commemorate 100 years of Protestant missionary work. It is a dictionary compiled by the combined efforts of the Japanese Christian studies and theology communities, covering all denominations. In 1964, the first vernacular translation of the Old and New Testaments by the Catholic Church in Japan was published by Don Bosco Publishing. This Bible was the result of a project begun in 1947 by Federico Barbaro(1913-1996) and Aloysio Del Col (1920-1995), members of the Salesian order of the Catholic Church.

①Influenced by Billy Graham

Billy Graham's evangelistic crusades in Japan were held four times in total: in 1956, 1967, 1980, and 1994. Among these, the “Second Billy Graham Evangelistic Crusade” held in 1967 garnered particular attention, sparking debates over the pros and cons of Graham's invitation, which in turn led to controversies regarding the political stances and social influence of religious figures.

The Second Billy Graham Evangelistic Crusade began to take shape in the summer of 1966, with preparations for hosting the event getting underway. According to a Christian newspaper, Graham's side presented several conditions for hosting the event, including that the Japanese Christian churches would take the lead in evangelistic activities and that broad cooperation would be sought for the event's organization. The conference took place over 10 days from October 20 to 29, 1967. The venue was the Budokan (Tokyo) from October 20 to 28, and the final day on the 29th was held at Korakuen Stadium. The number of participants averaged approximately 15,000 per day, reaching 36,000 on the final day (45,000 according to the Agency for Cultural Affairs' “Religious Affairs Bulletin (18)”), with over 200,000 participants in total. Approximately 16,000 participants made resolutions at the event, but it has been noted that many of them dropped out within the following month. (『宗務時報 (18)、文化庁、1968年、10頁).

Regarding the conference, some Christian leaders at the time praised it as an “interdenominational” initiative, but the Christian community in Japan did not fully welcome or cooperate with it. In fact, the Japan Christian Council left the decision to cooperate with the conference up to individual churches, and it became clear that the cooperative framework for the conference was formed primarily by evangelical churches such as the Japan Evangelical Alliance.This event strengthened the unity of the five evangelical organizations and led to the establishment of the Japan Evangelical Theological Society in 1970.

Opponents raised questions about Graham's stance on social issues and his strong ties to the US government. In particular, Graham's anti-communist stance and his distance from the Vietnam War became the main points of contention. Yasuo Furuya stated in 1966 that “it is impossible to discuss the views of American citizens, particularly Protestants, on the Vietnam War without considering Graham's statements or actions,” highlighting the need to closely monitor U.S. domestic perceptions of the Vietnam War through Graham (Yasuo Furuya『キリスト教国アメリカ』新教出版社、1967年、p.184). Furthermore, according to the records of the Nishi-Chiba Church of the Japan Christian Church, the “Billy Graham issue” developed into subsequent issues such as draft refusal and the nationalization of Yasukuni Shrine (『西千葉教会七十年史』日本基督教団西千葉教会、1983年、p.109). Furthermore, among the opponents, there were concrete efforts to strengthen solidarity with religious leaders in Vietnam. The Graham International Conference in Japan (particularly the 1967 conference) not only influenced the development of evangelicalism within the country but also served as a catalyst for raising issues related to the conscience and social responsibility of religious leaders.

②Second Vatican Council

The Second Vatican Council was held from October 11, 1962, to December 8, 1965, in four sessions, with the participation of bishops from around the world. This council was convened by Pope John XXIII with the aim of modernizing the Church and adapting it to the modern world, and an announcement of the council was issued.　

Aiming to renew the Church in the modern world, discussions were held on liturgical reform, freedom of religion, ecumenical movements with Protestant churches, and dialogue with other religions, and the results were communicated to the world.

For the first time in the history of ecumenical councils, the participation of “observers” from other Christian denominations, including Protestant churches and representatives of the Eastern Orthodox Church, was permitted. This made the council “innovative.” As a result, the council had a significant influence on the direction of the Catholic Church as a whole, particularly in Western countries.

Following the Second Vatican Council, the Catholic Church in Japan gradually undertook self-reform, revising liturgical practices and adopting a more open stance toward other religions. In particular, efforts to engage in interfaith dialogue became more prominent, and the 1967 “Zen and Christianity Symposium(禅とキリスト教懇談会)” saw active exchanges between the Rinzai and Soto schools of Zen Buddhism and Catholic and Protestant Christians.

Additionally, in 1976, the Nanzan University Institute of Religious and Cultural Studies was established based on the spirit of the Vatican Council, and research activities focused on dialogue between Catholicism and other religions became more active. These developments can be seen as examples of how the theme of “Christianity and Other Religions” promoted by the Vatican Council not only advanced ecumenism in Japan but also led to the practical implementation of interfaith dialogue rooted in the regional and cultural contexts and realities of each area.

③Confession on the Responsibility during WWⅡ

In 1967, the United Church of Christ in Japan issued a "Confession on the Responsibility during WWⅡ." This confession was an apology for the church's actions during the war, when it endorsed and supported the war in the name of the church and prayed for the victory of the Japanese military. It was one of the earliest Christian denominations in Japan and served as a pioneer for other denominations. Furthermore, this declaration of war serves as one of the grounds for the United Church of Christ in Japan to gain legitimacy and trust from people belonging to other denominations and religious groups who are similarly engaged in movements for peace, anti-war, anti-nuclear, and anti-discrimination. （Isshiki、110）

Confession on the Responsibility during WWⅡ

The twenty-fifth anniversary of the founding of the United Church of Christ in Japan ( Kyodan) was celebrated during the Fourteenth General Assembly of the Kyodan held in October 1966. The serious concern for us now is “The Kyodan of Tomorrow.” Taking this as our theme, we prayed and reflected on the responsibility the Kyodan bears towards the future of Japan and the world.

It was indeed on this very occasion that we freshly realized yet again the mistakes we committed in the mane of the Kyodan at the time of our formation and during the following war years. We therefore seek the mercy of our Lord and the forgiveness of our neighbors.

In those years, the government of our country, out of the imperatives of waging war, demanded as a matter of national policy that religious bodies be consolidated and cooperate with the war effort.

From the opening of Christian mission early in the Meiji Era (1868-1912), most Japanese Protestants had long desired to dissolve the various denominations and to establish a single evangelical church in Japan. Therefore, the church leaders of the time, taking advantage of this demand by the government, entered into church union whereby the Kyodan was formed.

When we recognize that the Providence of God, the Lord of History, was at work in the formation and continued existence of the Kyodan despite our weakness and failings, we not only feel a deep sense of profound gratitude, but with fear, we painfully realize our responsibility.

The church, as “the light of the world” and as “the salt of the earth,” should not have aligned itself with that war effort. Love of country should, rather, have led Christians to exercise a rightful judgment, based on Christian conscience, toward the course our nation pursued. However, in the name of the Kyodan, we issued a statement at home and abroad in which we approved of and supported that war, and encouraged prayers for victory.

Indeed, even as our country committed sin, so we too, as a church, fell into the same sin. We neglected to perform our mission as a “watchman.” Now, with deep pain in our hearts, we confess our sin and ask the Lord for forgiveness. We also seek the forgiveness of the people of all nations, particularly in Asia, and of the churches therein and of our brothers and sisters in Christ throughout the world; as well as the forgiveness of the people in our own country.

Over twenty years have passed since that war ended; and we are fearful that our beloved country, set in today’s problem-plagued world, is once again headed in a dangerous direction. At such a moment, we seek God’s help and guidance so that the Kyodan may not repeat its errors but, rather, may rightly carry out its mission in Japan and in the world. Looking toward tomorrow, we hereby make public this resolution.

SUZUKI Masahisa

Moderator

Approved by the Kyodan Executive Committee, Feb. 20, 1967

Issued on Easter Sunday, March 26, 1967
