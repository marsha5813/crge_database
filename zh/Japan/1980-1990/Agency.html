  1. Seiichi Yagi (八木誠一, 1932-)

Japanese New Testament scholar, theologian, and religious philosopher

Born: Yokohama, Kanagawa

Born and raised in a non-church movement father, Yagi graduated from the Department of German Language and Literature at the University of Tokyo in 1955, writing his thesis on Kierkegaard. He then attended graduate school and studied at the University of Göttingen in Germany. During his studies abroad, he encountered Buddhism. He began to question whether the underlying principles of Christianity and Buddhism were not the same, leading him to depart from the framework of Christianity (Karatani) radically.

In 1960, he became a full-time lecturer at Kanto Gakuin University. However, due to his critical stance toward New Testament studies, he was asked not to teach in his classes and resigned from the university in defense of academic freedom. He then moved to the Tokyo Institute of Technology and continued his research. Yagi's research began with New Testament studies and, through dialogue with Buddhism, sought to explore the essence of religion at the intersection of Christianity and Buddhism. Until the 1980s, he appeared to adopt a position extremely close to Buddhism.

As a result, while maintaining a critical stance toward traditional biblical interpretation, his understanding of Christianity at the intersection with Buddhism was highly radical, often leading to criticism from the church as heretical. (He may not have been a Christian.)

He served as president of the Japanese Society for Christian Studies.

主著：Yagi, Seiichi. Pauro Shinran Iesu Zen（パウロ、親鸞、イエス、禅）. 初版．. Kyōto: Hōzōkan, 1983.

  1. Lee In-ha (李仁夏, June 15, 1925 – June 30, 2008)

a pastor and an advocate for the human rights of foreign residents in Japan.

Born in Yeongdeok County, Gyeongsangbuk-do, Korea, Lee In-ha was born in 1925.

At 15, he moved to Japan to study and enrolled in the second year of Toji Middle School in Kyoto. There, he met Wada Masashi and studied English and the Bible. In 1943, during Easter, he was baptized at the Nishida-cho Church of the Japan Christian Church. After graduating from the Japan Christian Theological Seminary (now Tokyo Theological University) in 1952, he became a missionary at the Tama River Mission in Chofu, Tokyo. After graduation, he served as a missionary for the Korean Christian Church in Japan, working in the Tama River Mission in Chofu. From 1955 to 1957, he studied abroad at the University of Toronto in Canada. During his time abroad, he began to reflect on the connection between racial discrimination in the United States and the history of ethnic discrimination he had experienced in his own life.

In 1959, he returned to Japan and was assigned to Japan's Kawasaki Church of the Korean Christian Church.

One of his primary roles was accompanying and serving as interpreter for Isamu Ohmura, who represented the United Church of Christ in Japan and the Japan Christian Council, during his visit to Seoul in September 1965. This visit was realized at the invitation of the Presbyterian Church of Korea and marked the first time that representatives of the postwar Japanese church spoke at the General Assembly. At the assembly, Ohmura apologized for Japan's wartime aggression and the sins of the United Church of Christ in Japan.

Another significant role played by Lee was his leadership in the legal battle against employment discrimination against Koreans in Japan at Hitachi in the early 1970s, where he served as a co-convener and co-representative of the "Support Group for Mr. Park." At that time, there was a strong trend toward "assimilation or exclusion," and many Koreans in Japan were forced to use Japanese names. This movement later led to the establishment of the "Coordinating Council Against Ethnic Discrimination," where Lee was appointed as the national representative.

In 1969, a kindergarten was established that accepted children of various nationalities, including Japanese, Koreans, and North Koreans, to break down ethnic barriers.

In 1983, Lee served as the representative in the fingerprint refusal lawsuit. Under the previous "Foreign Registration Law," foreigners residing in Japan must submit fingerprints for registration certificates. These were deemed discriminatory toward Koreans and Chinese residing in Japan, and complaints began to surface in the early 1980s. In this movement, Lee took the lead in the fight, and in May 1985, it led to the revision of the fingerprinting method for foreigners.

His church served as a refuge for Koreans facing discrimination in Japan and facilitated exchanges between Koreans and Japanese, playing a bridging role between the two countries.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. February 1981: Visit of John Paul II to Japan

During his visit to Japan, John Paul II visited churches in Tokyo, Hiroshima, and Nagasaki, celebrated Mass, and appealed for peace. Following the Second Vatican Council, the Catholic Church has actively pursued a course of action aimed at implementing liturgical reforms, religious freedom, ecumenical movements with Protestant churches, interfaith dialogue, world peace, human rights issues, North-South issues, environmental issues, and other social justice concerns. (The Christ Weekly, 107) John Paul II appealed for peace worldwide during his visit to Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The four-day visit took place from February 23 to 27, with 35,000 Catholic believers attending the outdoor Mass held in Tokyo on the 24th and 40,000 attending the Mass in Nagasaki. On the other hand, at the “Ecumenical Gathering” with Protestant representatives, which was described as a morning “gathering” with the Pope, 10 representatives from groups such as the Japan Christian Council were absent out of the 32 invited guests (The Christ Weekly, 106). Therefore, while the Catholic Church described the visit as an ecumenical opportunity, it was difficult to communicate with each denomination (background to be added later).

2. April 1981: Mother Teresa visits Japan

Mother Teresa, a Catholic sister who won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979, visited Japan on April 24, 1981. She visited Japan three times in the 1980s, in 1982 and 1984. Regarding the reason for her visit, newspapers reported, “Mother Teresa came to Japan, which is referred to as an ‘abortion paradise,’ to advocate for the dignity of life of unborn children” (108). This visit was influenced by the efforts of the Catholic-affiliated organization Family Life Association (FLA), which promotes family planning through natural birth control. She also visited Sophia University, held lectures, and visited the Yamate district in Tokyo, where many day laborers and homeless people live, witnessing their living conditions firsthand. The media widely covered her visit to Japan. On April 24, 1981, NHK television broadcast a program titled “Mother Teresa: The Woman and Her World,” which allowed many people to learn about her visit and activities.

Key Theological Issues

  1. The New Interconfessional Translation Bible (Shin Kyōdō Yaku Seisho, "New Joint Translation Bible")" was published, in 1987

In September 1987, after 18 years of work, the "The New Interconfessional Translation Bible" was completed and published by the Japan Bible Society, hoping to unite the Catholic Church and Protestant denominations in Japan. The creation of the New Common Translation began in 1968 and was produced by a total of 76 translators, including biblical scholars, literary scholars, and linguists (The Christ Weekly, 123)

This Bible has two distinctive features: first, it is a "common translation" of the Roman Catholic Church and Protestant churches; second, its translation philosophy. Regarding the translation itself, the "New Testament Common Translation" was completed in 1978, but this version differed from the 1978 version in terms of editing and translation principles. The 1978 version of the "Common Translation" was translated based on the translation theory known as "dynamic equivalence," which was influential worldwide at the time. This approach aimed to capture the meaning of the language accurately, employing a translation method that prioritized interpretation over literal translation. This translation was done to make it accessible to people outside the church. However, it received criticism for being too new in its notation and terminology and for deviating significantly from the original text. For example, proper names and place names were rendered too faithfully to the original language's pronunciation, such as "Lukas" instead of the established "Luka," which was unfamiliar and awkward (The Christ Weekly, 123).

Subsequently, reflecting on the idea that "the Bible should spread gradually to more people through its use in the church," the translation philosophy was revised (The Christ Weekly, 123). In the "New Common Translation," the formal correspondence method was adopted instead of the free translation method (Kotomo, 2).

As a result of this translation, all Christians and all Japanese people could possess the Bible for the first time. In 1987, the first Bible in Japan, the "Gullough Translation," was published, marking the 150th anniversary of the first Bible translation and the 100th anniversary of the publication of the "Literary Translation."

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

Aoyama Gakuin University Department of Theology to Be Abolished (Aoyama Gakuin University Theology Department Issue)

This term refers to the series of controversies that arose at Aoyama Gakuin University, culminating in the March 1977 decision by the Board of Trustees to abolish the Department of Theology in the Faculty of Letters.

The core issue was that the university administration, led by President and Board Chairman Kinjiro Oki, judged that the Department of Theology was not adhering to the university’s ideological management stance—namely, “Christianity” as the school’s founding spirit—and unilaterally forced the department’s closure (Arai, pp. 22–23).

During the 1960 anti-Anpo (U.S.-Japan Security Treaty) protests, the university president (who was also serving as president of the board) issued the “Three Proclamations” under the president’s name, banning affiliation with the national student federation (Zengakuren), political activity, and strikes. Some theology faculty and students ignored these bans and participated in anti-Anpo demonstrations.

In 1962, when some theology faculty objected to making “Introduction to Christianity” a required course, the university president reacted angrily. In 1966, a group of students, including theology students, organized a lecture by Shibata, which was held in defiance of the president’s ban.

In December 1968, Zenkyoto (All-Campus Joint Struggle Committee), which included theology students, barricaded the campus, demanding the withdrawal of the “Three Proclamations.” The university-wide faculty council passed a resolution to abolish the proclamations. The president stepped down from the university presidency, and in January 1969, the acting president negotiated with Zenkyoto and signed the “Six-Point Agreement,” after which the proclamations were withdrawn and the barricades removed.

However, in June of the same year, Zenkyoto again barricaded the campus, demanding the implementation of the Six Points. The president called in riot police, initiated a lockout, and resumed classes under a checkpoint system.

In 1971, the Faculty of Letters faculty council (led by a theology faculty member) approved the admission of two students who had been expelled from Tokyo Union Theological Seminary. The president refused to accept this decision, leading the university president to resign from teaching duties. A court dismissed the students’ petition for a provisional injunction to secure their admission.

In November 1972, the Board of Trustees passed a resolution to suspend recruitment of new students to the Department of Theology. In April 1973, student Naoto Umemoto filed a lawsuit seeking confirmation that the university was obliged to conduct entrance exams for the theology master’s program. In the second trial, the university lost the case for violating the principle of good faith.

In March of the same year, assistant professor Yukiko Yanagida and her husband Masaki were dismissed for criticizing the university president off-campus. In June, they filed for a provisional injunction seeking to preserve their employment status.

  • Aoyama Gakuin University Department of Theology to be abolished (青山学院大学神学科問題)

1977年3月の理事会決定により、文学部新学科が廃科されるに至るまで青山学院大学で起こった諸問題の総称。

大木金次郎院長兼理事長に代表される大学管理者がその管理イデオロギー（「建学の精神」としての「キリスト教」）に神学科が従わないと断定し、これを強引に廃科したことの当否にある。（Arai, 22-23）いわゆる６０年安保の際に、院長（当時学長兼務）が学長名で「三公示」（全学連への加入、政治活動、ストの３つを禁止）を掲示したが、神学部教師、学生の一部がこれを無視して安保反対デモに参加。１９６２年、神学課教師の一部がキリスト教概論を必修として学生に課すことに異議を唱えたのに対し、学長が激怒。１９６６年、新学科学生を含む学生の一部が芝田講演会を企画、学長の禁止にもかかわらず、強行した。

１９６８年１２月、神学科学生を含む全共闘が「三公示」撤回などを求めて大学をバリケード封鎖。全学教授会は「三公示」廃止を決議、院長は学長職を退き１９６９年１月、学長代理が全共闘との団交の結果、「六項目確約書」に署名、「三公示」は撤回、封鎖も解除した。さらに同年６月、全共闘が「六項目」の実施を求めてバリケード封鎖、院長は機動隊を導入、ロックアウト、検問体制による授業を再開。

１９７１年に文学部教授会（学部長新学科教師）が東京神学大学を除籍された学生２名の新学科編有試験合格を決定したのに対し、院長はこれを拒否し、学長が講義辞職した。学生２名の「入学資格を有することをさだめる」仮処分申請を地裁は却下。１９７２年１１月、理事会は神学科学生募集停止を決議。１９７３年４月、学生梅本直人は「神学科修士課程入試施行義務確認」を提訴、第二審で学院側は、「信義則違反」に問われ敗訴した。同年３月、福助手柳田由紀子と夫正樹は学外における院長批判のため　首され、６月「身分保全を求める」仮処分を申請、地裁はこれを認可したが、学院側は本そして勝訴、原告側が控訴した。（Arai, 23）
