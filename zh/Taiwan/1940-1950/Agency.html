Taiwan: Agency (1940-1950)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. 孫理蓮（Lillian Dickson）

Lillian Dickson (孫理蓮, 1901.1.29 – 1983.1.14)

Lillian R. Dickson was anAmerican missionary, social worker, and humanitarian who played a key role inChristian missionary work and social welfare development in Taiwan, particularly amongindigenous communities, leprosy patients, and those in need of medical care and vocational training.

Born onJanuary 29, 1901, inPelican Lake, Minnesota, USA, she attendedMacalester College. After graduating in 1924, her future husband,James Dickson, continued his studies atPrinceton Theological Seminary, while Lillian worked as a high school teacher before enrolling at theNew York Bible Theological Seminary (Liu Huihua, 1987).

In1927, the newlywedJames and Lillian Dickson were sent asmissionaries by the Presbyterian Church in Canada. They traveled across the Pacific by ship, landing in Shanghai before making their way toKeelung, Taiwan, where they began theirmissionary journey—ahoneymoon that became a lifelong calling (Su Guangyang, 1978).

Early Years in Taiwan (1927-1941)

The couple settled inTamsui in1929, whereRev. James Dickson served asprincipal of Tamsui Middle School and laterpresident of Taiwan Theological Seminary. He alsopreached among indigenous communities in the mountains.

During this period, Lillian primarily took on therole of a wife and mother, raising their three children. However,World War II forced theDickson family to leave Taiwan due to their American nationality. They were reassigned toBritish Guiana (Guyana), South America, where they continued missionary work, gaining experience inSunday school programs and outdoor evangelism—preparing Lillian for herreturn to Taiwan after the war (Tank, 1986).

Post-War Return and Missionary Expansion (1947-1950s)

InApril 1947, James and Lillian Dickson returned to Taiwan, with Jamesfocusing on theological education andindigenous evangelism. He spentweekdays teaching at the seminary andweekends driving a jeep into remote mountain areas to spread the gospel.Lillian accompanied him, dedicating herself towomen’s and children’s ministries (Li Zhende, 2005: 98, 111).

She once told her husband:

“I don’t want to be just a missionary’s wife; I want to be a missionary wife.”

To which he replied:

“You can do things informally that the rest of us cannot.”

During this period,many indigenous churches in Taiwan were established, largely due to the couple’smissionary efforts. However, Lillian sawan even greater need in Taiwanese society and sooninitiated her own ministries (Su Guangyang, 1978).

Social Welfare and Medical Ministries (1951-1960s)

-1951 – Lillian began publishingmonthly newsletters to update supporters in Canada and the U.S. She signed them with”Yours in His service”, emphasizing that donors werepartners in the ministry rather than mere financial contributors (Dickson, 2016). At her peak, she sent5,000 airmail and 20,000 regular mail letters per month.

-1952 – Responding to localpastors’ calls for help, she beganministering at Lo-Sheng Leprosarium (樂生療養院) in Xinzhuang,providing medical, spiritual, and social care for leprosy patients. That year, shehelped establish Sheng-Wang Church (聖望教會) inside the leprosarium and continuedworshiping with patients until her death (Liu Huihua, 1987).

-1954 – She formally founded theMustard Seed Mission (芥菜種會), now known as theMustard Seed Christian Mission Foundation, tosupport social welfare efforts, funded primarily bydonors from the U.S. and Canada (Liu Huihua, 1987).

-1956 – Concerned abouttuberculosis among indigenous children, she establishedadult and children’s TB sanatoriums, which later led to the founding ofPuli Christian Hospital.

-1960 – InBeimen Township, Tainan, she established the”Gate of Mercy” clinic forblackfoot disease patients. TheMustard Seed Mission also providedmilk, rice, flour, and clothing for those in need (Dickson, 2016).

-1961 – She establishedmaternity wards for indigenous mothers, starting inHualien, and later expanded them across Taiwan. These maternity centers helpeddeliver approximately 240 babies per month until1975 (Tank, 1986).

Vocational Training and Church Building (1958-1970s)

-1958 – TheMustard Seed Mission established vocational training centers in Hualien for indigenous youth, training over20,000 graduates in various trades. Nearlyhalf of the early indigenous pastors in thePresbyterian Church in Taiwan were alumni of these programs (Huang Wu-dong, 1954).

-1963 – She launched an initiative encouragingindigenous Christians to build stone churches, usinglocally sourced stone whilecement was funded by North American donors (Tank, 1986).

International Missions and Final Years (1967-1983)

-1967 – At age66, Lillianexpanded her mission work overseas, founding theBurning Bush Mission (焚棘海外宣道會). She partnered with thePresbyterian Church in Taiwan, sendingeight indigenous pastors toSarawak, Malaysia, for mission work.

Lillian Dicksonpassed away on January 14, 1983, after51 years of missionary, charitable, and educational service in Taiwan. She diedwhile actively working and wasburied alongside her husband at Taiwan Theological Seminary inLingtou, Taipei (Su Guangyang, 1978).

Legacy

Lillian Dickson wasone of the most influential missionaries in Taiwan’s history. Her workbridged evangelism and social action, addressingthe physical, educational, and spiritual needs of Taiwanese communities—especiallyindigenous groups, leprosy patients, and the impoverished. Through herMustard Seed Mission, sheempowered thousands throughmedical care, education, and vocational training, shapingTaiwan’s Christian welfare landscape for generations.

  1. 孫雅各

Rev. James Ira Dickson (孫雅各, 1900.2.23 – 1967.6.15)

James Ira Dickson was anAmerican Presbyterian missionary, theologian, and educator who played apivotal role in theological education and indigenous missions in Taiwan. His work significantly impacted theTaiwan Theological Seminary, indigenous church development, and social welfare initiatives.

Early Life and Arrival in Taiwan (1927-1940)

Born inSouth Dakota, USA,Dickson graduated from Princeton Theological Seminary in 1927. That same year, he marriedLillian Dickson (孫理蓮).

During this period, theUnited Church of Canada movement causeddivisions within the Canadian Presbyterian Church, leading toa shortage of missionaries. Although he was American,Dickson was deeply influenced by the Student Volunteer Movement for Foreign Missions. InOctober 1927, he accepted amissionary appointment from the Presbyterian Church in Canada and was sent toTaiwan.

In Taiwan, Dickson served invarious leadership roles:

-Acting Principal of Tamsui Middle School (淡水中學)

-President of Taipei Theological Seminary (now Taiwan Theological Seminary, 台灣神學院)

-President of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan Bible College

However,in 1940, during thefinal years of World War II,the Japanese Governor-General’s Office pressured American missionaries to leave Taiwan. The Dickson family relocated toBritish Guiana (Guyana), South America, where they continuedmissionary work and theological education (Deng Xiangyang, 2003: 82).

Post-War Return and Indigenous Missions (1946-1960s)

After World War II, the Dicksonsreturned to Taiwan in 1946. That same year, James Dicksontraveled to Shanghai to invite Mennonite medical missionaries to serve Taiwan’s indigenous communities. As a result, in1947, theMennonite Central Committee (MCC) dispatched doctors and nurses, forming theMennonite Mountain Medical Team (Huang Wenjiang, Zhang Yunkai, Chen Zhiheng, 2015: 346).

In1948, Dickson resumed his role asPresident of Taiwan Theological Seminary, where he led significant institutional changes. His mostcontroversial yet impactful decision was torelocate the seminary from Taipei’s Shuanglian district to Lingtou, Yangmingshan in1955, despite opposition. Over the nextten years, he oversaw the development of the new campus, completing the facilities by1965, before retiring.

Church Renewal and Indigenous Evangelism

Beyond theological education, Dickson was akey figure in Taiwan’s church renewal movement, particularly among young Presbyterian leaders in northern Taiwan.

Hismost renowned contribution was hisdedication to indigenous missionary work.

  • InJune 1946, he visitedHualien’s mountain churches, marking the beginning ofhis deep engagement with indigenous evangelism.

  • Over the years, throughfundraising from local and international sources, he helpedbuild or renovate 385 churches, transforming many frombamboo structures to stone and concrete buildings.

Establishment of Yushan Theological Seminary (玉山神學院)

During theearly years of Yushan Theological Seminary,financial constraints threatened its survival.Dickson and President Huang Zhanghui (黃彰輝) activelysecured international funding, ultimately ensuring the seminary’s establishment as a center forindigenous theological education.

Support for Social Welfare and Medical Missions

ThroughPresbyterian Church resources, Dicksonsupported his wife, Lillian Dickson, in hersocial welfare work, including:

-Medical and relief programs in Taiwan’s mountain regions

-Expansion of healthcare access in indigenous communities

During thepost-war period, asurge in foreign missionaries and resources led to therapid growth of Mandarin-speaking churches (國語教會). Dickson played acrucial mediating role, helpingintegrate foreign mission efforts with local church initiatives.

Final Years and Legacy

In1967, while in the United States,Dickson was diagnosed with a brain tumor. Heimmediately returned to Taiwan, where hepassed away in June at his home in Lingtou. He was buried atTaiwan Theological Seminary, where he had devoted much of his life to theological education.

After his passing,Lillian Dickson carried on his mission work. In1968, she founded theBurning Bush Mission (焚棘海外宣道會), which sentTaiwanese indigenous missionaries toSarawak, Malaysia, marking the firstoverseas indigenous mission initiative from Taiwan (Indigenous Missions Committee of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, 1998: 297).

Conclusion

James Dickson was atransformational leader in Taiwan’s Christian history. Hispioneering work in theological education, indigenous evangelism, and church renewal left alasting legacy. Through hisinstitution-building, missionary efforts, and advocacy for indigenous ministry, he helpedshape Taiwan’s Presbyterian Church and strengthen its global connections.

3.楊美齋

Rev. Vang Mei-Chai (楊美齋, 1889.9.18 – 1961.2.23)

Rev.Vang Mei-Chai was aChinese Baptist missionary and church planter, recognized as thepioneer of Baptist ministry in Taiwan. His efforts were instrumental inestablishing the Baptist Church in Taiwan andtraining local Christian leaders.

Early Life and Ministry in China (1889-1948)

Born inMaguo Village, Shandong, China,Vang Mei-Chai received atraditional education in village schools before entering thePingdu Baptist Mission Middle School at age25. In1913, he enrolled at theBaptist Theological Seminary in Huang County, Shandong, graduating in1916.

After graduation, heserved as a missionary inNortheastern China and Shandong. Beginning in1920, he spenttwelve years establishingtwelve churches acrossHarbin, Anda, Lindian, Mingshui, Ang’angxi, Qiqihar, Fulaerji, Zalantun, Jiamusi, Suifenhe, Boketu, and Manzhouli. His pioneering efforts helpedexpand Baptist mission work in Manchuria.

In1936, at theCentennial Celebration of Baptist Missions in China in Guangzhou, he became an advocate forfrontier evangelism. The following year, during theFirst National Evangelistic Conference,21 pastors committed to frontier missions, includingVang Mei-Chai.

In1948, theChinese Baptist Convention convened afrontier missions conference in Shanghai, where it was decided tosend Rev. Vang Mei-Chai to Taiwan to begin Baptist work. After aone-month exploratory trip, he returned to Shanghai and receivedofficial approval to startBaptist missions in Taiwan.

Pioneering Baptist Missions in Taiwan (1949-1961)

OnFebruary 4, 1949,Vang Mei-Chai arrived in Taiwan, landing inKeelung with his family. Over the next13 years, helaid the foundation for the Baptist Church in Taiwan, earning him the title”Pioneer of Baptist Ministry in Taiwan”.

-1949 – Established theRen-Ai Baptist Church (仁愛堂), thefirst Baptist church in Taiwan.

-1951 – Initiatedhouse church gatherings inShulin, Hsinchu, which later developed intoHsinchu Baptist Church (established onDecember 16, 1951).

-1951 – Opened theXiamen Street Gospel Center in Taipei.

-1952 – Played a key role in the founding of theTaiwan Baptist Theological Seminary (台灣浸信會神學院), established by theSouthern Baptist Mission in Taiwan.

  • The seminary’s first president wasRev. Charles L. Culpepper (柯理培, 1895-1986).

  • Due to ashortage of faculty,Vang Mei-Chai served as aprofessor of Old Testament, training Taiwan’s early Baptist pastors.

  • Along withRev. Culpepper andMiss Bertha Smith (明俊德, arrived in 1948), he became one of thethree most influential early figures in Taiwan’s Baptist history (Wu Wenren, 1998: 49, 50, 233).

Final Years and Legacy

OnFebruary 23, 1961,Vang Mei-Chai passed away from liver disease and was buried in theBitan Mountain area of Xindian, New Taipei City.

At hismemorial service,Rev. Charles Culpepper honored him with the words:

“A faithful servant of the Lord throughout his life.”

Significance in Taiwan’s Christian History

Rev. Vang Mei-Chai’s contributions toTaiwan’s Baptist movement were foundational. He:

-Planted Taiwan’s first Baptist churches.

-Trained local pastors and church leaders.

-Strengthened theological education throughTaiwan Baptist Theological Seminary.

Hisevangelistic zeal, leadership, and dedication helpedestablish a lasting Baptist presence in Taiwan, shaping thegrowth of Baptist Christianity on the island.

李常受

Witness Lee (李常受, 1905.9.3 – 1997.6.9)

Witness Lee was aChinese Christian leader, preacher, and theologian, known for his leadership in theLocal Church Movement (召會/地方召會), as well as forestablishing Living Stream Ministry (水流職事站). As thesuccessor to Watchman Nee (倪柝聲), he played akey role in expanding the movement across China, Taiwan, and the United States.

Early Life and Ministry in China (1905-1949)

Born inNorth China, Lee was raised in aChristian family, with his mother being a devoted believer. Hisearly education was sporadic, but he attendedAmerican Presbyterian Church’s Yiwen Business School (美北長老教會益文商專), where hecommitted to full-time ministry at the age of 19.

After encounteringWatchman Nee (倪柝聲), Lee became deeply influenced by his teachings. He worked as amissionary in Yantai and Shanghai, establishingLocal Churches in Northern China, including in his hometown ofYantai, Shandong (Lee, 2005: 48-50).

In1934, Lee moved toShanghai, where he beganworking closely with Watchman Nee. That same year, Neeentrusted him with leading the Shanghai Gospel Book Room (上海福音書房), the publishing branch of the movement. Over the next15 years, he ministered inNanjing, Shanghai, South China, and beyond, planting and strengthening Local Churches across China (Lee, 2005: 181-182).

By1949, as theChinese Communist Party took control of China, Watchman Nee and other leaders sentLee to Taiwan to continue the work (Lee, 2005: 227-230).

Establishing the Local Church in Taiwan (1949-1962)

Arriving in Taiwan with only350 believers, Lee oversawrapid church growth—withinfive years, membershipgrew to 20,000, leading to the global expansion of theLocal Church Movement.

Key developments during this period included:

-Founding the Taiwan Gospel Book Room (台灣福音書房) to publish Christian literature.

  • ExpandingLocal Churches (地方召會) throughout Taiwan.

Doctrinal Disputes and Church Splits (1957-1965)

In1957, Lee had atheological disagreement withTheodore Austin-Sparks (史百克, 1888-1971), a leader of theInner Life Movement, regardingchurch structure and authority. This led to asplit in the Local Church in Taiwan (Lee, 2005: 32).

Further challenges to his leadership emerged:

-1961 – TheManila Local Church split, questioning his authority.

-1965 – Disputes overLee’s leadership led to furthercontroversies in Hong Kong (Lee, 2005: 84-88).

Expansion to the United States and Global Influence (1962-1997)

In1962, Lee moved toLos Angeles, USA, launching a35-year evangelical mission.

-1965 – He foundedLiving Stream Ministry (水流職事站) inAnaheim, California, becoming the official publishing house forNee and Lee’s teachings.

-1965-1987 – Leespent 22 years writing Life-Study of the Bible (生命讀經), acomprehensive biblical commentary spanning 25,000+ pages in 89 volumes and 1,984 messages.

-1987 – He completed theChinese Recovery Version of the New Testament (恢復本聖經) and supervised itsEnglish translation.

Later Years and Controversies (1987-1997)

In his later years, Leereturned to Taiwan to promote the”New Way” (新路運動, also called “God’s Ordained Way”/神命定之路), aiming torestructure the church and institutionalize leadership training. However, this movement facedresistance, particularly regardinghis handling of moral issues involving his son, Lee Meng-ze (李蒙澤).

Despite these challenges, Lee continuedpreaching and writing. Even at91 years old (February 1997), he delivered public sermons atspecial conferences.

OnJune 9, 1997,Witness Lee passed away in Anaheim, California, at the age of 92.

Legacy

Lee left a lasting impact onChinese Christianity, particularly through:

1.The Global Expansion of the Local Church Movement

2.The Establishment of Living Stream Ministry

3.His Extensive Biblical Commentaries and Doctrinal Teachings

Despitetheological controversies and organizational disputes, his work continues toshape evangelical movements in China, Taiwan, and beyond.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

  1. 日本基督教團

The United Church of Christ in Japan (日本基督教團, Nihon Kirisuto Kyōdan)

TheUnited Church of Christ in Japan (UCCJ) wasa wartime state-enforced union of Protestant denominations heavily influenced byJapanese militarism and nationalism during World War II. Established under theReligious Organizations Law (宗教組織法) onJune 24, 1941, it forcibly merged33 Protestant denominations into a single national church underwartime government control.

Formation and Early Influence (1940-1941)

  • OnOctober 17, 1940, anationwide Christian assembly in Japan announced theunification of churches.

  • Thefounding conference was held atFujimicho Church, originally founded byUemura Masahisa (上村正久).

Christian Missions in Taiwan Under Japanese Rule

At the beginning ofJapanese colonial rule (1895-1945), Japanese Protestant groupsagreed to conduct missions in Taiwan without denominational divisions. Theyfounded the “All Christian Unity Society” (基督信徒一致會) to coordinatemissionary efforts.Japanese pastors arrived in Taiwan and established key churches such as:

台灣聖公會

神召會

浸信會

Christian Denominations That Came to Taiwan in 1949

In October 1949, during the Chinese Civil War, the Kuomintang (KMT) was at a disadvantage. After the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) gained control of mainland China, the Republic of China (ROC) government retreated to Taiwan. The CCP’s atheistic policies led many churches from mainland China to relocate to Taiwan, contributing to the current diversity of religious denominations in Taiwan.

#Taiwan Episcopal Church

In 1897, the Nippon Sei Ko Kai (Anglican Church in Japan) sent its first itinerant pastor, Minagawa Akio, to Taiwan. From 1905 to 1945, four Anglican churches were established in Taipei, Taichung, Tainan, and Yilan, primarily serving Japanese congregants, who numbered nearly a thousand. After World War II, the Japanese Anglican Church withdrew from Taiwan. In 1948, Chinese Anglican believers who had followed the Nationalist government to Taiwan began gathering for worship in private homes. In 1954, the American Episcopal Church designated the Bishop of the Missionary District of Honolulu, the Rt. Rev. Harry S. Kennedy, to oversee church affairs in Taiwan, leading to the establishment of St. John’s Church in Taipei. In 1961, the 60th General Convention of the Episcopal Church in the United States resolved to establish the Missionary District of Taiwan.

#Assemblies of God

After World War II, in 1945, representatives of Assemblies of God churches across China gathered in Wuhan, Hubei, to form the General Council of the Assemblies of God in China. In 1948, amid deteriorating conditions in the Chinese Civil War, two missionaries came to Taiwan to explore the possibility of establishing a mission in Taipei. Within six months, 20 new believers were baptized, marking the beginning of the Taipei Assemblies of God Church. Soon after, other missionaries who had evacuated from China also arrived in Taiwan to continue their ministry.

#Baptist Church

In June 1948, the “National Baptist Frontier Mission Committee” sent Pastor Yang Meizhai to Taiwan for an assessment. The Southern Baptist Convention also dispatched missionary Miss Bertha Smith from Shandong via Shanghai to begin work in Taiwan. In November, Pastor Yang Meizhai was elected as the regional secretary for Taiwan, and by the end of the year, worship services had already started at Miss Smith’s residence. The following year, after attending the National Baptist Convention in Shanghai, Pastor Yang returned to Taiwan with his family in February. On August 28, 1949, Taiwan’s first Baptist church, the “Chinese Baptist Church in Taiwan,” was officially established. The Southern Baptist Convention missionaries who had withdrawn from mainland China in 1948 played a crucial role in supporting and assisting the early development of Baptist churches across Taiwan.

地方教會禮拜堂

召會（assembly）

Local Churches and Meeting Halls

#Taipei Mandarin Worship Hall

After World War II ended in 1945, General Fan Songyao was assigned to Taiwan to accept the surrender of the Japanese forces. As a devout Christian, General Fan noticed an increasing number of people, including Christians, arriving in Taiwan. However, many existing churches primarily conducted services in Taiwanese, creating difficulties for Mandarin-speaking believers. To address this, he collaborated with Mr. Tang Shouqian and Ms. Li Zhongxianjing to establish a Mandarin worship service at the Xu Chang Street YMCA conference hall. Over time, this gathering evolved into what is now the Taipei Mandarin Worship Hall.

In early 1946, Wu Yong, while passing by the Xu Chang Street Mandarin Worship Hall, was drawn in by the sound of hymns. Deeply moved, he converted to Christianity. By the end of the same year, Wu Yong founded the Xu Chang Street Youth Fellowship, where he actively spread the gospel, leading to a growing congregation. In 1952, under Wu’s leadership, the Nanjing East Road Chapel was constructed. Seventy members from the Xu Chang Street Youth Fellowship assisted in its establishment. Wu Yong was ordained as one of its elders and also took charge of the ministry at the Xu Chang Street Mandarin Worship Hall, which later became the Linsen South Road Worship Hall.

Under Wu Yong’s leadership, the local church system in Taipei was strengthened, and over 20 branch churches were subsequently established across the city.

#The Local Church (Assembly,召會)

Also known as the Local Church Movement, this Christian movement was founded in 1922 in Fuzhou by Watchman Nee (倪柝聲, 1903–1972) and his co-workers. Initially, the movement was referred to as the Local Church, the Christian Assembly, or the Little Flock. The Local Church emphasized indigenous Chinese Christian practices and developed a unique form of worship based on Watchman Nee’s spiritual visions and revelations. After the Nationalist government retreated to Taiwan, the movement was carried forward and expanded by Witness Lee (李常受, 1905–1997), one of Watchman Nee’s closest disciples.

In 1949, just before the Communist takeover of Shanghai, Watchman Nee instructed Witness Lee to relocate to Taiwan to prevent the Local Church leadership from being entirely eradicated. Witness Lee purchased land on Ren’ai Road in Taipei and constructed a meeting hall capable of accommodating 300 attendees. On August 1, 1949, the first official meeting was held at this new location. Other key figures were sent to different cities: Shi Bocheng was responsible for the church in Chiayi, Lin Sangang in Taichung, Wei Jianzhang in Keelung, He Guangming in Hsinchu, and Xu Erjian in Tainan.

In 1955 and 1957, British Inner Life Movement leader Theodore Austin-Sparks (1888–1971) visited Taiwan twice. However, in 1957, Witness Lee and Austin-Sparks had theological disagreements over the concept of the “local church position,” ultimately leading to a schism. This dispute resulted in five co-workers, including Shi Bocheng, leaving the movement in 1965. They later founded the “Christian Testimony Fellowship” in California, which subsequently established Christian gatherings in North America and Taiwan, becoming an influential offshoot of the Local Church.

The movement primarily uses theRecovery Version of the Bible, a study Bible translated and compiled under Witness Lee’s supervision. TheNew Testament Recovery Version was completed in both Chinese and English in 1987, theEnglish Old Testament Recovery Version in 1997, and theChinese Old Testament Recovery Version in 2003.

Members of the Local Church do not consider themselves part of a denomination, religious association, or formal organization. They emphasize that all Christians are part of the Local Church. The movement is also referred to as “The Lord’s Recovery” or “The Recovery Movement.” Since local churches in Taiwan register with the government under the name “Church Meeting Hall,” outsiders often refer to them as “Meeting Hall Churches.” Following their doctrine, each city, town, or village should have only one Local Church, leading to the naming convention: “X City/Town Local Church X Meeting Hall.”

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

  1. 中國教會遷入

The Migration of Chinese Churches to Taiwan

In 1949, as the Chinese Civil War turned unfavorable for the Kuomintang (KMT) government led by Chiang Kai-shek, the Republic of China (ROC) began retreating to Taiwan. On October 1, 1949, under the leadership of Mao Zedong, the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) declared victory and established the People’s Republic of China (PRC). In its first year of rule, the CCP’s atheist policies forced thousands of foreign missionaries to leave China.

Since the 19th century, various Christian missionary organizations from Europe and the United States had been active in China. With the political shift in 1949, many of these missions relocated to Taiwan. These included theBaptist Church, Methodist Church, Lutheran Church, Quaker Church, Christian Missionary Alliance, Assemblies of God, Episcopal Church, Holiness Church, Evangelical Fellowship, Church of the Nazarene, Zion Church, Christian and Missionary Alliance, Church of the Nazarene, Sabbath Church, Mennonite Church, Bread of Life Church, Lutheran Church, China Evangelistic Mission, and the Local Church (召會), among others. This migration contributed to Taiwan’s present-day landscape of diverse Christian denominations.

As these new religious groups arrived in Taiwan, they found a receptive environment. Many of the new immigrants who had followed the KMT to Taiwan were displaced from their homeland, experiencing uncertainty and seeking spiritual solace. The well-resourced missionaries and churches that had relocated from mainland China actively recruited members in densely populated areas and schools. Consequently, Christian churches began emerging rapidly across Taiwan.

Another significant factor in the expansion of Christianity in Taiwan during this period was the personal faith ofChiang Kai-shek and Soong Mei-ling, which held considerable influence in the authoritarian era. This relationship between the ruling elite and the church led to deep ties between the Christian community and the government, extending beyond political and military control. (Huang Kexian, 2021, 72-73)

Additionally, the denominations that relocated to Taiwan came from different missionary regions in China, resulting in variations within the same denominational traditions. This historical divergence continues to shape the landscape of Christianity in Taiwan today. Furthermore, the Christian groups that arrived in 1949 often held different political and theological positions from thePresbyterian Church in Taiwan, which had been established for over a century. These differences led to tensions that persist in Taiwan’s Christian community to this day.

Key Theological Issues

This period marks the transition from the Japanese era to the arrival of the Nationalist government in Taiwan. Key theological, doctrinal, and ecclesiological discussions, such as the Presbyterian Church’s New People Movement, the issue of shrine worship, and the Taiwan-Japan church relations, had largely begun in the previous period. Meanwhile, discussions related to the Nationalist government’s arrival in Taiwan had not yet fully emerged during this time and would be further explored in the next period.

Anti-Christian Leaders

  1. 中國共產黨

The Communist Party of China (CPC)

The Communist Party of China (CPC) is currently the ruling party of the People’s Republic of China. It was founded by Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao in August 1920, during the Beiyang Government period of the Republic of China. In 1922, the CPC joined the Communist International (Comintern), receiving its guidance and support. Subsequently, it cooperated with the Kuomintang (KMT) in the Northern Expedition, successfully dismantling the Beiyang warlords and the Beiyang Government. However, after the first KMT-CPC cooperation collapsed in 1927, the two parties engaged in a decade-long military struggle.

From the 1930s onward, a domestic faction led by Mao Zedong emerged, allowing the CPC to gradually break away from the influence of the Comintern. In 1931, the party established the “Chinese Soviet Republic,” the first Soviet-style regime in China led by the CPC, advocating the overthrow of the Nationalist Government and the KMT.

During the Second Sino-Japanese War, the CPC and the KMT formed a second cooperative alliance, establishing a united front against Japanese aggression. However, after Japan’s surrender in 1945, the CPC organized and led the People’s Liberation Army, ultimately defeating the KMT and the National Revolutionary Army in the second phase of the Chinese Civil War. The CPC then secured control over mainland China and most coastal islands. In 1949, it established the People’s Republic of China (PRC), becoming the de facto ruling party, a position it has maintained to the present day.

The CPC’s Religious Policies and Christian Churches in China

As an atheist Leninist party, the CPC has exercised control over religious activities, including Christian churches. Before the founding of the PRC in 1949, many foreign missionaries voluntarily left their mission areas in anticipation of impending changes. After 1949, foreign clergy were either forced to leave mainland China, followed the KMT government to Taiwan, relocated to British Hong Kong, or returned to their home countries.

Although the Constitution of the People’s Republic of China contains provisions guaranteeing religious freedom, their actual implementation has been subject to debate. Under the guidance of then-Premier Zhou Enlai, the government proposed the “Three-Self” principles—self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation. In July 1950, Christian leaders, including Wu Yaozong, jointly issued the “Three-Self Declaration,” launching the Three-Self Patriotic Movement in response to the government’s call for churches to operate independently from foreign influence (Xing Fuzeng, 2020, p. 105).

In terms of Protestant Christianity, the first National Christian Conference was held in Beijing in 1954, formally establishing the Three-Self Patriotic Movement Committee of Protestant Churches in China. Later, the China Christian Council was also established, and together they are collectively referred to as the “National Two Christian Organizations.” Protestant churches in China are required to join the state-sanctioned “Three-Self Church,” operate under CPC leadership, and accept supervision and regulation by the State Administration for Religious Affairs. Today, the Three-Self Patriotic Movement Church is the largest Christian organization in mainland China.

Anti-Christian Organizations

1.日本殖民政府

  1. 中國共產黨

Japanese Colonial Government

During Japan’s colonial rule over Taiwan, the Japanese government maintained a cautious attitude towards Christianity. However, since the Presbyterian Church largely cooperated with the authorities—for example, by conducting worship services in Japanese as part of the imperialization policy—Christianity was not eradicated as it had been during previous regime changes. By the late Japanese colonial period, tensions between the Presbyterian Church and the government increased due to the enforcement of the imperialization policy, which promoted reverence for Shintoism.

During the wartime period of the 1940s, contradictions deepened. As Japan’s anti-British and anti-American sentiment intensified, Western missionaries in Taiwan faced increasing difficulties. Eventually, due to the expansion of the war, they were forced to leave Taiwan. The colonial authorities demanded that Japanese individuals replace Western leaders in Christian medical and educational institutions. By the end of 1941, before Japan went to war with the United States, Scottish Presbyterian missionaries had withdrawn from Taiwan. As mentioned in Wan Ronghua’s account, the Japanese government coveted church school properties. To protect the school and ensure that the evangelical mission among students could continue, Wan Ronghua proactively appointed a Japanese principal and took a step back to serve as a teacher and chaplain. He was the last missionary to leave Tainan, departing on November 22, 1940.

At the same time, both the southern and northern branches of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan completely severed ties with their foreign mother churches. In response to the situation, the southern church adopted the Three-Self Movement, while the northern church initiated the New Man Movement—both serving as self-preservation strategies that ultimately enabled Taiwanese churches to transition into independently operated local churches. Eventually, the Presbyterian churches in southern and northern Taiwan merged with the churches of Japanese residents in Taiwan, forming the”Japanese Christian Taiwan Church” (日本基督教台灣教團).

The Chinese Communist Party

The Chinese Communist Party (CCP) is the ruling party of the People’s Republic of China (PRC). It was founded in August 1920 during the Beiyang Government era of the Republic of China by figures such as Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao. In 1922, the CCP joined the Communist International (Comintern), receiving guidance and support. Subsequently, the CCP collaborated with the Kuomintang (KMT) in the Northern Expedition, successfully dismantling the Beiyang warlords and their government. However, after the first KMT-CCP alliance collapsed in 1927, the two parties engaged in a decade-long military struggle.

Starting in the 1930s, a domestic faction led by Mao Zedong gained prominence within the CCP, allowing the party to gradually distance itself from Comintern influence. In 1931, the CCP established the”Chinese Soviet Republic” (中華蘇維埃共和國), marking the first communist regime in China, with the goal of overthrowing the KMT-led National Government.

During the Second Sino-Japanese War, the CCP and the KMT formed a second alliance, establishing a unified front against Japan. However, after Japan’s defeat in 1945, the CCP led the People’s Liberation Army in the Chinese Civil War, ultimately defeating the KMT and the Republic of China Armed Forces. The CCP gained control over mainland China and most coastal islands, leading to the founding of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, where it has remained the ruling party ever since.

As a Leninist party that embraces atheism, the CCP has maintained strict control over religious activities. Prior to the establishment of the PRC in 1949, many foreign missionaries voluntarily left China in anticipation of political upheaval. After 1949, foreign clergy were either expelled, followed the ROC government to Taiwan, or relocated to British Hong Kong or their home countries.

Although the PRC Constitution nominally guarantees freedom of religious belief, its implementation has been subject to scrutiny. Under the leadership of Zhou Enlai, then-Premier of the Central People’s Government, the CCP introduced the”Three-Self Principles”—self-governance, self-support, and self-propagation—for Christian organizations. In July 1950, Chinese Christian leader Wu Yaozong and others issued the”Three-Self Manifesto,” launching theThree-Self Patriotic Movement to promote state-sanctioned Christian self-reliance.

In the Protestant sector, theFirst National Christian Conference was held in Beijing in 1954, officially establishing theThree-Self Patriotic Movement Committee. Later, theChina Christian Council was formed, and together, these two organizations became known as the”National Two Christian Organizations” (基督教全國兩會). Protestant churches in China were required to join the state-controlledThree-Self Church, operate under CCP leadership, and accept oversight from the State Administration for Religious Affairs. Today, the Three-Self Patriotic Church remains the largest Christian organization in mainland China.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

1.神社參拜

Shinto Shrine Worship

After the Meiji Restoration, Japan promoted the nationalization of Shinto as a state religion, leading to the establishment of shrines throughout the country. These newly established shrines symbolized state power and served as a means to transmit national consciousness to local populations, with the ultimate goal of transforming society into loyal subjects of the emperor (Murakami Shigeyoshi, 1982, pp. 5-6). Shinto shrines became emblematic of Japanese imperialism, regarded as part of colonial governance through religion. As Japan expanded its colonial empire during World War II, shrines were established across its colonies, gradually integrating into the daily lives of colonial residents (Tsai Jin-tang, 1994, pp. 15-19). In newly developed territories such as Hokkaido and colonies like Taiwan and Korea, colonial subjects were often forced to participate in shrine customs and rituals during the war period.

The Japanese colonial government saw the swift “Japanization” of Taiwan as essential for governance, aiming to reshape Taiwanese identity into that of a nation centered on the emperor’s sovereignty. From 1895 to 1945, with the full support of the Japanese colonial administration, shrines dedicated to Shinto deities symbolizing absolute authority were constructed across towns and villages in Taiwan. By the end of the Naichi Enchō (Inner Territory Extension) policy in Shōwa 9 (1934), the government sought to achieve the goal of “one shrine per town or village” (Taiwan Governor-General’s Bureau of Education and Social Affairs, 1943).

In 1900, the Japanese House of Representatives passed a resolution on the “Proposal for the Construction of a Special Official Shrine in Taiwan.” Simultaneously, the Ministry of Home Affairs announced, in Notification No. 81, the decision to establish Taiwan Shrine (Tsai Jin-tang, 1994, pp. 15-19). This shrine was built to commemorate Prince Kitashirakawa Yoshihisa, who led the Imperial Guard Expeditionary Force that landed at Aodi on May 31, 1895. The force engaged in battles from Keelung to Tainan, where the prince eventually succumbed to illness. He was later honored as the “Guardian of Taiwan.” As the first official shrine in an overseas colony, Taiwan Shrine designated October 28—the day of the prince’s passing—as its annual festival day. The Governor-General declared this day a public holiday across the island, during which an imperial envoy and the Governor-General, accompanied by government and military officials as well as local elites, would participate in worship ceremonies, demonstrating the shrine’s supreme political and symbolic significance (Tsai Jin-tang, 1994, p. 23).

In 1936, the Taiwan Governor-General’s Office launched the Kominka (imperialization) movement, introducing the “Ethical Reform Movement,” which promoted reverence for gods and ancestors, the veneration of imperial ancestors, and the widespread adoption of the national language (Japanese) among Taiwanese people. As part of this initiative, Taiwanese were required to use Japanese in daily life and were encouraged to participate in shrine worship and the veneration of sacred Shinto amulets (Taima hōsai) (Tsai Jin-tang, 1991, pp. 69-72). By the final phase of the war, during the “Imperial Citizens Service Movement,” efforts were intensified to fully instill Japanese imperial ideology, leading to the establishment of various patriotic service organizations (Hsu Chien-hsin, 1965, pp. 262-264).

During this period, Christian missionary schools—particularly middle schools—faced severe challenges. Schools such as Chang Jung High School and Tamkang High School struggled with the requirement of “shrine worship,” which became a condition for government recognition. Initially, church leaders strongly resisted, but they were eventually forced to comply with the Japanese Ministry of Education’s argument that shrine worship was a “supra-religious” patriotic act. The Kominka movement posed significant dilemmas for missionary schools, not only altering curricula and school administration but also striking at the core of their religious identity through the demand for shrine worship (Lo Chi-ming, 2016, pp. 24-25).

  1. 二二八事件

The February 28 Incident

The February 28 Incident refers to the violent conflicts between the government and civilians that erupted across Taiwan from February 27 to May 16, 1947. The Taiwanese people demanded political reform, but the Nationalist Government of the Republic of China ultimately dispatched military forces to suppress the uprising with brutal force.

After World War II, the people of Taiwan eagerly anticipated the island’s reintegration into China. Some figures from the Presbyterian Church, such as Tsai Pei-huo and Chen Si-zhi, even composed songs to celebrate Taiwan’s “liberation” (Chen Guan-zhou, 2002, p. 35). However, the incoming Chinese administration treated Taiwan as a new colonial possession rather than as a reclaimed territory, subjecting the island’s people to even greater suffering.

Many Taiwanese civil servants who had been employed under the Japanese administration were dismissed en masse, while those who remained were often subjected to discrimination and humiliation by their Chinese colleagues (Chang Yan-xian et al., 2006, pp. 13-42, 171-221). The lived experiences, language, and education that Taiwanese had acquired under Japanese rule were not recognized; instead, they were denigrated as remnants of Japanese imperialism (Lai Ze-han, 1994, pp. 3-27). Economically, Taiwan suffered from severe hyperinflation due to the Nationalist government’s planned economy policies. Rising unemployment, food shortages, and widespread economic hardship led to a series of social crises (Chang Yan-xian et al., 2006, pp. 13-42). Additionally, infighting among different factions of the Kuomintang (KMT) further weakened political and military discipline, leading to rampant corruption (Lai Ze-han, 1994, pp. 3-27). These accumulated grievances set the stage for the February 28 Incident, triggered by an event on February 27, 1947. On that day, Monopoly Bureau agents attempted to crack down on illegal cigarette sales near Tianma Teahouse in Taipei’s Taipingding district. During the raid, they severely injured a cigarette vendor, Lin Jiang-mai, and accidentally shot and killed a bystander, Chen Wen-xi. This incident quickly escalated, igniting island-wide unrest within days (Lai Ze-han, 1994, pp. 47-81).

In an attempt to mediate the crisis and address public grievances, representatives from various counties and cities, along with local gentry, formed the February 28 Incident Settlement Committee. They hoped to resolve the conflict through political negotiations and proposed political reforms (Lai Ze-han, 1994, pp. 47-81).

At first, Governor-General Chen Yi appeared to compromise, accepting the committee’s recommendations. However, he secretly regarded the uprising as an act of “rebellion.” Soon after, the central government deployed troops to Taiwan to carry out large-scale purges. This military crackdown resulted in the mass arrest, execution, or disappearance of numerous Taiwanese, including many intellectuals and elites who had no direct involvement in the protests (Chang Yan-xian et al., 2006, pp. 43-76, 171-243, 449-489).

Among the victims were several prominent members of the Presbyterian Church, including Pastor Xiao Chao-jin (leader of the Three Principles of the People Youth League); Zhang Qi-lang, a renowned doctor from Fenglin (who was also a delegate to the Constitutional National Assembly, speaker of the Hualien County Council, and a physician), along with his two sons; Taipei City Councilor Xu Chun-qing; Dr. Lin Mao-sheng, acting dean of the College of Liberal Arts at National Taiwan University; Chen Neng-tong, principal of Tamkang High School; discipline director Huang A-tong; teacher Lu Yuan; chairman Xu Chun-qing; Kaohsiung City Councilors Xu Qiu-zong and Tu Guang-ming; lawyers and brothers Li Rui-han and Li Rui-feng; and Ruan Chao-ri, president of the Taiwan Shin Sheng Daily News, among many others (Li Xin-ren, 2011).
