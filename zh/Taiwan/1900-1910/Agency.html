Taiwan: Agency (1900-1910)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

An Bide (安彼得) (Peter Anderson, 1847-1913) began his service in Tainan on January 14, 1879, and returned to the UK for vacation in 1885. After his second arrival in Taiwan in October 1887, he planned to build a new medical center. After several years of dealing with complicated land disputes, the “New Building Medical Center” he supervised was completed on April 17, 1900, featuring the first manual elevator in Taiwan, which was quite modern compared to the “Old Building Medical Center.” After 1901, the new building was managed by Dr. James Laidlaw Maxwell Jr. (小馬雅各, 1873-1951), while An Bide moved to Takao (打狗).

Mrs. Peter Anderson (安夫人, 1847-1906), a Scotswoman, arrived in Taiwan on December 29, 1882, and left with her husband due to illness on September 20, 1906; she passed away in Edinburgh on December 27 of the same year. During her time in Taiwan, she assisted her husband in nursing work and taught patients colloquial script, helping in the construction of the new medical center.

After his wife’s death, An Bide returned to Takao to serve again on April 2, 1907, from the UK. In May 1908, when James Laidlaw Maxwell returned to the UK for vacation, An Bide collaborated with David Landsborough (蘭大衛, 1870-1957) to help at the New Building Medical Center. Due to health problems, An Bide went to Japan for recuperation in April 1909 and returned to Taiwan on May 7. He retired and returned to the UK in the autumn of 1910. An Bide spent a total of 31 years in Taiwan and passed away at the age of 66 in his hometown of Perthshire (珀斯) on March 12, 1913. (Zhu Zhenyi 2012: 67-75)

According to a report by Yang Shufen (楊淑芬) in the China Daily on September 7, 2023, regarding the application for the Nanshan Cemetery children’s burial site as a cultural monument, An Bide’s second daughter passed away early in 1891 and was buried at Sanfenci Cemetery, later relocated to the present Nanshan Cemetery in 1934. The inscription reads, “The grave of Miss An of Great Britain, who passed away on August 2, 1891, called by Jesus, a little child. See Matthew 18:2.” The biblical reference memorializes how “Jesus called a little child to stand among them,” indicating that children are welcomed into the kingdom of heaven.

In 2023, due to the announcement of relocation and preservation of the Tainan Christian Cemetery, on September 6, the public submitted the cultural asset application for the “Presbyterian Church of Taiwan Early Departed Believers Cemetery Group (32 Gates).” On January 17, 2024, the Tainan City Cultural Asset Management Office conducted a site survey and listing follow-up review meeting, announcing on February 26 to postpone the listing follow-up case but to continue cultural investigation and delay relocation operations. The An Bide family can be closely linked to the development history of the New Building Medical Center and Tainan City.

Mei Jianwu (梅監務) (1895-1931)

On October 16, 1895, Mei Jianwu departed from London with Dr. David Landsborough (蘭大衛) and Pastor A. B. Nielson (廉德烈), arriving at Anping Harbor on December 18. Starting in November 1896, the two, Mei and Lan, made the growing and more prosperous trade and administrative center of Changhua their main base of work, which at that time could be considered the “secondary missionary center” following Tainan. They also managed to balance their work in Dasha with regular medical missionary activities, for instance, starting in April 1897, they engaged in four months of medical missionary work in Dasha. Additionally, while Dr. Lan was busy with medical work, Mei Jianwu tirelessly preached in Changhua, Taichung, Dasha, Xiluo, Nantou, and Puli. His usual preaching methods included beating drums, blowing horns, and loudly exclaiming, “The Son of God is missing!” During a visit to the Penghu Islands in 1898, he became close friends with the Chinese missionary Lin Xuegong (林學恭), also known as Lin Chima, and later invited him to come and work together. He also led Yang Fuchun (楊福春) from Erlin to faith and helped to pioneer the work in Qingshui.

In July 1900, Mei and Lan returned to England for vacation. Later that year, due to health issues (malaria), Mei Jianwu asked the English Presbyterian Mission Overseas Commission for a transfer to work in Qing China. After discussions, he was reassigned to Singapore, which had a large Chinese community, and set off for work there in September 1901. However, Mei Jianwu was not happy in Singapore as he continued to suffer from malaria. He expressed that if malaria was sure to return, he would prefer to fall ill in Formosa!Mei Jianwu (梅監務) returned to Taiwan and resumed work in Changhua on August 9, 1902, continuing to pioneer ministries in Dasha, Fengyuan, and Puli. Between May and August 1903, he joined Dr. David Landsborough (蘭大衛) to conduct routine medical missionary work in Dasha. Starting in 1904, Mei Jianwu began leading Taiwanese evangelists in studying the Letters to the Corinthians, believing that Taiwan’s missionary context bore similarities to the pagan world faced by Paul.

Missionary Publications

In the autumn of 1906, Mei and Lan returned to the UK for vacation. In 1907, Mei Jianwu published “The Heathen Heart,” a reflection of his missionary experiences that gained significant attention. In early 1908, he married Margaret Rintoul Findlay, the daughter of his mentor, and in March of that year, he returned to Changhua with his new wife, receiving a warm welcome. Their new home, featuring 12 brick columns, was also known as the “Twelve Apostles.” Mei Jianwu also published annotations on the Book of Romans written in Romanized script. After arriving in Taiwan, Mrs. Mei actively learned Taiwanese from Pastor Lin Xuegong’s (林學恭) daughter, but unfortunately, by the end of the year, she had to go to Australia for treatment of a severe lung infection, prompting Mei Jianwu to resign from his work in Taiwan. Dr. Lan noted in his diary, “It was a sorrowful farewell; Mei Jianwu loved the Taiwanese, and the Taiwanese loved Mei Jianwu… Twelve years ago, when we first arrived in Changhua, there were only four churches in Central Taiwan; through Mei Jianwu’s efforts, there are now 23 small churches, which he should be proud of.”

At the end of 1914, Mei and his wife returned to work in the Changhua region and were greeted with a grand welcome. He also published “Evangelism,” expressing his belief that the method best suited for spreading the gospel was “evangelistic outdoor preaching.” In April 1915, he and Mrs. Mei went to Japan for recuperation, but there was no improvement. After returning to Taiwan, Mrs. Mei passed away in November that year, and they had only spent eight years together. Reverend Liu Zhongjian (劉忠堅) officiated at Mrs. Mei’s funeral, and Mei Jianwu himself led the band and sang “In Heaven, There Is No Pain,” conducting street preaching as the citizens of Changhua watched in silence. In 1916, Mei Jianwu suffered from severe influenza, which, due to delayed treatment, led to lung infection. With the careful attention of Dr. George Gushue-Taylor (戴仁壽) and his advice to return to England for treatment,

In September 1918, Mei Jianwu returned to Taiwan to continue his work and spent the summer of 1919 at Datun Mountain in Tamsui, preaching to nearby villages. In 1920, he published “The Mind of the Early Converts,” which received high acclaim. On November 29, 1921, Mei Jianwu remarried, this time to Margaret Christian Arthur, a nurse from the Changhua medical center. From the end of 1922, Mei Jianwu was assigned to Tainan Theological College to take over Reverend Song Zhongjian’s (宋忠堅) work, also publishing “Ko-Cha e Kau-Hoe” (古早的教會) in Romanized script.

In 1924, due to recurring fevers, Mei Jianwu’s health began to decline, and he had to leave Formosa in June to return to England for recuperation. He initially hoped to return to Taiwan soon, but circumstances led him to bid farewell to Taiwan. During his time in England, he wrote “The Purpose of Jesus” (1929), “The King’s Guest” (1932), “Christ for Us and in Us” (1935), and “The Childhood of the Church” (1938). In 1928, he was awarded an honorary Doctor of Divinity (D.D.) degree from the University of Glasgow, and he retired to England in 1931 due to health issues. Mei Jianwu passed away peacefully in Lennoxtown, Scotland, on February 28, 1940, at the age of 75. The humble Mei Jianwu was a preacher, theologian, and hymn writer, revered as the “British Beggar” and “Paul of Taiwan.” (Zheng Yang’en 2000: 22-27)

Fei Renchun (費仁純) (1900-1908 in Taiwan)

Fei Renchun (Mr. F. R.Fei Renchun (費仁純) (Mr. F. R. Johnson) arrived in Taiwan on January 12, 1901, to succeed Yu Rao Li as the second principal of the Presbyterian Middle School. During his tenure, he actively took on responsibilities, incorporating Chinese, Japanese, and English instruction, which diversified the educational methods of the school. In 1906, he received an official establishment certificate from the Taiwan Governor-General’s Office. He resigned and returned to his home country on July 4, 1908.

Mrs. Fei (費師母) was in Taiwan from November 17, 1901, until returning home on July 1, 1908. Her main responsibility was teaching “poetic meter” (to help students understand poetry), which was a music curriculum. Lin Jinsheng (林錦生) (1894-1980) was promoted early on by Mrs. Fei; she selected him from the Sunday School at Taipingjing in 1901 to transition to middle school and chose him in 1905 to learn to play the organ. At that time, the Taipingjing chapel had a medium-sized organ, and Mrs. Fei served as the organist, aiming to cultivate local organists to take over. The only students who had learned to play the organ at that time were Lin Maosheng. After graduating from middle school in 1907, Miansheng earned the appreciation of Reverend Gan Weilin and assisted in dictionary editing, also with a recommendation from Mrs. Fei. (Lai Yongxiang 1995)

According to the 767th meeting minutes of the Tainan Clergy Association (July 22, 1908), the work done by Mr. and Mrs. Fei during their seven years in Formosa was quite impactful. Their contributions encompassed not only the supervision of the middle school but also more valuable roles as treasurer and library manager for the clergy association, indicating their considerable management and operational abilities. (Campbell 1910: 767)

He Xiren (何希仁) (1909-1915 in Taiwan)

Pastor He Xiren (Hope Moncrieff) took over from the resigned Pastor Mei Jianwu (Campbell Moody) in 1909, continuing the missionary work in central Taiwan. In 1910, the Tainan Clergy Association decided to establish Changhua as a secondary missionary center and petitioned the London Missionary Society to build housing for missionaries there, a project he actively supported. In 1913, he led the establishment of a six-year “Changhua Elementary School” under the jurisdiction of the Changhua Church, with a two-year preparatory program for middle school, hiring Pastor Huang Siming as both director and instructor. Unfortunately, due to financial challenges and competition with public schools, He Xiren left Taiwan in 1915, and the elementary school ceased operations in 1918.

From 1926 to 1929, Pastor He served as the director of a theological school in Xiamen, and from 1929 to 1934, he pastored in Edinburgh; during his retirement, he gave speeches and participated in film tours. Detailed information about his gas lantern and film touring activities in the UK between 1933 and 1934 can be found in his file archived at the University of London’s School of Oriental and African Studies, under the number GB 102 PCE/FMC/3/02/31c&d, covering the years 1926-1936 and 1938-1950.

He Xiren traveled extensively throughout Taiwan, reaching as far as the outlying island of Penghu. He contributed numerous writings to the church bulletin from 1910 to 1915, focusing on three main categories: 1) Church news, 2) Gospel and culture, and 3) History, geography, and science.

He Xiren authored a number of works, including a translation of James S. Stewart’s “The Life and Teaching of Jesus Christ,” which was published by Guangxuehui in 1940 and has been reprinted multiple times, remaining in circulation today. Another notable translation is “Pi̍t Siâ Kui Chèng: 闢邪歸正,” published in 1913 by Juzhen Hall. This publication, translated for evangelistic use, consists of 68 chapters covering various topics, including God’s attributes (chapters 1-12), reasons against ancestor worship and offerings (chapters 13-19), beliefs about the supernatural (chapters 20-26), the attributes of Jesus (chapters 27-29), differences between true and false religions (chapters 30-41), various folk beliefs (chapters 42-63), and improvements in social customs like marriage and filial piety (chapters 64-68). The 30th chapter concerning the “Baptist” ceremony was not translated.

In conclusion, He Xiren embodied a blend of reason and faith in his evangelistic work, continuing in the footsteps of Mei Jianwu and introducing contemporary Western scientific knowledge.

Yue Meidan (約美但) (1905-1917 in Taiwan)

Yue Meidan (also written as “Dan”) was born in Chateauguay, Quebec, Canada, to a grandfather who immigrated from Scotland.His father, Robert Jack, was an advanced fruit farmer in Quebec, while his mother, Annie Linda, was a poet, writer, and horticulturist. The couple had 11 children, and most of their elementary education was conducted at home by their mother. Yue Meidan (約美但) graduated first in his class from the Presbyterian College of McGill University in 1905. After graduation, he pursued further studies at the University of Chicago.

On November 3, 1905, Yue Meidan, along with Pastor/Doctor Song Yage (James and Harriet Ferguson), Miss Jane Kinney, and Miss Hannah Connell, arrived in Taiwan as cross-cultural missionaries. After a period of language study and understanding local customs, they engaged in medical care, women’s education, and theological education according to their individual passions, founding the Tamsui Girls’ School. Soon after, these young missionaries, along with the already established missionaries William and Margaret Gauld, formed the Canadian Mission Council, marking the beginning of a cooperative missionary team from the Canadian Presbyterian Church in northern Taiwan.

In 1906, Yue Meidan traveled to Hong Kong, where he married Catherine Winifred Bennett (凱瑟琳‧溫妮弗雷德‧班奈特, 1882–1980), the daughter of the pastor who baptized him. Their first child, Lilian, passed away three days after birth and was laid to rest in the expatriate cemetery adjacent to the Mackay Cemetery. Later, they had two sons and two daughters in Taiwan: Lawrence, Wilfred, Elsie, and Marjorie. Mrs. Yue entered McGill University at the age of 15 and graduated first in her class at 19. Before marriage, she taught at a women’s college in Ontario. After marriage, in addition to raising her children, she taught at the Tamsui Girls’ School and at the Korean Christian College (now Yonsei University). Upon returning to Canada, she continued her teaching career to help support the family. Additionally, she was passionate about community service, took on leadership roles, and served for over 20 years as president of the local United Church Women’s Missionary Society and Sunday School superintendent.

In 1907, Yue Meidan took over the administration of the Oxford School from Pastor William Gauld, preparing to transform it into Taipei Theological School. As a result, entry qualifications for the Oxford School were set to require graduation from public elementary schools specifically for Taiwanese Chinese, with an academic year system established. Combining theoretical knowledge and missionary training, students were required to complete four to five years of study to graduate, with only three students graduating after their first year. The curriculum included liberal arts, Bible studies, and basic theology, and Yue Meidan introduced critical analysis and historical context to help students better understand and interpret biblical messages.

Regarding teaching, Yue Meidan wrote: “Foreign missionaries who do not understand Japanese will be at a significant disadvantage in providing better advice to students regarding literature selection. The language of the younger generation is changing rapidly; although some classical Chinese forms may be retained, they have effectively become more Japanese. Therefore, for students in Taiwan who have received education in Japanese elementary or middle schools, it is essential to possess knowledge of Japanese in addition to classical Chinese when teaching any abstract subjects like theology to ensure clarity and enhance teaching efficiency.”

Additionally, Yue Meidan aimed to train local theological educators as future successors and specifically selected several students to improve their English proficiency, hoping they could pursue further theological education abroad after graduation. Unfortunately, despite his commitment to improving the quality of theological education, due to limitations in the Taiwanese educational system at the time, graduates from Oxford School or Taipei Theological School in the early years needed to complete ordinary middle school and advanced studies before being eligible to enter the theological department at church universities in mainland Japan.

Beyond teaching, Yue Meidan was elected as the chairman at the 10th annual meeting of the Northern Presbyterial Assembly in February 1910. He also cared about missionary work among the indigenous peoples in the eastern regions; in 1911, he traveled with Pastor Guo Xixin to the Amis missionary area, departing from Keelung on a communication boat to understand the progress there. Regarding this mission, Yue Meidan wrote: “Each year, the Taiwan Church (referring to the plain area churches) contributes to the missions among the Amis people in the Qilai Plain.”

Later, Yue Meidan unfortunately contracted malaria in Taiwan, and after taking quinine for treatment, he suffered hearing loss.In 1917, due to health issues, Yue Meidan applied for a transfer to the cooler climate of Seoul, South Korea, where he worked in theological education at the recently established Korean Christian College. However, his hearing did not improve in Seoul, leading doctors to suggest that he engage in outdoor work.

In 1920, Yue Meidan returned to Canada and resigned from his pastoral role to pursue an interest in horticulture inspired by his parents. He established a flower nursery in the Mission Highlands facing the Fraser Valley, focusing on the cultivation and sales of varieties like gladiolus, peonies, dahlias, iris, and daylilies, distributing his products across North America, the UK, Australia, and New Zealand. Due to his achievements in this field, a deep red peony variety was named “Milton Jack” in his honor by his peers. (Guo Zhongji 2022: 21)

Song Yage (宋雅各) (Dr. and Rev. J. Y. Ferguson, 1877-1965, often mistakenly referred to as George V. Ferguson) was from Toronto, Canada. He was dispatched by the Canadian Methodist Church to Taiwan to reopen the church hospital, becoming the second significant figure in northern church medical missionary history after Dr. Mackay. Not only did he reopen the Tamsui Mackay Hospital, but he also moved the medical center to Taipei, founding what is now Mackay Memorial Hospital.

In 1905, at the request of the second Northern Presbytery Assembly of the Taiwan Presbyterian Church, Dr. Song was sent to Taiwan by the Canadian Methodist Church to reopen the church hospital, arriving in Tamsui in November of that year. Upon arrival, Dr. Song and his wife actively learned Taiwanese, and by the fall of 1906, they reopened the Mackay Hospital. Due to the lack of hospitals and Dr. Song’s excellent medical skills and friendly demeanor, he quickly gained a reputation that drew in patients from all over northern Taiwan. However, as Tamsui’s prosperity declined, the hospital’s location became inconvenient for the public due to the Japanese transportation developments centered around Taipei.

On the other hand, Dr. Song recognized that the current Mackay Hospital was outdated and sought to meet contemporary standards and government requirements by constructing a well-equipped Christian hospital. He planned with Pastor William Gauld to establish a new hospital in Shuanglian and requested additional personnel from the Canadian parent church for medical work. The Canadian parent church recognized the potential of this work and allocated $25,000 for construction expenses. In 1911, Nurse Isabel Elliott arrived in Tamsui, followed by Dr. A. A. Gray and his wife the next year to assist with medical care.

Subsequently, Dr. Song relocated the medical center from Tamsui to Taiwan’s capital, Taipei, naming it Mackay Memorial Hospital in honor of the character and contributions of Dr. Mackay, the first missionary of the northern church. As a result, Dr. Song became the second important figure in the history of northern medical missionary work. Not only did he establish Mackay Memorial Hospital, but he also made significant contributions to its development while serving as its first director. During his tenure, he took a year off for personal leave and returned to Taiwan to continue his service, fulfilling his mission at Mackay Hospital for about five years through substantial efforts.

In 1914, with the outbreak of World War I, Mackay Hospital was also affected due to a shortage of medical supplies and personnel, forcing it to temporarily close in June 1918. Meanwhile, after fourteen years of dedicated service, Dr. Song, due to declining health, left Taiwan and returned to Canada that same year, never to return to Taiwan again. Upon his return to Canada, he became the chief surgeon at Eastern General Hospital and later opened his practice, continuing to practice medicine until he passed away on February 23, 1965, in his hometown of Toronto, Canada, at the age of eighty-eight.

Dr. Song had a deep love for Taiwan. Despite leaving many years prior, he often thought of the island, especially regarding Mackay Memorial Hospital, which he had founded. Whenever church colleagues visited him in Canada, he would inquire about the hospital. His home frequently displayed photographs of Mackay Hospital from that time. His father was a lay preacher, and his mother, Liu Annie, hailed from the same village as his father. Both parents were respected and devoted Christians. Liu completed his high school education in Scotland before attending university in Manitoba, Canada, graduating in 1892 before entering the theological seminary of the Presbyterian Church. In 1904, he studied theology at Westminster College in Cambridge, England. In 1906, he was ordained as a pastor at the Lowertown Church in Brandon, Manitoba, a church he established, which still exists today and has undergone its third reconstruction.

In 1906, Liu Zhongjian married Constance MacLeod, a daughter of a Methodist pastor and a former elementary school teacher. They served at the Lowertown Church for a year, during which Liu felt a strong calling to become a missionary. This desire had been in his heart since youth, inspired by reading various missionary biographies, including those of Livingstone and Robert Moffat. Their stories of dedicating their lives to God’s work significantly influenced his resolution to pursue missionary work. It was during that year at Lowertown Church that Liu and his wife fervently prayed for God to guide them. They felt a calling to preach in mainland China and eventually volunteered with the Canadian Presbyterian Foreign Mission Board, which accepted their application and assigned them to Taiwan, an area where missionaries were already serving. Upon receiving this news, they were both surprised and delighted, dedicating themselves to prayer for God’s assistance as they prepared for their journey. God answered their prayers, and the young couple safely arrived in Taiwan in November 1907.

He was the third principal of the seminary and advocated for the merger of the northern and southern seminaries, leading northern students to study at Tainan Theological College. After two years, since the southern seminary showed no interest in coming to Taipei, returning students from the northern church reestablished Taipei Theological College in Tamsui. In 1927, as Taiwan remained under the Canadian Presbyterian Church, Liu Zhongjian transitioned to the southern school, serving as the principal until 1939. He returned to Canada from 1939 to 1946, and upon returning to Taiwan, he assisted in the reception of the former Japanese churches. He returned to his home country for retirement in 1949 and passed away in 1957. This year coincided with the strengthening of the Taiwan General Assembly, allowing him to leave without regrets. Dr. James Laidlaw Maxwell Jr. (馬雅各二世) (1900-1923 in Taiwan) and his wife…Dr. James Laidlaw Maxwell Jr. (馬雅各二世) (1873-1951) was a renowned physician specializing in leprosy. He arrived in Taiwan on February 24, 1901, at the age of 28, to serve as a medical missionary, working at the Tainan New Building Medical Center, which had been established by his father, Dr. Mackay, in 1865. On October 9 of the same year, he married nurse Millicent Bertha Saunders (桑德絲, 1871-1961) in Hong Kong, and together they dedicated themselves to medical missionary work in Taiwan. They had four children: Millicent Hope (1902-1983), Louisa Grace (1905-1985), Mary Edith (1907-1925), and Hilda Elizabeth (1911-1991).

Dr. Maxwell served at the New Building Medical Center for a total of 22 years, improving the hospital’s medical facilities and modernizing its services. With the assistance of his wife, Mrs. Saunders, who was a qualified nurse, he also trained numerous local interns and nurses, becoming a pioneer in nursing education in the southern region of Taiwan.

The couple frequently conducted medical missionary work, traveling around the island with assistants and medicine, reaching even into indigenous areas, particularly focusing on the Amis tribe. Dr. Maxwell had proposed a missionary movement focused on the Amis people to the British parent church, but due to the outbreak of the European war, the church was unable to expand its work, and this mission did not come to fruition.

During World War I, Dr. Maxwell was conscripted to serve in a battlefield hospital. After the war ended, they returned to Taiwan to serve again. At that time, the New Building Medical Center was in decline due to the war, so the couple immediately set to work revitalizing the hospital. Jane Kinney (金仁理) (1907-1928 in Taiwan) and Hannah Connell (高哈拿) (1905-1931 in Taiwan)

After the death of Pastor Mackay in 1901, the call from Pastor William Gauld led to the arrival of female missionaries in Taiwan in 1905. To improve the existing girls’ school and provide better professional education for women, the Canadian Presbyterian Church sent two female missionaries: Miss Jane Kinney, responsible for the girls’ school, and Miss Hannah Connell, responsible for the women’s school. In their first two years in Taiwan, their primary focus was on language learning while gradually acclimating to the customs and practices of northern Taiwan.

Miss Kinney hailed from New Brunswick in Eastern Canada, the youngest of eight siblings in a farming family. She attended Sunday school at the Greenfield Presbyterian Church, where she later taught. She possessed an uncommon level of education for her time, having graduated from a normal school and obtained a teaching license, teaching at public schools for four years. Ambitious, she passed the entrance exam for the University of New Brunswick, starting from her second year until graduation. In her first two years in Taiwan, she primarily focused on learning Taiwanese. Starting from her second year, she and Miss Connell worked diligently to plan the future of the girls’ school, diligently executing the establishment of the school. They traveled to Xiamen to study British Presbyterian efforts in establishing girls’ schools, drawing relevant experiences as a model. Additionally, they tirelessly visited northern churches to promote the girls’ school and recruit students.Miss Hannah Connell (高哈拿), who served as the first principal of the Women’s School, hailed from Ontario, Canada, and graduated from the Toronto Conservatory of Music, where she demonstrated considerable musical talent. Upon arriving in Taiwan, she took on the leadership of the Women’s School and also taught music at the girls’ school. The new Women’s School attracted more than 20 women when enrollment first opened, exceeding the school’s maximum capacity and leading to the rejection of some applicants. In the following semesters, new students continued to apply for admission, receiving education in the Christian faith. Miss Connell was known for her calm demeanor and kindness, being a gentle yet resolute woman. She displayed remarkable insight in her administration, making many appropriate decisions and maintaining her original intentions even when faced with setbacks. Under her leadership, the wives of Dr. Song Yage, Pastor Yue Meidan, Pastor Keweisi, and the young Miss Li Mamei formed the teaching staff, working together to train a group of Taiwanese women to proclaim the Gospel of salvation through Christ.

By the mid-1920s, the division of the Canadian parent church eventually impacted the missionaries in northern Taiwan. As a result, Jane Kinney left Taiwan in 1928, retired in 1941, and passed away in 1965; Hannah Connell moved to serve in the south but tragically passed away at a young age in 1931.

Overall, through the concerted efforts of the teaching team led by Jane Kinney and Hannah Connell, both young girls and adult women received substantial education. Graduates of Tamsui Girls’ School made remarkable contributions in both church and society. One notable alumna was Taiwan’s first female doctor, Cai A-xin, who pursued medical studies in Japan after graduation and returned to Taiwan to practice medicine, establishing Qingshin Hospital in 1926, along with a midwifery school to train midwives for the benefit of Taiwanese women. Other early graduates included Pastor Chen Xiaomeizhu, Ms. Chen Zhuanzhen, and Elder Li Chenhanmu, who were all deeply involved in women’s ministry in the church, forming the northern Women’s Missionary Society to drive various initiatives. They contributed significantly to the Gospel’s work, becoming important female leaders within the northern church. The curriculum of the Women’s School included subjects such as Bible studies, mathematics, Japanese, Chinese, colloquial writing, handwriting, singing, needlework, and geography. Graduates from the program could serve as missionary women, assisting in women’s evangelistic work across various churches and becoming the backbone of “Women’s Missions” within the church.

Song Yili (宋以利) (1892-1901 in Taiwan)

In the late 19th century in Taiwan, a female physician from England, Dr. Elizabeth Christine Christie (宋伊莉莎白, 1868-1901), also known as Song Yili or Dr. Elizabeth, was born on April 3, 1868, in Glasgow, Scotland. There are various birth date reports, but based on her application for a medical license to the Taiwan Governor-General’s Office in 1899, she graduated with high honors from the London School of Medicine for Women, established in 1874, and obtained her Licentiate of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons (L.R.C.P. & S., Ed.), making her the first woman in Scotland to earn this distinction. She registered as a practicing physician in Scotland on August 7, 1891, and worked at a hospital in Leith, near Edinburgh. She was the first licensed female physician in Scotland and throughout Great Britain and Ireland.

Dr. Song married Pastor Liu Zhongjian (宋忠堅) (Duncan Ferguson) in January 1892, and after completing their wedding in Hong Kong, they moved to Taiwan for medical and missionary work. Pastor Liu served in Tainan from 1889 to 1923, holding positions as principal of the seminary and the high school, as well as working on the Church Bulletin. Dr. Song arrived in Taiwan on February 19, 1892, becoming the first female physician in Taiwan. As a physician and pastor’s wife, she learned the local language within a year. Centered in Tainan, she used her medical practice to spread the Gospel and provided healing to many.

After Japan took control of Taiwan, on May 21, 1896, the Taiwan Governor-General’s Office issued Order No. 6, establishing the regulations for the medical profession in Taiwan, which required applicants for medical licenses to submit relevant documentation to the local administrative office. Dr. Song provided her credentials and was awarded the medical license No. 6. Other physicians who received medical licenses around the same time included Dr. William W. …In the June 29, 1899 issue of the Taiwan Daily News, an article titled “C-Section Miracle” noted: “Recently, a woman in labor experienced an arduous and prolonged delivery without success, almost nearing death. Fortunately, a Western female physician, the wife of a pastor, performed a cesarean section, enabling both mother and child to survive—an act that received unanimous admiration from those who heard of it.” This account is regarded as the first documented case of a cesarean section in Taiwan, performed by a formally trained female physician and pastor’s wife. In March 1893, while Dr. Song worked in Tainan, her newlywed husband, Pastor Liu Zhongjian (宋忠堅), nearly lost his life while returning home from Taitung (now Eastern Taiwan). Pastor Liu did not take a boat but rode and walked through the mountainous central region of Taiwan. Indigenous people misinterpreted him as a threat and shot him. A bullet pierced through his chest, but fortunately, the incident occurred not far from his home. With the help of Dr. Song and other physicians from the New Building Medical Center, Pastor Liu quickly recovered and immediately returned to his missionary duties.

She hired a Taiwanese nanny to care for the two children while she continued her medical and missionary work. Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Baker Memorial Hall/Classroom Completion

In 1875, Pastor Gan Weilin (甘為霖) completed the dormitory “New Building” in Xizhuwei, initiating the “Missionary Training Class,” which gradually developed the area near the East Gate of Tainan into a base for mission work and education in Taiwan. In 1876, the Presbyterian Church merged the “Missionary Training Classes” of Tainan and Qihu, establishing the predecessor of Tainan Theological College, “University,” at the Erlaokou Medical Hall, with Pastor Baker (巴克禮) as the first principal. In 1878, the Tainan Clergy Association decided to build additional classrooms and dormitories next to the “New Building.” After its completion in 1880 (the sixth year of the Guangxu reign of the Qing Dynasty), the “University” relocated from Erlaokou to Xizhuwei, the site of what is now the northern campus of the seminary. During the early Japanese occupation, the school’s affairs flourished; however, the “University” facilities were insufficient, prompting Pastor Baker to return to England in 1901 for fundraising, which resulted in the purchase of land south of the “New Building” for construction the following year. The building was completed in 1903 in the southern campus, with classrooms and a chapel on the first floor and student dormitories on the second floor, henceforth referred to as the “Main Building,” which established the scale of the southern and northern campuses.

The “Main Building” is oriented south to north, with an H-shaped symmetry. It comprises five areas: the central section, the east wing, the west wing, the east outer wing, and the west outer wing. The central section and the east and west outer wings slightly protrude from the main body, while the west outer wing features a corridor connecting to the western campus. The east and west wings are the shortest of the “Main Building.” The building features a double-sloped roof with a “王” (Wang) shaped configuration. The ridge of the central section and the two wings has decorative tiles at the ends, and the roof is covered with cement tiles, with some old tiles moved to the west campus during renovations in 2011. The facades facing east and west are symmetrical, with an octagonal spire on the northern ridge. The northern gable features a wooden cross, while the southern side is adorned with three decorative quatrefoils; the gables of the east and west outer wings have quatrefoil ventilation holes. The doors and windows are characterized by three styles: solid wood pointed arches, solid wood round arches, and solid wood squares, with all openings in the central section and outer wings having pointed arches.

The “Main Building” is primarily used as classrooms, with a foyer in the central section that connects the two wings through courtyards leading to the outer wings. The interior wall of the first-floor foyer is mainly divided into three segments, while the second-floor features two segments. The roof framework uses a post-and-lintel system, with wooden components joined using screws and L-shaped iron plates. There are traces of an arched ventilation opening in the red brick partition wall connecting the east wing and central section, and in 1955, an R.C. beam was added at the top of the wall. The west outer wing features a half-open door between the tenth classroom and the music classroom, with a small bar under the door. The second-floor ceiling is equipped with 15 ventilation openings to facilitate airflow, shaped as square boxes that protrude upward, with openings in the surrounding upright boards. The columns in the classrooms of the “Main Building” have rounded bases, and the column capitals resemble the Tuscan style.

Tainan Theological College features many ancient square copper locks on classroom doors, with a switch above that locks the two-part door when pushed to the right. The windows retain numerous traditional sliding window fixatives that secure the iron components on the window sills. The stairs are constructed of reinforced concrete.

Northern Presbytery (Taipei Presbyterian Church)

In 1872, George L. Mackay (馬偕), also known as Ma Jiei, was commissioned by the Presbyterian Church of Canada to begin mission work in Tamsui. Mackay devoted 29 years to his mission, achieving considerable success. In the first decade, he established twenty churches and baptized 300 believers. In the second decade, he founded Oxford College (牛津學堂, also known as 理學堂大書院), training pastoral staff and expanding Christianity into the Yilan area, establishing up to 60 churches in total over his lifetime.

However, during this time, Mackay tended towards “autocratic” leadership. Although he seemingly operated within a presbytery system, it was derisively referred to as a “false presbytery.” They would gather once a month in Tamsui, consisting of Mackay, William Gauld (吳威廉), Yan Qinghua (嚴清華), Chen Ronghui (陳榮輝), and several elders from subordinate churches. During meetings, it was always Mackay and Yan Qinghua who spoke, and the attending members approved every matter without recording the discussions or providing any official seals, earning it the nickname “false presbytery.”

After Mackay’s death, William Gauld took over the leadership. Under his leadership, in May 1904, together with Thurlow Fraser and Yan Qinghua, they petitioned the Canadian mother church for permission to establish a presbytery to promote self-governance.On October 4 of the same year, the Northern Presbytery (initially known as the Taipei Presbyterian Church) was established, with members including missionary William Gauld (吳威廉, Yú Wēilián) as president, local pastor Yan Qinghua (嚴清華) and 12 elders from various churches. After discussions among the members, the 57 churches in the northern region were divided into 12 “congregations,” and regulations were established to promote church affairs and hire additional pastors, hoping that churches across the region would gradually become independent. The “Records of the Taipei Presbyterian Church” at that time stated that the presbytery was composed of pastors and elders, with the purpose of governing church affairs for the glory of God and the benefit of humanity. The meeting began by announcing the main objectives of the presbytery, holding communion, and discussing congregation boundaries, church history, and rules for hiring pastors. As historical records note, “The pastors of the Taipei Jesus (耶穌) Holy Church and the elders of each congregation gathered at the Huwei Grand Academy to propose the establishment of a presbytery, following the command of God to govern the Taipei Church for the glory of God.”

Subsequently, in 1905, the second meeting discussed updates to the facilities of the women’s school, as well as the planning of a junior high school and the reopening of the medical hall. To support the affairs of the presbytery, the Canadian mother church quickly dispatched five new missionaries by the end of that year (1905). William Gauld, maintaining a democratic approach, subsequently established the Mission Council, facilitating a shared responsibility for church management among both new and veteran missionaries, and records of each meeting were submitted to the Canadian mother church to help them better understand the current situation and development of the church in Taiwan.

Moreover, in the following year (1906), Chen Qingyi (陳清義), Guo Xixin (郭希信), and Xiao Anju (蕭安居) passed the examinations to qualify as pastors. They could be considered the first pastors appointed according to the presbytery’s “formal” standards after the era of Mackay, subsequently serving in Mengjia, Xindian, and Hsinchu, respectively. In 1907, Li Renshui (李壬水) and Guo Chunmu (郭春木) also became pastors, marking the gradual transition of the northern churches toward a “new era of missionary teams,” further solidifying their influence.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Taipei Medical School Christian Youth Association

From the Japanese occupation to the early post-war period, the Christian youth movement has shown a remarkable continuity. This “prehistory” fostered a generation influenced positively by the international Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA), along with the journey of young people towards autonomy and self-determination. At that time, schools, churches, and business professionals gradually organized youth groups, which complemented each other and developed in diverse directions. In 1898, a “Taiwan YMCA” primarily composed of Japanese was established in Taipei, initiating educational and service activities such as sports workshops, religious film screenings, concerts, dance classes, English Bible study sessions, disaster relief for the poor, and the publication of the journal “Taiwan Youth” (臺灣青年).

In November 1903, students at Taipei Medical School established a Christian youth association, with participants including Zhou Fuquan (周福全), Liao Huanzhang (廖煥章), Wu Keji (吳克己), and Zhao Dusheng (趙篤生). By 1904, there were more than 20 Christian students (including some Japanese) at Taipei Medical School who wished to maintain their faith life on campus and promote fellowship. Zhou Fuquan sought permission from the school administration to borrow a venue, establishing the “Taipei Medical School Christian Youth Association” with a charter consisting of 12 articles. The officers included Huang Huiwo (黃惠我), Liu Huanxiang (劉煥詳), Wu Keji (吳克己), Li Shifang (李士方), and Zhao Dusheng (趙篤生), showcasing a degree of autonomy. A larger student YMCA was formed in the 1910s, led by Zhou Zaifu (周再福), which came to be known as “Taipei Medical College YMCA.” In 1913, the “Joint Promotion Society” from the Government General’s National Language School was merged into it, convening weekly at the Mengxia Church.

During the Japanese occupation period, only the Japanese-led “Taiwan YMCA” officially joined the YMCA Japan Alliance and the World Association. As for the “Student YMCA,” despite its self-identification as YMCA, it never joined the Japanese alliance due to potential national consciousness, thus representing a locally developed prototype of YMCA.

Establishment Movement for Anglo-Japanese College

In January 1904, church members in Tainan initiated a movement to establish the Anglo-Japanese College (英和學院) and submitted a petition to the Mission Council. By February 1904, the Mission Council “sympathetically acknowledged the hopes expressed by many brothers for the establishment of this college,” and decided to appoint missionaries Song Zhongjian (Duncan Ferguson) and Fei Renchun (Frederick R. Johnson) to discuss financial resources, board responsibilities, land acquisition, construction estimates, curriculum, faculty selection, annual costs, student regulations, and the naming of the college with local Taiwanese. However, by April, after consultations with local believers regarding the establishment of Anglo-Japanese College, it was concluded that currently no clear opinion could be provided on the college’s future, and numerous questions regarding the blueprint, curriculum, management, and operations remained unresolved. Consequently, given the economic tension from the Russo-Japanese War and the difficulty in securing Western instructors to manage the college, the Mission Council decided to temporarily shelve the project.

The historical significance of this event lies in the fact that the core of the “cross-cultural” elements brought by Christianity was school education.

The “secondary school,” founded in 1885, initially could not be financially supported by local believers, and there was a disparity in expectations between the believers and the Mission Council; they did not want it to be merely a preparatory school for a seminary. They wished to emulate the Fuzhou Yinhua College or Meiji Gakuin in Japan to establish the “Anglo-Japanese College,” focusing on “the primary goal of promoting education and civilization.” However, the Mission Council did not support this, and the believers lacked funds. Some funds were redirected to Taichung Middle School, prompting attention from the Governor’s Office, which forced believers to cease donations. Later, in 1915, after the school relocated outside East Gate, local believers gained more control over its management and formed support groups to raise funds. However, under the premise of “visiting Shinto shrines” recognized by the authorities, tensions arose among governing power, religious education, and local education.

Key Theological Issues

Modern Education during the Japanese Occupation Period

In the early years of Japanese rule, the Governor-General’s Office suppressed the power of Taiwanese and foreign interests in politics, economics, and education, but had little interference with existing temple beliefs and foreign clergy in the realm of religion. At the same time, the Governor-General’s Office viewed Christianity as a representative of Western culture, leading to a favorable attitude towards churches, which allowed missionary work to progress fairly smoothly. On the other hand, most foreign clergymen generally affirmed the governance of the Governor-General’s Office, believing that the “atmosphere of progress” greatly benefited the development of the church. During the transition of power, a new generation of intellectuals grew up in the society of Japanese-occupied Taiwan; they not only received modern education but many also studied abroad. This group, known as the “Yiyi War Post-Generation” (乙未戰後新生代), was different in both background and ideology from previous and subsequent generations. This period’s Christians were no exception; they began to engage with Japanese culture and, in some cases, were born as Japanese citizens. Church leaders believed that Christians had earlier and better opportunities to receive education compared to ordinary Taiwanese, so Christians were expected to become good citizens, performing well and living a disciplined life. In the early days of Japanese rule, general Taiwanese harbored doubts about Japanese education, fearing that their children would be conscripted after attending school, but many Christians quickly accepted education from the Japanese-language schools and aspired to study in Japan. At that time, the total number of Christians was less than one percent of the population in Taiwan, but compared to the general Taiwanese populace, they were more capable of responding to and accepting Japan’s modern education. For example, there were many Christians among the early medical school students, who accounted for a quarter of the graduated students between 1902 and 1906, a rate that warrants attention. Furthermore, due to their active acceptance of Western-style education, Christians initiated a trend for Taiwanese students to study abroad. As a result, Christians perpetually faced decisions between “assimilation” and “modernization,” which prompted them to deeply contemplate issues of identity.

Hymnal

Taiwan’s first church hymnal, “Hymn Book” (聖詩歌), was compiled by British Presbyterian missionary Rev. Gan Weilin (甘為霖) in Tainan in 1900. This hymnal, which was lost for decades, was finally rediscovered in 1988. It includes 122 hymns. The “Hymn Book” contains only one hymn with a melody; the remaining 121 consist solely of lyrics written in Tainan pronunciation using Romanization, without Chinese characters. Because these hymnals had lyrics without tunes or scores, a leader would recite the lyrics, then start the tune for the congregation to join in the singing. When organs were established, selecting hymn tunes was mostly the responsibility of the organist.

Formosa under the Dutch Era

“Formosa under the Dutch” (荷蘭時代的福爾摩沙), translated from Dutch historical materials, is a representative work by Taiwan’s missionary pioneer Rev. Gan Weilin (William Campbell). Since its publication in 1903, it has been highly regarded, remaining a classic essential for studying Taiwan’s history during the Dutch colonial period. The English version of the book consists of three parts. The first part includes geographical, trade, and religious descriptions about Taiwan from Francois Valentyn’s authoritative work, “Oud en Nieuw Oost-Indien,” as well as the famous report on Taiwanese indigenous peoples written by the first Dutch resident pastor in Taiwan, Ganjishi. The second part draws from J.A. Grothe’s “Early Dutch Overseas Mission Files,” mainly consisting of letters and resolutions from administrative personnel and church members in Taiwan, detailing indigenous customs, historical grievances between different community groups, enemy-friend relations between indigenous peoples and Han Chinese, competition for Taiwan’s sovereignty from Japanese and Spanish forces, as well as the Dutch colonial government’s promotion of trade, agriculture, and missionary affairs in Taiwan, with thorough first-hand accounts that are quite valuable. The third part is a summarized translation of “The Forgotten Taiwan,” written by the last Dutch governor, which also has a separate published Chinese version.

Church Governance

In 1893, Rev. Thomas Barclay (牧師巴克禮) read a paper on “The Autonomy of the Local Church” at a missionary gathering (《教會史話》 413), while Rev. William Campbell (甘為霖牧師) also published a short article titled “Governance of the Church” in the church bulletin using colloquial script (《臺南府城教會報》第99卷，光緒19年6月，頁16).

In this article, Rev. Campbell pointed out that there are three main models for church governance: Episcopacy, Congregationalism, and Presbyterianism. The Presbyterianism model usually includes the establishment of a presbytery (later referred to as “督會” and then renamed “小會”), a synod (later renamed “中會”), and a general assembly (later referred to as “大會” and the highest level referred to as “總會”). Under the Presbyterian system, elders govern the church, and the pastor is also considered an elder.Thus, Rev. Campbell published the list of clergy serving in the southern churches, titled “Register of Elders in Tainan Prefecture” (see 《教會史話》 414).

The southern churches naturally adopted the Presbyterian system. Therefore, Rev. Campbell later provided a detailed introduction to the operational principles of the Presbyterian system in a piece titled “Governance of the Church.” This text was published in the Tainan Church Bulletin, covering seven topics (issues 232-236, 240, and 146, from July 1904 to September 1905), and was later released as a separate booklet in 1915.

His main reference was the system of the British Presbyterian Church, translating “Session” as “長老會” (Elder’s Assembly) and “presbytery” as “大會” (Synod); the highest level was referred to as “總會” (General Assembly) with the third level as Synod, and the highest level as Assembly. Thus, Rev. Campbell’s writing is divided into four chapters: (1) Congregation, (2) Elder’s Assembly, (3) Synod, (4) General Assembly. The chairperson of the Elder’s Assembly, Synod, and General Assembly was referred to as “會正” (later changed to “議長” or moderator), while the recorder was called “紀事” (later changed to “書記” or clerk). Because the Elder’s Assembly became the later “小會” (Local Assembly) and the Synod became the later “中會” (Regional Assembly), for convenience, “小會” and “中會” will be used instead of the original terms.

Under the Presbyterian system, the “小會” is composed of elders and the pastor (the pastor being a teaching elder), with responsibilities including accepting new members, processing transfers, maintaining a “Membership List” (those not subject to church discipline may partake in communion—referred to as “Chheng-khi-mia e”; divided into adults and children), keeping a “Record Book,” convening meetings (known as “Leng hoe”), and striving to promote the spiritual growth of the members… Additionally, it mandates a minimum of three members for the local assembly. It contains guidelines for the Deacon Board; typically, the local pastor serves as the administrator for both the local assembly and the Deacon Board. The “中會” is formed by the pastors and elders elected from various congregations within the region. Since it handles matters of pastor appointment, commissioning, and dismissal, Rev. Campbell elaborates on facets such as “Assistant Pastors,” “Pastor Vacancies,” “Regulations for Hiring Pastors,” “Establishing Pastors,” and “Transitioning Pastors.” There are also provisions for congregational requests to the Synod.

Rev. Campbell’s “Governance of the Church” serves as an important document for understanding the organization process of local churches!

Anti-Christian Leaders

Lín Dēngguì Incident

Lín Dēngguì (林登貴), also known as Dīng Guì (丁貴), courtesy name Yùqí (毓奇), hailing from Mādu (麻豆) in Tainan, attended the predecessor of the Tainan Theological Seminary, known as “Capital College” (大學), in 1894. During the invasion of southern Taiwan by Japanese troops in 1895, he narrowly escaped death in the Mādu Incident, where violent mobs committed massacres. On the eve of the Japanese army entering Tainan City, he volunteered to accompany missionaries to meet General Nogi Maresuke (乃木希典).

In September 1899, Lín Dēngguì, who was serving as a teacher at the Tainan Tīngzǎijiǎo Elementary School, was accused of having an inappropriate relationship with a married woman, Mrs. Khim (音譯). While the matter appeared to be easily manageable, Lín’s vehement protests transformed him from a mere believer into a figure opposing the church, causing considerable turmoil. This incident is regarded as the longest recorded event in the historical annals of the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan, leading to significant unrest within the church.

At that time, the members of the Tainan Ministerial Association included Dr. Peter Anderson (安彼得) and pastors Duncan Ferguson (宋忠堅), William Campbell (甘為霖), and Andrew B. Neilson (廉德烈), totaling four individuals. Records indicate that the first two believed there was room for discussion, while the latter two felt that Lín should be dismissed. The two sides remained deadlocked, and even after requesting the intervention of Pastor Campbell Moody (梅監務) from Changhua and Dr. David Landsborough III (蘭大衛), no resolution was reached. Ultimately, the Overseas Mission Board of the Presbyterian Church in England was alerted, prompting them to send Pastor William McGregor (倪為霖) from Xiamen to Taiwan to mediate.

The investigation committee interviewed Huang Yuede (黃月德, Elder), Wu Hai (吳道源, Deacon), Xu Shang (徐賞, Doctor), Guo Mu, Yan Yongshen (顏永嬸), and Zhao Juexiang (趙爵祥, Evangelist, also recorded as Bu-siong (武祥) or Zhu Chang (祝常)), as well as Mrs. Khim, on February 18, 1900, at the Tīngzǎijiǎo chapel. Subsequently, on March 1, 1900, Lín Dēngguì heard the committee’s Chinese judgment read in the presence of ministerial members, local elders, deacons, and himself, while the English record was noted in the minutes. The content was as follows:

“There is no evidence of any crime or hidden malevolent intention between the two in their interactions. Therefore, we determine that they are not guilty of such wrongdoing.

Due to the potential for young men and women’s interactions to incite suspicion, all slander should be avoided; this is the duty of church members, especially important for those preparing for ministry.

We determine that Dēngguì did not take sufficient precautions to avoid rumors.

We determine that Dēngguì showed a poor attitude during the investigation, stating, ‘I forgot, go ask someone else’ or ignoring the notice of dismissal from Gān Wéilín (甘為霖), only responding with ‘I am busy,’ and even resorting to harsh words in a letter addressed to superiors, showing a lack of humility.

We determine that Dēngguì’s insulting remarks were a grave mistake, thus it is not advisable for him to enter the seminary hastily. However, we recommend that the Ministerial Association employ him for another year at another elementary school to observe his conduct before considering his admission to the seminary.”

Lín Dēngguì was dismissed from the Ministerial Association due to the allegations involving Mrs. Khim, but one year later, in 1901, he successfully re-enrolled at “Capital College,” indicating that his character had been accepted. According to the first issue of the Alumni Association Magazine of the Private Tainan Presbyterian Theological Seminary (1928.1), Lín was listed in the “Alumni Directory,” and he indeed enrolled successfully, although he did not become a formal pastor.
