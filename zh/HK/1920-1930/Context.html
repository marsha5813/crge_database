Hong Kong: Context (1920-1930)

Governmental Regulations on Religion and Legal Cases

Young Men’s Christian Association Ordinance, 1923

In June 1923, the Legislative Council passed the Young Men’s Christian Association Ordinance, establishing the corporate status of the Young Men’s Christian Association of Hong Kong. The YMCA originated from the “Hong Kong Young Men’s Christian Association” (Western Section) founded in 1901. In 1908, the Western and Chinese sections became independent entities, each with its own Board of Directors. The Chinese section became the “Chinese Young Men’s Christian Association of Hong Kong,” while the Western section continued as the “Young Men’s Christian Association of Hong Kong.”

German Missions Trustees Ordinance, 1923(Leung 2020: 175–176; 梁翠華2020: 123–124)

In July 1923, the Legislative Council passed the German Missions Trustees Ordinance, primarily to address the lack of legal basis for the trustees established in 1919. According to the ordinance, the German Missions Trust consisted of five members appointed by the Governor, including Bishop Charles R. Duppuy (Anglican Church), Ernest J. Barnett (Anglican Missionary Society), Thomas W. Pearce (London Missionary Society), John K. Maconachie (Union Church Kowloon), and Charles C. Porri (Methodist English Church). The trust had full ownership and control over properties and revenues previously belonging to German missions in Hong Kong, ensuring they were used to sustain the missions’ original work.

Bishop of Victoria Incorporation Ordinance, 1925(Hong Kong Law, 1925)

In March 1925, the Legislative Council passed the Bishop of Victoria Incorporation Ordinance, formally establishing the corporate status of the Bishop of Victoria. Among other provisions, it transferred the ownership of St. Paul’s College’s land (Inland Lot No. 76) to the incorporated entity of the Bishop of Victoria.

Sailors’ Home Ordinance, 1925(Davies 2017)

In May 1925, the Legislative Council passed the Sailors’ Home Ordinance. The Sailors’ Home had been established in 1863 in Sai Wan by the Anglican Church to cater to the pastoral needs of foreign sailors transiting through Hong Kong. In 1930, the Sailors’ Home merged with the Mission to Seafarers to form The Sailors’ Home and Mission to Seafarers.

Chinese Temple Ordinance, 1928(Chen 2018; Chen 2023: 127–153; 危丁明2007: 39–44)

In April 1928, the Legislative Council passed the Chinese Temple Ordinance, aiming “to suppress and prevent abuses in the management of Chinese temples and in the administration of the funds of Chinese temples.” The ordinance defined “temples” broadly to include temples, monasteries, Taoist temples, nunneries, and sites for worship or fortune-telling. It required temples to register with the Secretary for Chinese Affairs’ office; otherwise, they could not operate. The Secretary for Chinese Affairs was empowered to investigate and confiscate temples if necessary.

Under the ordinance, the newly established Chinese Temples Committee served as the regulatory body. The committee, composed mainly of Chinese elites and chaired ex officio by the Secretary for Chinese Affairs, oversaw temple management. Temples were required to allocate a portion of their surplus income to the General Chinese Charities Fund. The ordinance primarily targeted the ownership and management of temple properties, stipulating that temple revenues must be used for charitable purposes.

1928年4月，立法局通過《華人廟宇條例》（Chinese Temple Bill），旨在「遏止及預防華人廟宇管理失當及華人廟宇基金遭濫用」（to suppress and prevent abuses in the management of Chinese temples and in the administration of the funds of Chinese temples）。條例對「廟宇」作出界定，除了廟、寺、觀、道院及庵外，也包括拜神或占卦算命之場地。條例要求廟宇酬要向華民政務司署（office of the Secretary for Chinese Affaires）登記，否則不得開設。華民政務司又被賦予權力，可以查抄廟宇。同時，根據條例，新成立「華人廟宇委員會」(Chinese Temples Committee)作執行機構，成員主要是華人紳商，華民政務司是當然主席。各廟宇須將若干盈餘撥入「普通華人慈善基金」（General Chinese Charities Fund）。條例主要針對廟產的擁有權和管理權，並規定其收入必須用於慈善。

Social Hostility toward Christianity

The Suspension of Break (Da Po) (Xing Fuzeng, 2014)

In 1923, an incident in the history of Christian publishing in Hong Kong led to the suspension of a weekly publication after only 10 issues. The publication, titled Break (Da Po), ceased due to ideological disputes. According to Mak Ling Sang, who was working for the Anglican Church at the time, Break was initiated in 1923 by a group of secretaries from the Hong Kong Chinese YMCA, including figures such as Wong Chai Fan, Tu Ching Yuen, Fok Ling Kin, Lee Kin Sang, and Ko Sik On. Regarding the publication’s name, Wong Chai Fan once explained to Mak Ling Sang:

“The church’s reliance on unequal treaties as its protective charm should be broken. Foreign missionaries treating Chinese church workers like slaves should be broken. Foreign missionaries advocating respect for Confucian moral values while requiring separate seating for men and women during worship, despite their familial closeness as husbands, wives, parents, and siblings, should be broken. The church’s allowance for wealthy members to take concubines and keep maidservants should be broken. The practice of foreign missionaries and Chinese church leaders eagerly visiting affluent members with minor ailments to pray and offer comfort, while ignoring the poor, should be broken…”

The birth of Break thus stemmed from the reformist demands of a group discontented with the conservatism within the church. The cover design featured several skulls, reflecting its critical stance. This sharp criticism displeased foreign clergy in the Hong Kong church, including John I. McPherson, the foreign secretary of the YMCA. McPherson suspected Break of harboring political motives and even notified the police to monitor it closely. At the time, some remarked, “Rebellion! Rebellion! They’ve consumed the anti-church poison of Chen Duxiu and are rebelling against the church!”

The immediate cause of Break’s suspension was an article in its 10th issue that reported on the wedding banquet of Wong Do On, the son of Reverend Wong Oi Tong of the Hong Kong Methodist Church (and a YMCA board member). The article reportedly insulted Wong Oi Tong, who subsequently resigned from his YMCA board position. While the board unanimously urged Wong to stay, opinions were divided regarding the handling of Break and the YMCA staff involved.

McPherson, along with other prominent figures such as Reverend William Hammond of the London Missionary Society, Reverend Arthur D. Stewart (Principal of St. Paul’s College, Anglican Church), Wong Oi Tong, Reverend Denniston of the Seamen’s Mission, and Tang Hip Chi (general secretary of the United Brethren Church), vehemently opposed Break. They labeled its content heretical and politically motivated, insisting it should be banned by government authorities. In contrast, figures like Reverend Lee Kau Yan of the Anglican Church, Reverend Chang Chuk Ling of the To Tsai Church, and Elder Mak Mui Sang of the Methodist Church believed the matter was purely religious and unrelated to politics.

Ultimately, McPherson deemed Tu and Wong unfit for their positions and forced their resignations. Break was subsequently compelled to cease publication. Under pressure from Stewart, Mak Ling Sang also left Hong Kong.

Geopolitical Dynamics and International Relationships

The Tumultuous 1920s: Communist Movements, Strikes, and Hong Kong

(Carroll 2005: 131–158; 2007: 96–105; Liu Shuyong 2016: 149–161; Chan M 2003)

In 1921, the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) was founded in Shanghai. In August 1923, the CCP Hong Kong Special Branch was established, with Lam Kwan Wai serving as cadre director. Lam, who worked as an inspector for the Hong Kong government, had already been involved in publishing Truth, Goodness, and Beauty in Hong Kong between 1920 and 1921, a journal introducing Marxism. Lam had an opportunity to meet Chen Duxiu in Hong Kong in late 1920, where Chen encouraged them to form a Marxist study group. Later, they established contact with the Guangdong Regional Committee and, upon returning to Hong Kong, founded a Socialist Youth League. In October 1923, the Hong Kong Local Committee (Local Executive Committee) was formed, with Leung Pang Man as chairman. Early party activities focused on organizing labor movements among young workers, while student movements remained relatively weak.

In the early 1920s, labor consciousness in Hong Kong began to rise, leading to the first union-organized strike. In the spring of 1920, the Mechanics Union initiated a strike demanding a 40% wage increase to offset the 50% devaluation of the Hong Kong dollar after World War I and the rising cost of living. Employers repeatedly refused these demands, prompting workers to strike on March 31, with nearly 10,000 participants within a week. Many strikers returned to Guangzhou, where they received support from Sun Yat-sen’s government and local unions. After over three weeks of strikes, the employers, under pressure from the Chinese Affairs Secretary, agreed to a 32.5% wage increase. The 1920 strike marked a significant moment in the history of Hong Kong labor movements, characterized as a purely worker-organized action for rights. Notably, Hong Kong workers received solidarity from Guangzhou unions, inspiring Guangzhou workers to strike in 1921.

From January to March 1922, the Hong Kong Seamen’s Union (Chinese Seamen’s Industrial Union) organized a major strike. By late January, over 10,000 seamen had left Hong Kong for Guangzhou. The strike, supported by the Guangzhou military government, received financial assistance and a warm welcome from Guangzhou Mayor Sun Fo.

The colonial government viewed the strike as more than an economic movement, identifying it as a political one with support from Guangzhou. Local Chinese business leaders also believed the strike was incited by leftist ideologies influenced by “Bolshevism.” Consequently, the government adopted a hardline stance, declaring the Seamen’s Union an “illegal organization” that threatened colonial order. Police raided the union, arrested its leaders, and prompted the union to call for further strikes, resulting in 100,000 workers leaving for Guangzhou. The Legislative Council passed the Emergency Regulations Ordinance, and the governor suspended train services to Guangzhou. Workers walked back to Guangdong, with some being fired upon by police in Sha Tin, resulting in five deaths and eight injuries, further fueling resistance. Ultimately, the Chinese Affairs Secretary negotiated with the Guangzhou government, leading to the strike’s resolution on March 7, with significant concessions made to the workers. Labor scholar Chan Ming Kiu observed that the colonial government’s measures turned the strike “from an economic movement into a full-scale political confrontation between British colonial power and Chinese national dignity.” Following the strike, its leaders Lam Wai Man and Su Zhaozheng joined the CCP.

The 1920 and 1922 strikes in Hong Kong were primarily driven by local factors. However, the Canton-Hong Kong General Strike of 1925–1926 was evidently influenced by Chinese revolutionary nationalism. In 1925, the May 30th Incident in Shanghai, where British police fired on demonstrators in the International Settlement, sparked nationwide anti-British protests. On June 23, the Shamian Massacre in Guangzhou further incited outrage, prompting Guangzhou workers and union leaders to call for a general strike in Hong Kong. Anti-British leaflets appeared in Hong Kong, urging Chinese to expel British colonialists and their local collaborators. Members of the Hong Kong Communist Party actively engaged in anti-British propaganda and organized workers for resistance.

Following the Shamian Massacre, over 50,000 Chinese left Hong Kong in protest, severely impacting the local economy. By late July, the number of Chinese returning to Guangdong exceeded 250,000. The strike lasted over a year and coincided with a boycott of British goods. Governor Stubbs declared a state of emergency, censored Chinese newspapers, and prohibited the publication of strike advertisements. He also suggested contacting regional warlords to overthrow the Guangzhou Nationalist Government, but his proposals were not supported by the British government. In 1925, Sir Cecil Clementi, a noted Sinologist, succeeded Stubbs as governor, reflecting Britain’s hope to resolve the crisis diplomatically.

Clementi made several attempts to negotiate with the Guangzhou government. However, internal divisions between left and right factions within the Guangzhou Nationalist Government, coupled with Chiang Kai-shek’s Northern Expedition to unify China, changed the political landscape. By October 1926, the Guangzhou government agreed to end the strike, concluding the 15-month Canton-Hong Kong General Strike.

The strike highlighted the power of Chinese nationalism. However, local Chinese business leaders, eager to cooperate with the British for their own interests, rejected the radical methods of communism. In May 1926, Clementi appointed Chow Shou-son as the first Chinese member of the Executive Council, a position later succeeded by Lo Yuk-wo in 1936.

In 1927, Chiang Kai-shek initiated the Purge of Communists, targeting both the CCP and leftist factions within the Kuomintang. After the purge, the CCP Guangdong Regional Committee relocated to Hong Kong, making the city a command center for South China’s communist movement. In response, the colonial government implemented stricter measures to curb communist activities.

The General Strike also led to changes in colonial governance. To counteract radical nationalism, the colonial government sought to promote Confucian education. In 1927, the University of Hong Kong established a Chinese Studies Faculty, providing a platform for former Qing loyalists to promote traditional Chinese culture and ethics, countering revolutionary nationalism.

After the establishment of the Nationalist Government in Nanjing, Britain pursued cooperation with the Kuomintang, returning leased territories such as Hankou and Jiujiang in 1927–1928 and Weihaiwei in 1930. There were even suggestions to return Hong Kong to China. Despite the influence of Chinese nationalism during the General Strike, it did not evolve into anti-colonialism. Compared to the turbulent political situation in neighboring Guangdong, Hong Kong’s Chinese population valued the stability and economic opportunities of colonial rule. However, the enduring social realities of racial segregation and inequality remained unchanged in colonial Hong Kong.
