Korea: Agency (1920-1930)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

  1. Choi, Byung-hun 최병헌 崔炳憲

1858/01/26 (Choongcheong-do) ~1927/?/? (?)

Though he lacked formal Western education credentials, Choi possessed extensive knowledge of Chinese classical literature. Despite multiple unsuccessful attempts at the Joseon civil service examinations, Methodist missionary Henry Appenzeller recognized his scholarly abilities and brought him to Baejae School to teach Chinese classics. Through Appenzeller's influence, Choi was introduced to Christianity and eventually became a pastor.

Professional Roles and Achievements

Choi served multiple roles as a pastor, Chinese literature scholar, educator, and activist in Korea's literary independence movement. His most significant contribution was creating a theoretical framework for Korean-adapted Christianity, drawing from his deep understanding of Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist traditions. He pioneered comparative religious studies and Christian dialectical discourse in Korea. His initial publication appeared in 1908, followed by collections of his works including religious comparative studies published in 1922.

Advancing Protestant Literary Mission Work

Choi's background in Chinese literature shaped his approach to missionary work. After failing the civil service examinations three times, he worked as a Korean language tutor under American Methodist missionary George Heber Jones before joining Baejae School's faculty. During this period, Western missionaries faced suspicion from locals, who sometimes called them derogatory names and blamed them for various troubles, including incidents like the unrest in Pyongyang in 1988.

Choi adopted a different perspective, recognizing the genuine efforts of Western missionaries to address poverty and suffering among the Korean people. Using his Confucian knowledge as a foundation, he worked to understand Christianity and its representatives. According to Lee Deok-ju's analysis, Choi's religious conversion stemmed from his concerns about governmental corruption and uncertainty about the future, his appreciation for Western missionaries' humanitarian work, and his view of Christianity as a path toward Korean modernization.

Following his baptism by Jones, Choi served as an assistant pastor at Jeongdong First Church in Seoul and later became the church's first Korean pastor after Appenzeller's death in a drowning accident while rescuing someone. He simultaneously held a government position, which allowed him to advocate for ending military actions by various resistance groups.

Choi's participation in rural enlightenment efforts has generated scholarly debate about the true nature of his contributions. Despite political complications, he wrote a serialized novel examining Christianity's relationship with existing religions, published in a religious monthly publication. These writings were later compiled and published in 1912, representing the first dialectical Christian work challenging traditional Korean religions. Through his dialectical publications, Choi presented Christianity as a natural extension of Korean culture and indigenous beliefs rather than a foreign intrusion. Some researchers have suggested that Choi, working with Yun Chi-ho and Ahn Chang-ho, may have contributed to writing the Korean national anthem lyrics, which would exemplify his approach to indigenous theology.

Choi's perspectives appealed to intellectuals struggling with competing ideologies while seeking paths to personal salvation and national improvement. He actively participated in the Independence Club and contributed to independence-related publications. He also assisted Jones in producing an English-Korean dictionary in 1914, with Jones acknowledging Choi's crucial contributions in accurate translation work and vocabulary selection as Western terminology increasingly entered Korean usage.

Additionally, Choi played an important role in preserving the Korean language during the colonial period. Beyond Bible translations, he published Korean-language newspapers and served as an editor for various Korean-language religious publications. His work significantly enhanced understanding of Christianity within the context of Korean religious traditions and culture, earning respect from intellectuals seeking to strengthen Korea during that era. These ideas later influenced theologians and church leaders in developing Korean-contextualized Christianity.

Developing Indigenous Theological Framework

Choi's comparative religious study initiated systematic examination of world religions, covering major traditions including Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism, Islam, Hinduism, and newer movements such as Tenrikyo and Gyeongcheon-gyo, while providing justification for Christianity. He argued that Confucianism, within the broader religious context, viewed virtuous individuals as heaven-like, functioning not merely as monotheistic belief but as a polytheistic system incorporating ancestor veneration, earth deities, and mountain spirits.

Regarding Buddhism, he criticized its lack of afterlife promises and its passive stance toward social service and societal transformation. Choi's fundamental argument was not that Confucianism, Buddhism, or Daoism were inherently flawed, but rather that their essential truths found fulfillment within Christianity. He noted that Christians similarly embraced the concept of spiritual rebirth through Christ. Kim Kwang-sik characterized Choi's viewpoint as emphasizing that authentic Christianity focuses not on metaphysical theories or worldly power expansion, nor does its validity depend on miraculous demonstrations, but rather on human salvation as its core purpose.

Choi's inclusive methodology of incorporating rather than rejecting other religious traditions earned respect within the Korean Christian community of his time and continues to generate discussion in contemporary theological circles.

  1. Kim, Maria. 김마리아 金瑪利亞

1892/06/18 (Hwanghae-do, Jangyeon) ~ 1944/03/23 (Hamgyeongnam-do, Wonsan)

Kim Maria (김마리아 金瑪利亞) Born: June 18, 1892 (Jangyeon, Hwanghae Province) - Died: March 23, 1944 (Wonsan, South Hamgyong Province)

Educational Background: Kim Maria came from a wealthy Christian family where her father had embraced Christianity early and founded a local elementary school. Several family members were involved in independence activities within the provisional government. She completed her studies at Jeong-Shin Girls' School, an institution focused on developing Korean female leaders rather than promoting Western-style education for women. A significant influence was Shin Maria (1873-1921), a deputy principal who advocated that believers should demonstrate love for their country and fellow citizens while serving as positive influences globally.

Kim Maria provided medical assistance at Severance Hospital to wounded independence fighters and local people who had protested against King Gojong's forced abdication. In 1914, she traveled to Japan to attend Do-kyo Women's College. She later continued her education in America in 1923, completing a master's degree at Park College and the University of Chicago, followed by a doctoral degree in theology from New York's Theological Seminary School.

Major Achievements: Her primary contributions involved establishing women-focused organizations across Japan, the United States, and Korea to advance both women's rights and the independence movement. Her leadership roles in the Korean Patriotic Women's Association and Geunhwahoe during the 1920s were particularly significant.

Tokyo Korean Women Student Council (1910s): While studying in Japan during the 1910s, Kim Maria joined approximately 500-600 other Korean students who had formed the Korean Student Association and published a biannual publication called 'Lux Scientiae.' Female students, initially led by Kim Pil-rye, established the Tokyo Women's Student Friendship Association in 1915 as an alternative to the male-dominated student organization. Kim Maria became the organization's second president in 1916 and launched 'Women's World' magazine in 1917, marking the first publication created by and for women during Japanese colonial rule, which gained considerable attention both domestically and internationally.

Under Kim Maria's direction, the Tokyo Women's Student Friendship Association gained recognition among Japanese exchange students and became a focal point for women's rights and independence activities. Notable female intellectuals like Na Hye-seok and Hwang Esther joined the organization, enhancing its influence. The group supported the March 1st Independence Movement in 1919 by contributing 30 won to independence efforts. However, no women were among the eleven signers of the February 8th Independence Declaration, prompting Kim Maria to return to Korea while contemplating the future direction of women's activism.

Notably, she smuggled a copy of the March 1st Independence Declaration back to Korea by hiding it in her clothing. Upon her return, she shared information about the March 1st Movement and actively organized preliminary activities for the movement in cities including Daegu, Gwangju, Seoul, and her home province of Hwanghae. At a March 2nd gathering, she reportedly urged female students to participate actively, arguing that since male students were already involved, women could not remain passive observers. She was detained by Japanese military police on March 5th but was released on August 5th after experiencing severe treatment. She subsequently joined the faculty at Jeongshin Women's School.

Korean Patriotic Women's Association (1919): On October 19, 1919, Kim Maria established the Korean Patriotic Women's Association at the residence of Lillian Dean Miller, an instructor at Jeongshin Women's School. This new organization combined two existing groups: the Blood Loyalty Women's Patriotic Association, which supported the Provisional Government, and the Greater Korea Independence Women's Patriotic Association, which operated independently but shared similar objectives.

Kim Maria expressed her vision that women should participate in independence activities with the same intensity as men. She noted that while Oh Hyun-joo had previously organized a women's association, it had limited Korean membership. Her proposal was to establish regional branches throughout Korea to enable nationwide female participation in independence work.

This approach represented a shift toward equal gender participation in independence activities, moving beyond traditional supportive roles for women in anti-Japanese resistance. The organization's second article indicated that their purpose was to support the Republic of Korea's Constitution, suggesting that women should fulfill their civic responsibilities.

Kim Maria transformed the previous localized organizational model into a national network, creating branches in North Korea, Jeju Island, and Hawaii. She forwarded the association's guidelines along with 2,000 won to Lee Seung-man, who was serving as the Provisional Government's president.

In November 1919, she and other key leaders were again arrested by Japanese military police. Missionary L.D. Miller, Dr. F.W. Schofield, and Pastor W.N. Blair advocated for her release due to harsh treatment during detention, and she was freed on May 22, 1920.

Independence Work with the Provisional Government: Her decision to seek refuge in China on July 21, 1921, surprised Japanese authorities. The independence movement was experiencing internal divisions between the Creation Party, which strongly opposed Lee Seung-man's Provisional Government, and the Reform Party, which accepted the government's authority while seeking changes. A National Representatives' Conference was organized on January 31, 1923, to address these conflicts, where Kim Maria presented a report on the Korean Patriotic Women's Association's activities.

Believing in education as a path to national salvation, she enrolled at the Bible Teacher Training School in Nanjing (now Nanjing Normal University) for additional studies. There, she organized the Korean Young Women's Association in 1923 to mobilize female students.

Geunhwahoe (U.S. Korean Patriotic Women's Association): Committed to achieving independence through education, she traveled to the United States. After completing her studies at Park College and moving to New York, she discovered that the local Korean community's organization, the Dongjihoi, which published the March 1st newspaper, excluded women from membership. Notable figures from Korean and Chinese independence movements, including Park In-deok, Hwang Esther, and Jeong Ae-kyung, were also in New York. Together with them, she founded Geunhwahoe in 1928, with the name referencing the rose of Sharon, Korea's national flower.

Geunhwahoe's objectives included: fostering national consciousness to promote Korean unity; supporting education and professional development; informing the international community about Korea's colonial circumstances; and assisting in establishing a new nation. The organization created specialized divisions including Unemployment, Education, and Social sections to conduct systematic independence activities.

According to reports, Kim Maria emphasized that their goal was not simply promoting nationalism that might appear outdated, but rather nurturing genuine love for their homeland that could be overlooked when people become deeply involved in other pursuits.

When reports emerged that Chinese officials in Manchuria were issuing harmful directives against Korean expatriates, the Dongjihoi New York branch, New York Korean Residents Association, National Association of New York, and Geunhwahoe collaborated to form the New York Korean Expatriate Advocacy Association to support Korean emigrants. Kim Maria participated as Geunhwahoe's president.

Although Korean expatriates in New York maintained frequent contact, most stayed temporarily for work or education before relocating elsewhere, resulting in a small permanent community. Many Geunhwahoe leaders were students with uncertain status, making consistent organizational activities challenging. Nevertheless, Geunhwahoe remains significant as an organization created by Korean women abroad during the colonial period who persisted in supporting their homeland's liberation despite obstacles. Kim Maria's dedication to independence, maintained even during the difficulties of student life in a foreign country, led her to organize and actively participate in a women's anti-Japanese resistance organization.

  1. Lee, Sang-jae 이상재 李商在

1850/10/26 (Choongcheong-do, Hansan-myeon) ~ 1927/03/29 (Seoul)

Background: Lee received education in Chinese literature and held governmental positions during both the Joseon dynasty and Korean Empire periods. He aligned himself with the Enlightenment Party and participated in the Gapshin Coup of 1884 and the Gabo Revolution of 1894-1895. His religious conversion occurred in 1902 while imprisoned due to his connections with the Independence Club and Manmingongdong-hoe affair. During his incarceration, Rhee Syngman introduced him to Christianity through the Bible, with Matthew's teaching about loving one's enemies making a profound impact on him. This period of imprisonment, referred to as the "Revolutionary Party Incident," saw numerous independence activists including Kim Jeong-sik, Yoo Seng-jun, Lee Dong-ryeong, Lee Sang-jae, and Rhee Syngman embrace Protestantism. This development represented a significant shift in Korean Christianity, as it expanded beyond its previous base among middle and lower social classes to include upper-class intellectuals and activists. Following his conversion, Lee established the "Thatched House Church" in his native area and became actively involved with Hwangseong YMCA and educational initiatives for young people.

Significance: Lee Sang-jae stands as a prominent figure in Korea's enlightenment and independence movements, as well as in education, with particular focus on youth development. His legacy in Korea's independence struggle has earned recognition across political divides. His inclusion in discussions of the 1920s emphasizes his role in the Shanghai provisional government and Korean youth education initiatives.

YMCA Leadership: Following his 1904 prison release, Lee Sang-jae committed himself to YMCA activities. While the Independence Association had pursued political means to counter Russian influence on Korean sovereignty, the YMCA approach sought to resist Japanese occupation through religious and cultural channels. The transition of many Independence Association members to YMCA work after their release suggests continuity in their independence efforts through different methods.

Hwangseong YMCA at that time operated under international leadership, with Gale serving as president and Gillett as treasurer. The governing board included representatives from five nations: four Americans, three British, two Canadians, one Japanese, and two Koreans, giving the organization quasi-extraterritorial characteristics. Korean leadership appointments began in 1905, with Lee Sang-jae taking charge of the Education Department that year and later heading the Religious Department in 1908. The YMCA's focus evolved toward fostering Korean nationalist consciousness. Working alongside former Independence Association colleagues like Yoon Chi-ho and Kim Gyu-sik, Lee Sang-jae introduced contemporary sports and academic subjects including Korean language, English, and music, while also conducting biblical studies and evening classes for working youth. Unlike Chinese YMCA's sports emphasis or Japanese YMCA's church focus, the Korean branch adopted an educational model. As Japan consolidated colonial control, Lee Sang-jae traveled extensively, promoting national awakening through YMCA programs and seminars, culminating in the 1909 "One Million Souls Movement."

Organizational Independence: In 1913, Lee Sang-jae assumed the role of general secretary for the Chosun Central Christian Youth Association, replacing Gillette. Facing attempts by the Japanese colonial government to bring the Chosun YMCA under Japanese control, Lee Sang-jae rejected Japanese financial assistance and developed independent funding mechanisms. He revitalized evening educational programs for day students and enhanced vocational training opportunities. This approach aimed to extend educational access to previously excluded groups while creating an environment where both aristocratic and common classes could study together within the YMCA framework. The April 1914 Triennial Convention saw the public announcement of the "Constitution of the Union of Chosun Christian Youth Associations," formally severing ties with the Japanese YMCA.

Although selected as one of 33 national representatives for the March 1st Movement, Lee Sang-jae declined participation, citing extensive behind-the-scenes work required after the movement. His concerns included potential conflicts with missionary workers opposed to political involvement and possible YMCA suppression if he became a representative. Instead, he worked with Yun Chi-ho to bring American congressional representatives to Korea via Shanghai for international support and prepared to send Korean youth delegates to the Paris Peace Conference.

1920s Activities: Lee Sang-jae secured Chosun YMCA independence from the Japanese YMCA in May 1922, and by 1924, the Chosun YMCA gained formal membership in the World Alliance of YMCA as an autonomous organization. At the First International Christian Students' Union Conference in Beijing in April 1922, Lee Sang-jae represented the Chosun Christian Youth Association alongside female leaders Kim Pil-ye and Kim Hwal-ran. Upon returning, he contributed significantly to establishing the YWCA.

The decade saw productive collaboration between Lee Sang-jae and Lee Seung-man. Lee Sang-jae facilitated visits by Korean student groups from Hawaii, which Lee Seung-man utilized to assess Korea's independence movement situation. While Lee Sang-jae served as a key figure among domestic Christian supporters of Lee Seung-man, Lee Seung-man secured funding and support for Korean representatives to attend the Pan-Pacific Educational Conference in Hawaii in August 1921 and the Pan-Pacific Problem Research Conference in June 1925.

Lee Sang-jae also engaged actively in private university establishment efforts. He was elected president of the "Chosun Educational Association" at its June 1920 general assembly, leading to the formation of the "Chosun Private University Preparatory Committee" in November. However, fundamental Japanese opposition to university development in Korea prevented the establishment of these institutions.

During this period, both domestic and international nationalist activists recognized the need for self-reliant independence efforts and internal Korean focal points for the movement, particularly following disappointments at the Paris Peace Conference. While Lee Seung-man worked to limit Ahn Chang-ho faction influence within the Provisional Government, Lee Sang-jae led efforts to establish domestic support for Lee Seung-man's independence activities. The 1925 formation of Heungup Gullakbu served as a counter-organization to Heungsadan, working to control Christian cultural organizations in Korea for fundraising and recruitment purposes.

As the 1920s independence movement divided into nationalist and socialist camps, Lee Sang-jae accepted the presidency of Chosun Ilbo and promoted cooperation between these factions through a "National Cooperative Front." He subsequently became the first chairman of the Shin-gan Society. However, in 1927, he passed away in modest circumstances in a small Seoul attic, having never owned a home. He remained a central figure in the Christian nationalist movement until his death.

  1. Hyun, Soon, 현순, 玄楯

Birth/Death: 1880/02/28 (Seoul) ~ 1968/07/11 (Los Angeles, U.S.A.)

Hyun Soon's educational journey began with Chinese literature studies in his youth, followed by enrollment at a government English school. In 1899, he traveled to Japan for education at Suncheon Middle School, though financial constraints prevented him from pursuing his goal of attending Imperial University, forcing his return to Korea. During his time in Japan, he embraced Christianity, having been previously introduced to the faith through his involvement with the Independence Association and exposure to Yun Chi-ho's presentations in Seoul. A friend named Shim Ui-sung helped deepen his understanding of Christianity, explaining how it differed from Confucianism and Buddhism in its focus on eternal life rather than ethics or reincarnation concepts.

Back in Korea, Hyun Soon worked as a translator for the East-West Development Company, where he encountered Methodist missionary George H. Jones. Jones was actively recruiting Korean workers for Hawaii, which led to Hyun Soon and his family's 1903 immigration. Recognizing the difficulties faced by the immigrant community, he collaborated with others to establish Hawaii's first Korean church, the Honolulu Korean Methodist Church. He completed ministerial training through a local circuit program on Kauai Island in 1905 and briefly worked as an assistant pastor with Pastor Choi Byung-heon at Jungdong Jaeil Church. After returning to Korea in 1910, he enrolled at the Methodist-affiliated Hyupsung Theological Seminary, graduating in 1911 and officially becoming a Methodist minister.

Contributions

Early Ministry in Hawaii (1903-1910) Upon reaching Hawaii in 1903, Hyun Soon served dual roles as interpreter and community organizer, forming a mutual aid society with approximately fifty Korean workers who met weekly for religious services. Working with other first-generation Korean leaders and under Methodist Pastor Pearson's guidance, they founded the Korean Methodist Church in Honolulu that same year. In 1905, Pastor Wadman requested his appointment as a missionary to Kauai's plantation areas, where he ministered to roughly 2,000 Korean immigrants while continuing his interpreter duties and conflict mediation work. Many of these early Korean leaders remained active in Hawaii's Korean community movements through the 1940s and later supported Hyun Soon's subsequent endeavors.

Independence Movement Leadership (1911-1920) In February 1919, following the Korean students' February 8th Independence Declaration in Tokyo, Hyun Soon collaborated with Christian leaders including Lee Seung-hoon, Ryu Jo-dae, Ham Tae-yong, and Shin Hong-sik in planning the March 1st Movement. He received appointment as a diplomatic representative and traveled to Shanghai with the mission of delivering correspondence to President Woodrow Wilson during the Paris Peace Conference, advocating for Korean independence as essential for global reform and East Asian peace.

After the March 1st Movement, Korean independence activists established a temporary headquarters on Shanghai's Bochang Road in the French concession area. Hyun Soon served as secretary-general, coordinating communication about domestic movement developments with Korean communities in Korea, the United States, and Canada to strengthen independence movement unity and establish groundwork for the Shanghai Provisional Government. Following the Provisional Government's establishment on April 10, 1919, he held successive positions as Vice Minister of Foreign Affairs and Foreign Affairs Commission member. He played an important role in consolidating various provisional governments by recruiting figures like Kim Seong-gyeom and Lee Dong-hwi. In 1920, he became interim chairman of the North American Regional Committee, conducting fundraising tours throughout the United States. However, disagreements with the Korean community regarding a proposed Korean Embassy in America led to his return to Shanghai, with Seo Jae-pil assuming the interim chairmanship.

Return to Hawaii and Later Activities (1922 onwards) Hyun Soon returned to pastoral duties at Honolulu's Korean Methodist Church in 1922, drawing on his immigration experience to serve Korean laborers and immigrants. He conducted household visits to understand community members' circumstances and challenges. From 1923, he contributed to Korean education by helping compile English textbooks for the Methodist Church's Korean department and constructing housing for Korean leaders and Methodist Korean students. He also organized evening classes teaching Chinese characters to help Korean students maintain cultural connections.

Beginning in 1923, he became involved in the Korean-American independence movement, envisioning Hawaii as a support base for independence activities through media, economic, educational, and promotional efforts. However, he initially avoided joining major Korean organizations like the National Association or Association of Comrades. His influence within the independence movement community declined following the 1921 consulate opening and independence petition submission. Facing community rejection and difficulties advancing independence plans within organizations under Rhee Syng-man's influence, he began participating in the "Great Korean Independence Corps" from 1923.

The Independence Corps, which had competed with Rhee Syng-man's faction since March 1919, provided Hyun Soon a platform for his Hawaii independence movement ideas. He publicly criticized Rhee Syng-man's delegate administration petition on behalf of the "33 Representatives of the Great Korean Independence Corps." From 1925, he shifted support toward the Provisional Government through the "Supporting Committee for the Provisional Government," continuing fundraising efforts for Kim Gu during Rhee Syng-man's impeachment period. His political activities and fundraising efforts created tensions with the Methodist Church in the 1930s. While some historians debate whether he held socialist tendencies within his Christian beliefs, clear evidence is lacking. He remained committed to establishing unified government through Christian reform principles. Contemporary Christian historians are reassessing his contributions to Korean Christian community revival and independence movement support.

  1. Korean National Christian Council 조선예수교연합공의회 朝鮮예수敎聯合公議會

The establishment of Korea's first national Christian council emerged from post-World War I ecumenical movements. When the American "Interchurch World Movement" began in December 1918, Korean missionaries attempted to create a similar collaborative initiative. However, Korean churches showed limited enthusiasm for this unified approach, remaining committed to their individual denominational identities.

Several factors contributed to this lukewarm response. The Korean church community was still recovering from significant human and material losses sustained during the 1919 March First Independence Movement. Additionally, memories of the unsuccessful 1905 church unity attempt created skepticism about cooperative ventures.

Despite these challenges, the movement gradually gained momentum. The initial effort transformed into the Korean Church Federal Council (조선예수교장감연합협의회) in 1918. Six years later, this organization merged with the Korean Missionary Association to establish the Korean National Christian Council (조선예수교연합공의회) in 1924.

Organizational Structure and Leadership

Rev. Chae Jae-myeong served as the council's first chairman. The founding charter articulated three primary goals: advancing gospel evangelization, promoting social ethical standards, and spreading Christian cultural values through cooperative efforts.

This organization represented the foundational predecessor to what would later become the National Council of Churches in Korea (NCCK). The council's membership included three Korean denominations—Presbyterian, Methodist, and Southern Presbyterian churches—alongside six foreign missionary organizations. Two Christian institutions, the British and Foreign Bible Society and the Korean Christian Youth Association, also participated. A significant shift in organizational dynamics occurred as Korean representatives (40 members) outnumbered foreign missionaries (20 members), suggesting an emerging Korean leadership structure. This demographic change prompted missionaries to maintain their separate Korean Missionary Association rather than dissolving it as originally planned.

Activities and International Connections

The council assumed responsibility for various missionary territories and programs previously managed by the Korean Missionary Association, including work in Japan and China. Its domestic activities encompassed evangelist education, rural development initiatives, temperance campaigns, and religious broadcasting.

The organization served as Korea's connection to international Christian movements. Following the 1921 establishment of the International Missionary Council (IMC), the Korean council helped introduce global church developments to local audiences. In 1925, they coordinated Dr. John R. Mott's visit to Korea during his tenure as IMC president.

The council's international engagement culminated in 1928 when four Korean delegates—Shin Hyung-woo, Yang Ju-sam, Jung In-gwa, and Kim Hwal-ran—attended the International Missionary Council meeting in Jerusalem. This participation marked a pivotal moment in developing Korean Christian social awareness and motivated the church to pursue what became known as "faith socialization and realization," focusing efforts on reforming Korean society and rural communities.

  1. YMCA

Student-Led Reform Initiatives

The 1920s witnessed the Korean YMCA student movement evolving beyond its traditional religious boundaries to become a primary force for social enlightenment. This transformation was catalyzed by the heightened national awareness that emerged from the 1919 March First Movement. The organization's core mission centered on fostering comprehensive development among youth—intellectually, morally, and physically—while serving as a unifying platform for students navigating life under Japanese colonial rule.

This period saw efforts to create the Korean Student Congress, designed to overcome the fragmentation that characterized earlier student organizations like alumni groups and social societies. The congress represented an attempt to establish a unified voice for the entire student community through diverse programmatic activities. However, Japanese colonial authorities prohibited this consolidation effort.

A pivotal moment occurred in 1922 when the 11th World Student Christian Federation Conference convened in Beijing, coinciding with rising anti-Christian sentiments. Student participants committed to integrating religious faith with scientific knowledge as tools for personal transformation and societal reform. Their vision encompassed eliminating social problems like poverty and establishing genuine peace founded on justice and human equality.

During this period, the YMCA promoted economic nationalism through the "Buy-Korean" movement while simultaneously engaging with socialist ideologies. The organization embraced a worldview that measured civilizational progress not through material wealth but through humanitarian principles and justice, advocating for global transformation based on these values.

The relationship between Christianity and socialism became a significant discussion point within YMCA student circles, extending their global reform ideology. At the 10th Student Congress in August 1924, held in Gaecheon, Chaehadong, participants adopted resolutions committing the Student YMCA to temperance advocacy, anti-prostitution campaigns, frugality promotion, and agricultural development support. These commitments aligned with broader contemporary movements for industrial progress and social temperance.

However, by October 1925, intensified anti-Christian activities by socialist groups prompted some Student YMCA members to reconsider their approach, shifting toward strengthening Christian identity rather than pursuing Christian-socialist partnerships.

Rural Development and Education Programs

The Student YMCA's rural initiatives underwent significant expansion after 1929, broadening from basic educational services to comprehensive community development including cooperative organization, agricultural enhancement, and alternative livelihood promotion. These programs operated through two distinct timeframes: vacation periods and regular academic terms.

Vacation-period activities encompassed deploying educational teams to combat illiteracy, conducting training workshops, establishing children's religious education programs, and operating evening schools for rural communities. These initiatives targeted multiple objectives: literacy improvement, cooperative leadership development, hygiene education, and general living standard enhancement.

A representative example occurred when Yeonhee College Student YMCA dispatched a missionary team beginning December 27, 1928, for week-long engagements in locations including Gangneung, Geumcheon, Yonggang, and Ui-ju. Their program concentrated on three primary areas: religious instruction, economic nationalism promotion, and rural community development, demonstrating the comprehensive nature of their outreach.

Regular vacation-based missionary deployments to various regions became standard practice for rural development work. As major newspapers launched their own educational campaigns—Chosun Ilbo's literacy initiative (1929-1934) and Dong-a Ilbo's broadcasting program (1931-1934)—student participation in vacation-period rural enlightenment activities became increasingly common in Korean academic life.

The momentum of these collaborative efforts faced significant disruption following the September 1931 Manchurian Incident. The experience gained through joint YM-YWCA activities provided crucial organizational capacity and leadership development that contributed to this significant resistance effort against colonial rule.

The integration of female student participation in previously male-dominated religious organizations represented a progressive step toward gender equality within Korean Christian student movements, establishing precedents for future collaborative social action initiatives.

  1. New Korea Young Men’s Association 신한청년단 新韓靑年黨

Historical Context and Formation

The New Korea Young Men Association emerged in Shanghai during a period of significant Korean diaspora movement to China. Following 1910, Korean migration to China intensified due to multiple factors including political persecution, economic hardship, and religious oppression. This included fallout from the incident involving 105 officials connected to the Chosun Governor-General's Office.

Statistical records from 1921 indicate that Korean immigrant populations in Manchuria had reached approximately 49,771 individuals. Shanghai's unique position as Asia's premier international hub made it an ideal location for Korean political organization. The city's concentration of Western diplomatic and commercial interests provided Korean activists with access to international political concepts, particularly President Woodrow Wilson's self-determination doctrine.

The restrictive Japanese policies that prohibited Korean students from pursuing education in America further drove many to seek opportunities in China. This convergence of factors created fertile ground for organized political activity among Korean expatriates.

Core Activities and Diplomatic Efforts

The association's primary mission centered on international diplomatic engagement. Their strategy involved two main components: direct appeals to American leadership and participation in global diplomatic forums. The organization prepared formal documentation requesting Korean independence and attempted direct communication with President Wilson. Kim Kyu-sik served as the association's representative to the Paris Peace Conference, marking a significant attempt to bring Korean independence concerns to the international stage. This diplomatic approach represented a strategic shift toward leveraging global political movements for Korean liberation.

The Independence Appeal Document

The organization's most significant written work was an independence petition, largely authored by Yun Chi-ho. This document strategically framed Korean independence within the context of Wilson's self-determination principles and the emerging League of Nations framework. The petition presented a comparative analysis between Japanese and Korean political cultures. It characterized Japan's governance philosophy as militaristic and hierarchical, emphasizing emperor worship and absolute obedience. The document argued that these qualities made Japan unsuitable for League membership and positioned it as a potential threat to both regional and global stability. Conversely, the petition portrayed Korea as inheriting the legacy of ancient kingdoms—Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla—and embodying the philosophical principle of Hongik Ingan (universal human welfare). The document detailed Japanese oppression across multiple dimensions: spiritual suppression through religious control, educational restrictions, economic exploitation, and political repression through police-state tactics.

Christian Influence

The association's Christian character significantly influenced its political philosophy and organizational structure. Korean Christian communities in Shanghai, supported by international missionary networks, provided both ideological framework and practical support for independence activities.

This religious foundation manifested in the petition's moral arguments and its appeal to universal principles of justice and human dignity. The Christian perspective shaped how the organization framed Korean suffering and justified independence claims to Western audiences.

Shift in Independence Movement Goals

The association represented a fundamental transformation in Korean independence movement objectives. Earlier movements had focused on restoring traditional monarchy under Emperor Gojong and reestablishing the Joseon system. The Shanghai-based organization instead advocated for democratic governance modeled on American principles.

This ideological evolution reflected broader changes in Korean political thought, moving from restoration of traditional authority to embrace of modern democratic concepts. The shift also represented a strategic adaptation to contemporary international political discourse.

  1. Enlightening Movement of Rural Areas

The early decades of Japanese colonial rule in Korea witnessed a systematic transformation of the peninsula's agricultural landscape. Beginning with comprehensive land surveys initiated in 1910, Japanese authorities implemented policies designed to maximize Korea's utility as a food supplier for the expanding empire. The "Campaign to increase rice production" launched in the 1920s exemplified this strategy, fundamentally altering rural Korean society through the deliberate creation of a polarized class structure between landlords and tenant farmers while suppressing the emergence of a rural middle class.

Statistical evidence reveals the dramatic impact of these policies on Korean food security. While rice production grew substantially from 12.7 million suk in 1920 to 17.3 million suk by 1928, the proportion exported to Japan increased even more dramatically, rising from 1.85 million suk to 7.42 million suk during the same period. This extraction forced Koreans to substitute their traditional rice diet with imported millet from Manchuria, creating widespread hardship that was exacerbated by global economic instability.

In response to these challenging conditions, various Christian organizations emerged as catalysts for rural reform movements. The Young Men's Christian Association took pioneering steps by organizing nationwide gospel teams in 1920, focusing on inspiring rural youth and fostering community solidarity while conducting systematic studies of rural conditions. By 1925, the organization had restructured itself to better address rural needs, establishing specialized departments and engaging six city branches in coordinated rural initiatives. Educational institutions including Sungsil School, Youngmyung School in Gongju, Yeongseng School in Hamhung, and Gwangsung High School in Kaeseong became active participants in these efforts.

The international dimension of these movements became evident through collaborative research projects. Professor Brunner from Columbia University worked alongside Korean scholar Ha Gyeong-deok in 1926 to produce "Rural Korea," a comprehensive study intended to document Korean rural movement achievements for global audiences and emphasize the critical need for such initiatives in non-missionary territories. Brunner's findings were notably critical of existing church approaches, citing failures to adapt to changing times, inadequate scientific education, suppression of youth voices, internal organizational problems, and disconnect from intellectual concerns.

These criticisms prompted practical responses, including the 1928 deployment of Canadian missionary Barnes, who brought expertise in livestock, horticulture, and poultry management, along with Clark, an experienced agricultural administrator. Working with Korean colleagues, they promoted rural education, cooperative associations, and leadership development programs. However, the 1930s and 1940s brought significant challenges including economic depression, pressure to merge Korean organizations with Japanese counterparts, and the imprisonment or death of key leaders such as Yun Chi-ho and Lee Sang-jae.

Women's participation in rural reform gained momentum through the Young Women's Christian Association, which formally entered rural work in 1928 following the International Missionary Council meeting. This conference marked a crucial shift in Korean Christian social consciousness, redirecting attention from urban to rural concerns in recognition that most Koreans and churchgoers were farmers. The organization established a Rural Department that same year, launching programs to train rural women leaders and promote women's education as essential components of community development.

The Rural Women Leaders Training Institute aimed to enhance the social position of rural housewives, foster recognition of their spiritual value, and encourage rational living practices that would benefit entire communities. Graduates were expected to return to their villages as change agents, working collaboratively with other women to improve rural life. Complementing this effort, the Rural Women's Education Center utilized local newspapers to provide instruction focused on emotional development, industrial cooperatives, and home-based income generation strategies designed to strengthen rural economies.

Choi Yong-sin exemplified the philosophy underlying these women's programs, advocating that literacy elimination would enable rural women to advance their education through intensive instruction, ultimately elevating their status and contributing to national development. Under her influence, specialized agricultural missionaries including Canadian Burns were brought to Korea to work with local assistants in promoting rural enlightenment, cooperative movements, and leadership training.

Presbyterian involvement in rural reform crystallized following the 1928 Jerusalem International Missionary Council, which sparked clear social consciousness within the church. The establishment of a Rural Department, Rural Association, and children's education initiatives marked the beginning of systematic Presbyterian rural engagement. A 1929 conference responding to Motte's visit addressed church life, leadership training, financial support, spiritual unity, and business cooperation, generating recommendations for practical gospel preaching, ethical commerce, Old Testament implementation, savings promotion, diligence encouragement, cooperative associations, and the assignment of agricultural specialist missionaries to each district.

The Methodist approach to rural ministry was distinguished by its extensive use of unpaid lay preachers, a strategy that addressed the economic challenges facing rural churches. Statistics from 1937 revealed that among 112 Methodist preachers, only 26 held annual conference membership, while 60 served without compensation. This volunteer group included 34 farmers, 11 shopkeepers, 6 unemployed individuals, 4 teachers, and various other occupations. A Gongju region survey found that 35 of 45 worship services were led by these unpaid ministers, providing what Methodist leader Peyton viewed as a solution to rural church financial difficulties.

Methodist churches also pioneered the "Church Farm Practical Use" concept, beginning with Noble's 1927 land purchase to secure church finances. By 1933, nearly one-third of the 52 Methodist churches owned land, with holdings ranging from 200 to 20,000 jeongbo and averaging about 2 acres per church. Three-quarters of this land was leased to tenant farmers, generating rental income for church budgets, while the remainder was cultivated by church members. Although the average 1937 income of 74 won represented only one-fifth of a pastor's annual salary, these farms contributed meaningfully to rural church economic development through cash donations, labor contributions, cooperative spirit, and agricultural innovation and experimentation.

  1. Literary Mission Movement

Following the March 1st Independence Movement, Japanese colonial authorities implemented a "cultural politics" approach that granted modest press freedoms as part of their governing strategy. This policy shift enabled the establishment of influential newspapers during the 1920s, including Dong-A Ilbo and Chosun Ilbo, both of which remain prominent Korean media outlets today. Within Christian circles, this relaxed atmosphere stimulated documentary missionary work through various publications, including newspapers and periodicals. The collaborative "Christian Shinbo," established in 1915 through Presbyterian and Methodist cooperation, secured colonial government authorization to cover general news topics spanning politics, diplomacy, and social affairs, functioning essentially as a mainstream newspaper.

Prior to the March 1st Movement, missionary publications were already emerging, including "The Korea Mission Field" (1905), an English-language magazine, alongside denominational publications such as the Methodist Hyupsung Theological Seminary's "Shinhangsegye" (Theological World) from 1916 and the Presbyterian Pyongyang Theological Seminary's "Sinhangjinam" (Theological South) from 1918. The subsequent decade witnessed further expansion with the Holiness Church launching "Hwalseon" (1922) and the Salvation Army establishing "Sagwan" (1927). Christian organizations contributed additional publications, including the Tokyo Chosen YMCA's "Hyundai" (1920) and "Jeolmeuni" (1923), the Seoul YMCA's "Cheongnyeon" (1921), the Korea Sunday School Union's "Jooilhakgyojapji" (Sunday School Magazine) from 1925, and the Christian Endeavor of the Korea United Association's "Jinsaeng" (1925). Personal and literary circle publications also flourished during this period.

Publication statistics reveal the extent of this literary expansion: the 1910s produced 21 magazines, the 1920s generated 43, the 1930s saw 49, while the 1940s yielded only 6, creating a total of 119 magazines according to research by Han (1987), Yoon (2003), and The Institute of the History of Christianity in Korea (2019). These figures demonstrate how colonial cultural politics policies stimulated Korean church engagement in documentary missionary activities.

Prominent Korean church leaders embraced this publishing opportunity with enthusiasm. Notable figures including Park Seung-bong, Yoo Sung-jun, Yoon Chi-ho, Lee Sang-jae, and Choi Byung-heon from the YMCA, alongside March 1st Movement leaders Kim Baek-won, Kim Se-hwan, Kim Chang-jun, Lee Seung-hoon, and Jung No-sik, actively participated in creating specialized Christian publishing companies and promoting Christian literature production.

The transformation of the 'Chosun Christian Light Company' into 'Changmunsa' in 1922 marked a significant development in Christian publishing. This organization produced magazines such as 'Sin Saengmyeong' (New Life) and various books while advancing Korean Bible translation projects. A notable achievement occurred in 1925 when J.S. This era witnessed intensive Bible and hymnal revision activities. Scholarly resources included the 'Gwanju (貫珠, Reference) Seonhanmun Old Testament' (1926) and 'New Testament Commentary' for biblical researchers. The Korean National Christian Council prioritized hymnal revision as its inaugural project in 1924, beginning comprehensive updates to the jointly published Presbyterian and Methodist hymnal from 1908.

  1. Sunday School Movement

The March 1st Movement generated considerable momentum that extended beyond political activism into educational reform, particularly affecting children's religious instruction. William N. Blair, a missionary, championed the expansion of Sunday school programs during this period. Within a single year following 1919, Sunday school establishments grew dramatically from approximately 10,000 to more than 14,000 institutions by 1920. The international community recognized Korea's educational potential when the 1920 World Sunday School Convention in Japan committed to dispatching global Sunday school experts to Korea for three consecutive years, with each specialist spending six months annually providing guidance and oversight to Korean Sunday school operations.

Three pivotal developments coincided with the November 1922 formation of the Korean Sunday School Union, significantly advancing Sunday school activities throughout the peninsula. The Presbyterian Church launched its Forward Movement while the Methodist Church initiated its Century Movement, both programs emerging from denominational assemblies in September 1919 and substantially expanding Sunday school participation following the March 1st Independence Movement.

The Presbyterian General Assembly's 1919 decision established a three-year Forward Movement timeline, appointing thirty-six representatives drawn equally from each presbytery to coordinate promotional efforts. This initiative aimed for organizational consolidation in 1919 with substantial achievements targeted for 1921. While the movement encompassed broader church activities beyond Sunday schools, its primary objective focused on rapidly increasing both Sunday worship attendance and Sunday school enrollment. The explosive expansion of Sunday school programs after the March 1st Movement directly correlated with these Forward Movement and Century Movement initiatives.

Presbyterian-centered Forward Movement operations occurred across two distinct phases spanning 1919 to 1926 and 1929 to 1935. Youth-oriented Sunday schools within churches represented the movement's most significant accomplishment. Over three years beginning in 1920, the Forward Movement established essential groundwork for addressing Korea's 1919 crisis. Korean Presbyterian Sunday schools experienced substantial growth in both student enrollment and teaching staff during this transformative period.

Post-March 1st Movement Sunday school expansion reached remarkable proportions, achieving 3,899 Sunday schools serving 223,732 students by 1921. Sunday school advocates maintained strong convictions that educational programs could facilitate popular reform while expanding church membership to build a superior nation. This historical context positioned Sunday schools as optimal vehicles for instilling Christian principles among Korean youth.

The extraordinary Sunday school growth provided mechanisms for attracting young people who might otherwise gravitate toward socialist or communist ideologies or experience moral deterioration. Churches offered these individuals meaningful roles and responsibilities, sparking renewed revival movements. Thousands of youth educated through Sunday school programs during this era subsequently developed into distinguished Korean church leadership.

Despite increasingly difficult conditions for gaining new converts, prevailing wisdom held that "flourishing Sunday schools naturally lead to thriving churches." Yang Joo-sam reported at the Fourth National Sunday School Convention that Sunday schools recruited eighty-five percent of new church members. Sunday school activation specifically addressed contemporary demands placed upon Korean churches for effective evangelism and appropriate education for future generations.

The 8th Tokyo World Sunday School Convention held in Japan from October 5-14, 1920, exerted the "most direct influence" on Korean church Sunday school movements. Originally planned for 1916 but delayed due to World War I, this convention occurred in Tokyo just one year after the March 1st Movement. More than 300 international representatives attending the 8th World Sunday School Convention subsequently visited Korea to observe and support Sunday school activities. These delegates traveled to regions with active Sunday school programs including Seoul, Pyongyang, Kaesong, and Daegu, conducting educational lectures to promote Korean Sunday school development. This supportive atmosphere enabled organization of the 1st National Sunday School Convention in 1921, attracting 960 representatives from throughout Korea, including both northern and southern regions.

On November 1, 1922, twenty-seven representatives from ten organizations convened at the Episcopal Church meeting facility to establish the Korean Sunday School Union. The Sunday School Committee, operational since 1911, contributed significantly to the Korean Sunday School Union's formation, while the 1920 8th World Sunday School Convention and 1921 1st National Sunday School Convention provided crucial foundational events.

Among the twenty-seven founding assembly participants, Presbyterian and Northern Methodist Churches each contributed eight representatives, while the Northern Presbyterian Church Mission and Southern Methodist Church Mission each provided two delegates. The Northern Methodist Church Mission, Southern Presbyterian Church Mission, Canadian Mission, Australian Presbyterian Mission, Sunday School Union, and Korean Bible Society each sent one representative. Leadership positions included Oh Cheon-gyeong (Presbyterian) as president, Hong Byeong-seon (Methodist) as vice president, Byeon Seong-ok (Methodist) as secretary, and Bang Wi-ryang (Presbyterian) as treasurer.

In September 1930, the Korean Sunday School Union joined the World Sunday School Association, strengthening international connections. The organization sent Kim Gwan-sik, Lee Seong-hwi, and Kwak An-ryeon to the 1928 World Sunday School Convention in Los Angeles, further solidifying relationships between Korean and global Sunday school organizations.

Lacy offered enthusiastic praise for the Korean Sunday School Union in January 1929, describing it as "a growing organization" with expanding staff and increasing budgets that had grown from an estimated 9,000 won to over 12,000 won annually. He identified three primary accomplishments of the Korean Sunday School Union before Liberation: publishing Sunday school educational materials, sending representatives to World Sunday School Conventions for continuous international contact, and hosting National Sunday School Conventions within Korea.

  1. Temperance Movement

During the 1920s, Korean Christianity became a driving force behind two interconnected social reform initiatives: rural enlightenment efforts and temperance advocacy. These movements emerged as responses to both colonial exploitation and traditional social problems, representing broader attempts at cultural transformation through moral and spiritual renewal.

The temperance campaign encompassed far more than simple alcohol prohibition, evolving into a comprehensive moral reform agenda that addressed multiple social vices. Organizations promoted the Gold-Ju Movement, which advocated complete abstention from alcohol, while simultaneously campaigning against gambling, opium consumption, and excessive indulgence in material goods. This holistic approach to social betterment aligned with contemporary cultural movements emphasizing personal cultivation, moral improvement, and rural development, all of which formed part of a larger "Cultural Movement" that sought to modernize Korean society through spiritual and ethical transformation.

Japanese colonial policies created conditions that made temperance advocacy particularly urgent and relevant. The colonial administration's economic exploitation through land surveys and agricultural production increases was accompanied by the deliberate introduction of addictive substances including alcohol, tobacco, and opium as means of social control and revenue generation. The establishment of state-regulated prostitution under the "Gongchang system" further encouraged moral degradation, while liquor and tobacco taxes became significant sources of colonial revenue. These circumstances provided both the motivation and moral justification for Christian temperance initiatives.

The theological and cultural foundations of the temperance movement reflected multiple influences that shaped early Korean Christianity. Conservative Presbyterian missionaries from America brought strong prohibitionist convictions that emphasized abstinence from alcohol and tobacco as essential elements of Christian living. Christine Isabel Tinling and other representatives of the World Women's Christian Temperance Union played particularly influential roles in introducing and promoting these ideas among Korean converts. Their Puritan-influenced ethics resonated with Korean Christians seeking alternatives to both traditional practices and colonial-imposed vices.

The movement's development was also significantly influenced by broader intellectual and political tensions of the era. Socialist critics of the 1920s attacked Christianity as an imperialist instrument that promoted passive acceptance of unjust conditions while rejecting scientific reasoning in favor of superstitious beliefs. This anti-Christian campaign prompted defensive responses within Korean Christian communities, with conservative elements emerging to strongly oppose socialist ideology. Simultaneously, progressive Christian nationalists and younger church members pushed for internal reforms that would make Christianity more relevant to Korean social and political aspirations.

Women's participation proved crucial to the success of temperance advocacy, as female activists found in these movements opportunities to challenge traditional gender restrictions while promoting social reform. The establishment of organizations like the Chosun YWCA provided platforms for women's education and activism, enabling their participation in broader enlightenment efforts that addressed both gender inequality and social problems. These women's organizations became essential components of the temperance movement's organizational structure and public outreach efforts.

The practical implementation of temperance advocacy involved diverse strategies designed to reach broad segments of Korean society. Organizations coordinated their efforts across denominational lines to conduct nationwide campaigns that utilized public gatherings, holidays, and market days as opportunities for education and persuasion. Christian newspapers and magazines served as important vehicles for promoting temperance messages and documenting movement activities. Special attention was given to enforcing laws against alcohol and tobacco use among minors, reflecting the movement's concern for protecting younger generations from colonial-imposed vices.

The strategic timing and methods of temperance campaigning demonstrated sophisticated understanding of Korean social patterns and communication networks. Activists recognized that traditional gathering occasions provided optimal opportunities for reaching large audiences with their messages, while the use of existing Christian media infrastructure enabled sustained advocacy across geographic boundaries. The non-denominational cooperation that characterized these efforts reflected both the urgency of the social problems being addressed and the movement's success in transcending sectarian divisions in pursuit of common goals.

These temperance and rural enlightenment movements represented Korean Christians' attempts to navigate the complex challenges of colonial rule while maintaining cultural integrity and promoting social progress. By focusing on moral reform and community development, Christian activists carved out space for legitimate social action that could address pressing problems without directly challenging colonial political authority. The movements' emphasis on personal transformation and community improvement provided frameworks for resistance that were both spiritually meaningful and practically sustainable under colonial conditions.

The establishment of state-sanctioned prostitution in Korea emerged as a direct consequence of Japanese colonial expansion and settlement patterns following the peninsula's forced opening to international trade in 1876. Military personnel and civilian settlers brought with them organized commercial sex industries that gradually evolved into officially regulated systems. Busan witnessed the first appearance of government-licensed prostitution in 1902, marking the institutionalization of what would become known as the gongchang system, wherein prostitution operated under formal state supervision and control.

Statistical evidence reveals the dramatic expansion of this regulated sex trade throughout the colonial period. From an initial count of 6,018 licensed sex workers in 1916, the numbers more than doubled to reach 15,623 by 1942, reflecting both the system's entrenchment and its growing scale. The demographic composition of these establishments underwent significant transformation over time, with Japanese women initially predominating but eventually being replaced by Korean women as economic exploitation intensified colonial subjects' vulnerability to sexual commodification.

The systematic nature of this exploitation became evident through the conditions experienced by women trapped within the licensed system. Workers frequently discovered that promised wages never materialized, while physical abuse became routine aspects of their daily experience. Mounting debts created cycles of bondage that prevented escape, and widespread sexually transmitted diseases posed constant health threats. These circumstances transformed what colonial authorities presented as regulated commerce into mechanisms of systematic oppression that particularly targeted economically vulnerable Korean women.

International developments provided crucial context and legitimacy for Korean opposition to state-regulated prostitution. The 1921 international treaty prohibiting trafficking in women and children, signed by twenty nations, established global precedents for combating sexual exploitation. Japan's participation in this agreement on September 30, 1921, though qualified by two reservations, created legal and moral foundations that Korean activists could invoke in their campaigns against the gongchang system.

Korean missionary leadership recognized the prostitution issue as integral to broader religious and social reform objectives. At the Joint Conference of Korean Missionaries convened on September 25, 1919, participants identified the abolition of licensed prostitution as a priority demand alongside other religious concerns. This early recognition reflected growing awareness that sexual exploitation represented both a moral challenge and a symptom of colonial oppression that required coordinated Christian response.

Organized resistance to the licensed prostitution system gained momentum during the mid-1920s as Korean Christians established dedicated advocacy organizations. The Society for the Abolition of Licensed Prostitution emerged as a primary vehicle for coordinated action, while the Korean Women's Christian Temperance Union provided focused attention to gender-specific aspects of the issue. These organizations transcended denominational boundaries, reflecting recognition that sexual exploitation required unified Christian opposition regardless of sectarian differences.

The practical strategies employed by abolition advocates demonstrated sophisticated understanding of both public education and political advocacy. Large-scale lecture campaigns brought awareness to broad audiences, while printed materials including pamphlets and educational literature provided sustained messaging about the moral and social problems created by regulated prostitution. The establishment of protective agencies offered concrete alternatives for women seeking to escape the system, providing both immediate assistance and long-term support for rehabilitation.

Political action complemented educational and service efforts as organizations pursued direct engagement with colonial authorities. The submission of formal petitions demanding abolition of licensed prostitution represented bold challenges to established policy, particularly when undertaken jointly with international allies including the Foreign Missionaries' Association and the Japanese Christian Youth Association. These collaborative efforts demonstrated the movement's ability to build coalitions that crossed national and organizational boundaries in pursuit of shared moral objectives.

Individual denominations contributed distinct approaches to the broader campaign while maintaining cooperation with interdenominational efforts. Presbyterian and Methodist churches developed specialized programs and strategies suited to their particular organizational structures and theological emphases, while groups like the Christian Endeavor provided additional platforms for youth engagement and leadership development. This diversity of approaches within overall unity of purpose reflected the movement's strength in addressing different aspects of the prostitution system while maintaining coordinated opposition to its continuation.

The campaign against state-regulated prostitution thus represented one dimension of broader Christian social reform efforts in colonial Korea, connecting concerns about sexual exploitation with temperance advocacy, women's rights, and moral reform. By challenging the gongchang system, Korean Christians asserted alternative visions of social organization that rejected both traditional patriarchal oppression and colonial-imposed degradation, offering instead frameworks based on human dignity and spiritual transformation.

Key Theological Issues

  1. Kim, Ik-doo’s Divine Healing Revival Movement

At the dawn of the twentieth century, Kim Ik-doo's spiritual journey began when Park Tae-hwan, a close friend, introduced him to a religious awakening conducted by Pastor W. L. Swallen in 1900. This encounter marked a pivotal transformation in Kim's life as he embraced Christianity and underwent a profound spiritual experience with the Holy Spirit following three days of devoted fasting and prayer.

Kim's ministerial path commenced with his involvement at Jangryeong Church and Sincheon Church during his formative years. His formal theological training began at Pyongyang Theological Seminary in 1906, leading to his ordination as a pastor four years later in 1910. His pastoral assignments took him to multiple congregations over the subsequent decades. Between 1922 and 1927, he led Seoul Nammoon Church, followed by a five-year tenure at Sincheon Church from 1928 to 1933. Beginning in 1934, he established his ministry at Seoul Seungdong Church, where he continued his religious work.

Throughout his clerical career, Kim developed a reputation for divine healing through prayer, with his earliest recorded miraculous intervention occurring between 1901 and 1903. During this period at Jangryeong Church, he fervently prayed for the critically ill child of a congregation member, reportedly resulting in the child's recovery.

Despite these early successes, Kim experienced a crisis of confidence that temporarily halted his healing ministry. When his prayers failed to restore a paralyzed individual at Sincheon Church, discouragement led him to abandon this aspect of his work. His renewed commitment to healing ministry emerged in October 1919 during a theological discussion with Pastor Jeong Seok-jong of Yeolbae Church in Gangdong County, Pyeongannam-do, focusing on Mark 16:17-18. This conversation rekindled his conviction in divine healing.

The restoration of his healing ministry bore immediate fruit upon his return to Sincheon Church. Within a month, he successfully prayed for Hah Kyung-soon, a seriously afflicted believer, whose recovery marked the beginning of Kim's prominent healing revival movement. By 1920, his revival gatherings had become massive public events, often drawing thousands of participants. The scale of these meetings was so substantial that temporary marketplaces were established in locations like Suncheon, Hwanghae Province, to accommodate the crowds. Seoul venues attracted as many as 5,000 attendees, witnessing dramatic manifestations of spiritual power and numerous reported healings.

The May 1920 Busan revival meeting exemplified these extraordinary events, featuring the alleged restoration of speech to a mute person and mobility to a paralyzed individual. Kim's approach to healing prayer evolved through three distinct phases. Initially, he prayed individually for each afflicted person. As numbers grew overwhelming, he organized people by their specific ailments for group prayer sessions. The final stage, beginning with the 1920 Seoul revival, involved healing through the faith of the sick individuals themselves, who reportedly recovered merely by observing Pastor Kim.

Kim's healing ministry sparked intense debate and opposition throughout Korean society. Intellectuals, communist activists, and liberal theologians condemned his practices as deviations from authentic Christian doctrine, characterizing them as superstitious beliefs. These critics expressed deep skepticism about the reported healings and occasionally resorted to physical disruption during revival gatherings.

Communist activists proved particularly hostile, organizing events such as the "Kim Ik-doo Charlatan Expose Rally," where they branded him a "high-level shaman" and condemned his alleged promotion of superstition. The opposition reached its zenith in 1926 during the Yongjeong revival meeting, when communist activists encircled the church building, interrupted Kim's preaching, and physically attacked church leaders including A. R. Ross. The violence extended to property damage and injuries to multiple individuals. Similar confrontations occurred in May of that year at Iri Church, where local activists in Jeollabuk-do organized counter-demonstrations against the religious gathering.

Notwithstanding the persistent criticism and violent opposition, Kim and his supporters interpreted the healing phenomena as tangible evidence of divine intervention in contemporary life. Moreover, his healing ministry served a crucial role in reinvigorating the Korean Christian community, which had endured severe hardships under Japanese colonial rule and exploitation, helping to restore its growth and vitality during a challenging historical period.

Anti-Christian Organizations

  1. Cheondogyo

During the 1920s, Cheondoism emerged as Korea's predominant religious force, commanding an impressive following of roughly 2 million adherents. This substantial membership base dwarfed other faiths, including Christianity, which maintained approximately 350,000 followers at the time. Given Korea's total population of about 18 million, Cheondoism's reach encompassed more than one-tenth of the entire nation, establishing it as the most influential religious organization by sheer numbers.

The sociopolitical climate of colonial Korea underwent dramatic transformation as 1924 approached. Socialist movements gained considerable momentum, culminating in the formation of the Chosun Youth Alliance in April of that year. This organization represented the first comprehensive nationwide coalition of socialist groups, uniting various youth movements from Seoul with emerging organizations and the existing Chosun Youth Federation. The Alliance's establishment marked a turning point, as socialist principles began permeating Korean society both organizationally and culturally.

This evolving ideological landscape inevitably impacted Cheondoist communities. Progressive publications increasingly featured articles about socialist movements and theories, while individuals within Cheondoist circles who harbored socialist sympathies found their convictions strengthening. By 1924, these influences became apparent in the pronouncements and writings of Cheondoist leaders. A notable example occurred in March 1924, when Yi Don-hwa contributed to the Kaebek E, referencing Kropotkin's theories to argue that societal misconduct stemmed from systemic flaws rather than individual failings. He advocated for comprehensive social reform as the solution to humanity's collective problems.

This ideological shift also manifested in how Cheondoists approached religious matters. Rather than broadly condemning all "established religions," they developed more targeted critiques focusing on faiths they perceived as contributors to social inequities. Beginning in 1924, Christianity became a particular focus of Cheondoist criticism.

Christianity's position as the largest officially recognized religion made it a natural target for such scrutiny. The faith maintained strong connections with the Korean Government-General and possessed significant economic resources through well-funded institutions. These attributes positioned Christianity closely with Korea's socioeconomic power structure. Even Christian leaders like Sin Heung-woo recognized the faith's vulnerability to exploitation by capitalist interests, though they stopped short of condemning Christianity itself as inherently capitalistic.

Cheondoists identified Christianity's entanglement with social privilege as emblematic of broader systemic problems. However, their criticism remained carefully circumscribed, avoiding direct attacks on Christianity as a belief system since such condemnation might invite similar scrutiny of their own religious practices. Consequently, Cheondoist opposition to established religions operated primarily as an intellectual discourse within publications like the Kaebek E, rather than developing into a broader popular movement.

  1. The Korean Communist Party

The emergence of organized socialist resistance to Christianity in colonial Korea represented a significant shift from theoretical opposition to practical political action during the mid-1920s. While earlier socialist critiques of religion had remained largely abstract and philosophical, the period following 1925 witnessed a more focused and systematic campaign targeting Christian institutions specifically.

The transformation of anti-religious sentiment into targeted opposition occurred at the 1925 All-Korea People's Movement Activists Conference, where socialist organizations articulated explicit criticisms of Christianity as distinct from general religious opposition. Although Japanese police intervention prevented the conference from proceeding as planned, confiscated materials revealed comprehensive critiques that positioned Christianity within broader attacks on capitalist and imperialist structures. These documents demonstrated how socialist theorists had begun to identify Christianity not merely as a religious system but as an integral component of the economic and political order they sought to overthrow.

The ideological foundation for this opposition rested on the perception that Christian institutions had become closely aligned with capitalist interests. Socialist organizations, particularly those associated with the nascent Korean Communist Party, maintained that Christianity served as a tool for maintaining class divisions and perpetuating economic inequality. The Korean Communist Party's revolutionary ideology, which advocated for immediate proletarian revolution bypassing traditional nationalist phases, naturally positioned Christian organizations as obstacles to their vision of comprehensive social transformation centered on workers and peasants.

The strategic considerations surrounding the National Unification Front further complicated socialist attitudes toward Christianity. When the Korean Communist Party established its founding principles in 1925, leadership adopted strict criteria for potential alliance partners, explicitly rejecting organizations deemed "compromising" or "reformist" in their approach to social change. This framework led to interesting distinctions between different religious traditions. While Cheondoism was identified as a potentially revolutionary force suitable for inclusion in unified resistance efforts, Christianity was characterized as requiring exposure and opposition due to its perceived collaboration with existing power structures.

The Communist Youth League shared similar organizational perspectives, implementing policies that distinguished between religious movements based on their perceived revolutionary potential. These strategic calculations reflected broader tensions within Korean resistance movements regarding the balance between inclusive nationalist solidarity and ideological purity in anti-colonial organizing.

The New Youth League's institutional response to these ideological positions became evident in their formal planning processes. During their third regular meeting in 1925, the organization adopted a comprehensive action plan that included specific provisions addressing religious questions. The seventh item of this plan articulated detailed criticisms of Christianity as an institution fundamentally supportive of capitalist class interests, echoing themes that had emerged in other socialist forums. Implementation of this agenda was assigned to the organization's Intelligence Committee, indicating the serious attention given to anti-Christian activities.

By September 1925, the New Youth League had developed more concrete operational plans for implementing their anti-Christian agenda. The fourth regular meeting produced detailed strategies for producing and distributing anti-Christian literature while organizing various scales of public gatherings to promote their message. These plans represented a significant escalation from theoretical opposition to practical propaganda campaigns designed to influence public opinion and mobilize broader opposition to Christian institutions.

However, the implementation of these ambitious plans encountered significant obstacles due to intensive surveillance and repression by colonial authorities. Japanese administrators demonstrated particular sensitivity to socialist activities targeting religious organizations, viewing such campaigns as potential sources of social instability. Police intervention effectively prevented the distribution of anti-Christian materials based on the resolutions adopted during the New Youth League's third meeting, with authorities citing concerns about threats to public morality and social order.

Despite these restrictive measures, opportunities for anti-Christian organizing emerged around major Christian events. The announcement of the Second All-Korea Sunday School Conference in October 1925 provided socialist organizations with a specific target for mobilization efforts. The Han-yang Youth League, a coalition of youth organizations formed in August 1925 around the Seoul area, took primary responsibility for coordinating opposition activities related to this conference.

The Sunday School Conference itself represented a significant gathering of Christian communities from across Korea, attracting approximately 3,000 participants to venues in the Insadong and Jongno districts of Seoul. The conference featured diverse programming including educational sessions on psychology and teaching methods alongside traditional religious activities, demonstrating the institutional sophistication and broad appeal of Korean Christianity at this time.

The Han-yang Youth League's efforts to organize counter-programming during the conference faced direct police suppression. When authorities canceled the planned anti-Christian lecture meeting, the intervention paradoxically generated public sympathy for Christian organizations while simultaneously fueling socialist determination to continue their opposition efforts. This dynamic illustrated the complex ways in which colonial repression could inadvertently strengthen the very institutions it sought to protect.

The enthusiasm generated by this confrontation led the Han-yang Youth League to establish December 25th as "Anti-Christian Day," creating an institutional framework for ongoing opposition activities. Their plans included systematic propaganda campaigns involving literature production and public education efforts designed to expose what they viewed as Christianity's role in supporting oppressive social structures.

Yet these ambitious organizational efforts ultimately failed to achieve their intended impact. The broader crackdown following the Sinuiju Incident devastated socialist organizations throughout Korea, leading to the arrest of most Communist Youth League and Korean Communist Party leadership. This repression effectively dismantled the organizational infrastructure necessary for sustained anti-Christian campaigns, bringing these groups to the verge of complete collapse.

The final factor contributing to the decline of organized anti-Christian activities was the shift in Soviet Communist Party directives regarding coalition building. In 1925, Moscow issued new guidance emphasizing the formation of broader, more inclusive National Unification Front strategies. This populist approach conflicted with the exclusionary tactics that had characterized anti-Christian campaigns, effectively undermining the ideological foundation for continued opposition to Christian organizations.

By 1926, the combination of organizational destruction through police repression and strategic reorientation toward more inclusive resistance frameworks had effectively ended the organized anti-Christian movement. The brief but intense period of socialist opposition to Christianity in Korea thus represented a unique moment when ideological opposition, organizational capacity, and political opportunity converged to create systematic resistance to Christian institutions within the broader context of anti-colonial struggle.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

  1. Anti-Missionary Campaign

The relationship between Japanese colonial authorities and Christian missionaries in Korea underwent significant transformation during the early 1920s. This transformation was catalyzed by the 1919 independence demonstrations and evolved through a complex interplay of political pragmatism, religious accommodation, and growing cultural tensions.

The independence movement of March 1919 served as a watershed moment for Japanese colonial policy in Korea. Colonial administrators were compelled to reassess their approach to religious communities, which had demonstrated considerable organizational capacity and influence. Christianity emerged as a particular concern due to its extensive network of Korean adherents, connection to Western nations through missionary presence, and potential for mobilizing resistance activities.

The colonial administration faced a strategic dilemma. Direct confrontation with Christian organizations risked diplomatic complications with Western powers, particularly the United States, whose missionaries maintained significant presence in Korea. This constraint necessitated a more nuanced approach that sought to neutralize potential opposition through accommodation rather than suppression.

The foundation of Japanese religious control rested on comprehensive regulations implemented in August 1915. These measures established a bureaucratic framework requiring official authorization for all evangelical personnel, government approval for religious facilities, administrative oversight of missionary activities, and compliance with standardized operational procedures. This regulatory system effectively subordinated religious autonomy to state control, creating significant operational challenges for missionary organizations.

By 1919, accumulated frustrations among foreign missionaries reached a critical threshold. The All-Korea Missionaries Conference became the venue for organized resistance to colonial religious policies. Nearly every major missionary organization participated in formulating a comprehensive response to administrative restrictions. The conference produced a formal petition addressing streamlining approval processes, reducing bureaucratic requirements, expanding operational autonomy, protecting evangelical rights, and ensuring fair treatment of religious institutions.

The colonial government's response proved surprisingly accommodating. Administrative reforms implemented by 1920 substantially modified the restrictive framework that had governed missionary operations. Documentation requirements were simplified, penalty provisions were eliminated, and approval processes became more efficient. While complete religious freedom remained elusive, these modifications represented meaningful concessions that addressed many missionary concerns.

These policy adjustments facilitated important institutional developments for Christian organizations. The legal recognition of missionary societies as foundations became possible, beginning with the Methodist Missionary Society in December 1920. Foundation status provided crucial legal protections for property ownership and organizational operations that missionary groups had long sought. This development demonstrated the colonial administration's commitment to practical accommodation of foreign missionary interests.

The impact of these policy changes manifested in measurable Christian expansion. Statistical records indicate that Christian membership increased by approximately 27,000 individuals between 1919 and 1920, rising from under 300,000 to over 320,000 believers. This growth pattern continued throughout the early 1920s as missionary influence expanded under more favorable administrative conditions.

Foreign missionaries initially approached political matters with considerable caution, focusing their concerns on operational constraints rather than fundamental opposition to Japanese governance. As colonial policies became more accommodating, missionary cooperation with administrative authorities increased correspondingly. This pragmatic alignment reflected missionaries' primary interest in securing favorable conditions for their evangelical work rather than challenging the political order.

However, this evolving cooperation between missionaries and colonial authorities began to create tensions with Korean Christians who had previously regarded foreign missionaries as sympathetic allies. Korean disillusionment with missionary leadership became particularly evident in educational institutions operated by Christian organizations.

Student unrest at a Seoul missionary school in October 1922 exemplified these growing tensions. Over one hundred students organized protests against what they characterized as authoritarian administrative practices, oppressive institutional policies, and educational methods that failed to address contemporary Korean needs. The students maintained their boycott despite threats of academic consequences, demonstrating the depth of their dissatisfaction.

Institutional authorities responded with severe disciplinary measures, expelling thirty student leaders, dismissing supportive faculty members, and effectively closing the school by sending remaining students away. Contemporary observers recognized that these events reflected broader problems with missionary educational approaches. Samuel A. Moffett noted that Western educators who had worked in Korea for decades had provided educational models inappropriate for the changing times, directly implicating missionary leadership in the crisis.

The deterioration of missionary-Korean relations was further exacerbated by incidents involving racial prejudice and cultural insensitivity. The 1925 case involving C.A. Hasymer, a Seventh-day Adventist medical missionary, became particularly notorious. When a twelve-year-old Korean child from the mission station took apples from Hasymer's orchard, the missionary punished the boy by using acid to brand the word "thief" permanently on his face.

Japanese prosecutors eventually filed criminal charges against Hasymer for this incident, which prompted widespread condemnation from Korean Christian organizations. The Adventist Church dismissed Hasymer, who was subsequently deported. Japanese authorities and nationalist organizations exploited this incident to justify broader anti-missionary campaigns, demonstrating how individual misconduct could be leveraged for political purposes.

The escalating tensions between missionaries and Korean Christians prompted intervention from international Christian leadership. J.R. Mott, serving as YMCA General Secretary, arrived in Seoul in late December 1925 to mediate the growing conflict. The resulting "Council of Korean Christian Workers" brought together equal numbers of missionary and Korean representatives in an attempt to address underlying tensions and chart a path forward.

While this mediation effort temporarily reduced immediate tensions, it also highlighted fundamental issues regarding cultural adaptation and institutional control within Korean Christianity. A.W. Wasson summarized the proceedings by noting that missionaries were challenged to follow the apostle Paul's example of becoming "all things to all men" by adopting more Korean approaches to their work.

These conflicts significantly impeded the development of Korean Christianity throughout the 1920s and 1930s. The Japanese colonial administration skillfully exploited missionary-Korean tensions to justify increasingly restrictive policies toward Christian organizations. This strategy ultimately facilitated the eventual subordination of Korean Christianity to Japanese imperial religious structures, achieving through manipulation what direct suppression had failed to accomplish.
