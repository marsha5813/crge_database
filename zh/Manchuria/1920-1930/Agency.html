Manchuria: Agency (1920-1930)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

Bishop Auguste-Ernest-Désiré-Marie Gaspais, M.E.P.

Born in 1856 in Vannes, France, Auguste Gaspais joined the Société des Missions Etrangères in 1903 and was ordained a priest of the order in 1905. He arrived in Manchuria in 1908. From 1908-1920, he was placed in charge of a series of far-flung churches. In 1920, he was named Coadjutor Vicar Apostolic of Northern Manchuria, and in 1923 the sole Vicar Apostolic.

During the 1920s, Gaspais oversaw the significant expansion of the Catholic Church, in terms of converts, institutions, and territory. These successes were despite the declining state of internal security, which created significant practical difficulties for communication. In contrast, Gaspais seemed to maintain good relations with public authorities, a talent that would continue to serve him in the future.

As Japanese influence grew throughout the decade, Gaspais came to view Japan not so much as an aggressor as a guarantor of safety, preferable to the predations of bandits and especially important as a bulwark against the baleful influence of the Soviet Union. These early experiences shaped how Gaspais would deal with the formation of the Japanese client state of Manchoukuo during the subsequent years.

Bishop Jean-Marie Blois, MEP, 1881-1946

Born in Nantes in 1881 and ordained to the MEP in 1905. Arrived in Mukden 1905 and placed in charge of church construction and work as parish priest. Returned briefly to France in 1919 to take up position as director of Paris Seminary, but was subsequently brought back to China in 1921 as VA of Mukden.

Despite the atmosphere of political and military uncertainty, Blois was concerned during this decade with institution building. Achievements in this regard include the French-Chinese college, which was founded in 1923 and subsequently handed over to the Marists, and a smaller school for catechists that opened in 1924. He also sought to expand the mission beyond the MEP, inviting Catholic missionaries from Quebec and the United States to take up territories that were later detached from Mukden.

Blois also oversaw the internationalization of the Catholic mission, using land granted from Japanese Christians in Fushun and Dalian/Dairen to build churches for the foreign (Japanese, Korean and European) Catholic communities. Although new church construction was prohibited after 1927, Dalian was able to rebuild the Francis-Xavier parish church into a such a community, served by a Japanese-speaking missionary priest. (I am not sure about Fushun)

Korean Christian Leaders

Choi, Naomi 최나오미

Choi Naomi became the first Korean woman missionary officially commissioned by the Southern Methodist Church of Korea to serve in Siberia in 1922. During an era when Korean women rarely had opportunities to function as independent religious leaders, she crossed international boundaries as a fully authorized missionary. Her mission field presented formidable challenges including extreme cold, political instability, and economic hardship, yet she developed an indigenous model of women's ministry centered on organizing sustainable women's missionary societies.

Throughout the mid-1920s, Choi established women's missionary societies among local women in Siberia. These organizations functioned as more than devotional groups; they operated as fully autonomous, self-governing bodies. Members contributed dues, engaged in mutual aid programs, and undertook educational and charitable initiatives. By 1929, merely seven years after her arrival, thirty-six women's missionary societies had been established across Siberia and Manchuria, encompassing a total membership of over 1,060 individuals1.

Choi additionally trained female leaders capable of continuing the work across various regions, facilitating both spiritual and social transformation. Her mission extended beyond Christian outreach to help women reclaim their names and identities. Her work embodied a theological commitment to ensuring that women functioned not merely as "named beings" but as moral agents responding to their historical moment. She understood women's personal suffering as interconnected with the broader suffering of the era, and her missionary work addressed the collective burden experienced by Korean women under colonial oppression.

Rhoda Yang 梁雨露德

Rhoda Yang (양우로더), commissioned to northern Manchuria in 1924 by the Women's Foreign Missionary Society of the Methodist Episcopal Church, was among the earliest officially appointed Korean women missionaries to that region. During a period when women's participation in missions faced severe restrictions, she exemplified the principle of "women evangelizing women," receiving support from the American Methodist women's society. Her education and articulation equipped her to provide both spiritual and organizational leadership.

In Manchuria, Rhoda Yang established women's self-governing missionary associations known as Boho Yeohoe (Protection Societies for Women). These groups conducted prayer meetings, Bible study sessions, temperance campaigns, and charitable work. Significantly, they operated independently of male-dominated church structures, with members pooling personal resources to fund regional missionary initiatives2.

Yang's mission particularly emphasized marginalized women—including widows, rural women, and young girls—encouraging them to reclaim agency through education and spiritual formation. Her Wednesday Bible classes created innovative spaces where women learned not only to read Scripture but to interpret and apply it independently. This represented a radical development within a patriarchal religious culture that typically restricted women's theological expression.

Her mission maintained deep connections to national identity. Under Japanese colonial rule, women's missions in Manchuria frequently served as sites of both spiritual awakening and national consciousness. Rhoda Yang believed that "educating women is the foundation for rebuilding the nation," and this conviction motivated her investment in Christian education that prepared local women to become future church leaders.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

The growing size and complexity of the Manchurian Catholic church prompted a number of divisions during this decade:

Yanji, the heavily Korean region adjacent to the Korean border along the Yalu River was made a Prefecture Apostolic in 1928. A Prefecture Apostolic is a designation specifically created for evangelization. It precedes the formation of a Vicariate Apostolic, which is erected to nurture a fledgling church. Yanji was given the designation of Vicariate Apostolic in 1938.

Qiqihar was erected as a “sui iuris” (self-governing, i.e., independent) mission in 1928. This sort of designation is for an area with very few Catholics or one that is under persecution, I suspect that the meaning here was the former, but cannot say precisely why the decision was made to separate the region in this way. By way of context, Outer Mongolia was given the same designation in 1922, so my suspicion is that what was being intended was to protect the small but growing church in Jilin by removing the burden of ministering Qiqihar. Qiqihar would be named a Prefecture Apostolic in 1939.

From the two VAs of Mukden and Jehol (Rehe), the new VA of Sipingjie (Szepingkai) was created in 1929. Unlike the previous two divisions, which this was the result of the growth of the church in this area. This region would be administered by the Canadian-born Bishop Joseph Lapierre (MEP), who took up his post in 1930.

Catholic estimate the entire Christian (certainly to mean Catholic) population of Manchuria at 60 thousand. In 1920, there were four religious communities: Changchun, Harbin, Xiaobajiazi, and Hulan.

Missionaries from the Quebec Society of Foreign Missions arrived in 1925. They were followed by priests of the Society of Foreign Missions of America (Maryknoll) in 1929.

During the 1920s, Korean settlers began arriving overland from Korea into Jilin. The timing of this exodus represented a push factor of land poverty and Japanese repression at home (particularly for Christians following the unrest of 1919), as well as a pull factor of relatively open farmland in Jilin and a market for high-yield flooded field (Japonica) rice, a crop that Koreans knew much better than the local Chinese farmers, and which had a growing demand in Japan. Sources mention that there were initially very few Catholics among these Korean settlers, and that there were plans to send someone to Korea to find a catechumen to preach the Gospel among them.

Protestants

The Protestant Union remained under its existing structure, both in terms of its constituent mission groups and of its relation to the larger organization (Synod) based in Shanghai. Sources emphasize that the number of Christians was consistently growing, but give no numbers, most likely because they did not themselves have a way of counting a congregation that was intended to grow organically under its own power. The rise of nationalism and desire to increase Chinese ownership over the church prompted the Protestants to lower the profile of their social mission. This trend would accelerate under the Japanese in the subsequent decade.

Canadian Presbyterian Mission

During the 1920s, Canadian Presbyterian missionaries operating in northeastern Korea and southern Manchuria encountered significant resistance from the Christian communities they had established. The tensions reached a critical point in 1922 when students at the Hamheung Boys' Academy initiated a strike to challenge the mission's authoritarian approach to school administration, curriculum development, and personnel decisions. The students demanded increased representation of Korean teachers, expanded admission policies, and enhanced educational facilities. When mission leadership rejected these requests, the conflict intensified, culminating in February 1922 when several students committed arson against the academy building. This dramatic incident demonstrated the depth of frustration among young Korean Christians toward what they perceived as missionary paternalism3.

The student uprising marked the beginning of a sustained campaign for institutional reform. Throughout the mid-1920s, various groups within the Korean Christian community, including church elders, students, and lay members, organized systematic efforts to demand structural changes in mission governance. Although missionaries such as L.L. Young initially opposed these demands due to concerns about maintaining institutional authority, they gradually recognized the necessity of power-sharing arrangements. This recognition led to the establishment of a Joint Board that included both Canadian missionaries and Korean church leaders, creating a collaborative framework for educational, financial, and administrative decision-making4. These modifications represented more than superficial adjustments; they reflected a fundamental shift in mission philosophy responding to the growing influence of Korean nationalism under Japanese colonial rule.

The transformation produced far-reaching consequences for both educational institutions and religious communities. Schools such as the Hamheung academies underwent secularization processes that better aligned their educational approaches with Korean expectations for modern schooling. Simultaneously, Korean Christians assumed greater responsibility for evangelistic activities and institutional management, establishing the foundation for an autonomous diasporic Christian network throughout Manchuria.

Scottish and Irish Presbyterian Missions

Scottish and Irish Presbyterian missionaries, who concentrated their efforts primarily in southern Manchuria, entered the 1920s with established theological frameworks and increasing dedication to ecumenical collaboration. Their mission activities encompassed religious education, refugee assistance, and comprehensive medical services. However, the changing political environment, characterized by rising anti-foreign sentiment and expanding Chinese nationalism, compelled them to reevaluate their operational strategies.

The May Thirtieth Movement of 1925 represented a decisive moment in this reassessment process. Previously committed to political neutrality, many British missionaries, particularly those affiliated with the United Free Church of Scotland, began expressing sympathy for Chinese sovereignty movements. The movement's anti-imperialist demonstrations and the violent responses from foreign authorities forced missionaries to examine their roles within colonial power structures. Some began advocating for more respectful cross-cultural engagement and the development of local church leadership5.

These missionaries also expanded their social involvement, increasing relief and development activities among marginalized populations, including Korean immigrants, war refugees, and individuals requiring medical care. While tensions with traditional Chinese religious practices continued, several missionaries began investigating potential compatibility between Confucian or Buddhist values and Christian theology, thereby establishing preliminary foundations for contextual theological approaches. Despite inherent risks, Scottish and Irish missions contributed significantly to developing theological and organizational groundwork for Chinese-led Presbyterian churches.

Evangelical Lutheran Church of America (ELCA)

The 1920s represented a strategic transition period for the Evangelical Lutheran Church of America mission in Manchuria. Initially focused on Western-directed evangelism, the Lutheran mission progressively adopted more localized and sustainable methodologies in response to evolving social and political conditions. This transformation from a "mission-centered" to a "church-centered" approach emphasized indigenous leadership development, theological education for local pastors, and the transfer of institutional authority to Chinese Christians.

The Communist uprisings of 1927 served as a crucial catalyst for this strategic shift. As violence escalated and missionaries faced evacuation or operational suspension, ELCA leadership recognized that long-term viability required the cultivation of resilient local church communities. This understanding prompted initiatives including seminary establishment, liturgical text translation, and the expansion of Chinese lay leadership networks6.

By the decade's conclusion, the ELCA's Manchurian mission had evolved into a more collaborative enterprise. Theological and administrative decision-making increasingly transferred to Chinese Christians. This transformation not only strengthened Lutheran presence in the region but also ensured institutional survival during the turbulent 1930s and subsequent Japanese occupation.

Wesleyan Methodist Medical Missions

Confronting rising anti-foreign sentiment and political instability, Wesleyan Methodist missionaries concentrated on medical missions as one of the most effective and trusted forms of Christian engagement. Throughout the 1920s, Wesleyan hospitals in cities including Hankow and Wuchang provided essential healthcare services to thousands of Chinese patients. The Jubilee Women's Hospital exemplified this approach by not only treating medical conditions but also training female physicians and nurses, an innovation that significantly influenced gender roles in both medical practice and church leadership7.

Medical care, unlike evangelical preaching, generally received welcome reception even in regions characterized by anti-Christian sentiment or hostility. Consequently, Methodist missions established reputations for compassion, professional competence, and public service. Medical practitioners such as W. Arthur Tatchell and Margaret Bennett functioned as both healthcare providers and cultural intermediaries, incorporating Christian values through service rather than explicit evangelistic activities.

The broader impact of medical missions included enhanced legitimacy for Christianity among local populations. Hospitals became venues for dialogue, community building, and mutual respect, particularly benefiting women and economically disadvantaged individuals. These institutions addressed not only physical health needs but also established foundations for future evangelistic receptivity in both urban and rural Manchurian communities.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Catholic sources emphasize the good political relations enjoyed during this period. As stated in the annual report from 1922, “We could not flatter ourselves to have the sympathy of the civil authorities and military, but it is right to recognize that demonstrations of hostility are rare and that in several circumstances, we profited from a rather benevolent neutrality.”

Another describes a Catholic ceremony in Changchun presided over by Bishop Lalouyer, and attended by numerous Chinese and international notables: Lépissier, the French Consul to Harbin, a French member of the East China Railway technical council, the Japanese Consul (of Changchun, presumably), the “Chinese General in charge of Changchun” [sic], the prefect, the chief of police, the first two judges of the court, and several members of various administrations. Assuming that few if any of the assembled dignitaries were themselves Catholic, this was a display of political support. A similar scene is described at a procession in Harbin, with the city’s Polish population turning out in large numbers.

Korean Presbyterians and the Rise of Indigenous Leadership

The 1920s witnessed a pivotal transformation in Christian missions in Manchuria, as Presbyterian churches transitioned from foreign missionary control to Korean-led ecclesiastical governance. During the earlier missionary period, Western organizations dominated the Manchurian mission field, particularly the American Northern Presbyterian Church (PCUSA) and the Canadian Presbyterian Mission. Missionaries including John Ross, George McIntyre, and Robert Grierson established foundational work in Bible translation, evangelist training, and church planting throughout the region8.

This era of Western leadership began to shift following the March First Independence Movement of 1919, which catalyzed significant demographic changes. Manchuria became a primary destination for Korean refugees, including nationalists, lay believers, and pastors fleeing colonial persecution. These migrants rapidly transformed Manchuria into a dynamic center of Korean Christianity, establishing churches, schools, and publishing houses while navigating political exile and cultural displacement9.

Responding to the growing need for pastoral care and institutional organization, the General Assembly of the Chosun Presbyterian Church implemented decisive structural reforms. In 1920, it established the Southern Manchurian Presbytery (남만노회), followed by the Kando (Jiandao) Presbytery (간도노회) in 1921. These newly formed presbyteries operated under Korean clergy leadership, including Kim Jin-geun, Kim Young-je, and Choi Sung-joo, who received official ordination and commissioning from the Korean General Assembly10. Under their direction, local churches expanded substantially, theological training centers were founded, and new congregations emerged independent of direct foreign oversight.

By the decade's end, Presbyterian churches in Manchuria had developed into self-governing extensions of the Korean church rather than mere mission outposts of Western boards. While foreign missionaries occasionally provided financial assistance or logistical support, Korean pastors exercised complete authority over liturgical, administrative, and educational matters. Churches increasingly served multifunctional roles, hosting political meetings, operating printing presses, and providing community education. This transformation established the Manchurian church as both a model of indigenous Christian leadership and a means of preserving Korean national identity within the context of Japanese colonialism.

Korean Christian Women’s Education and Evangelism

In 1920s Manchuria, Korean Christian women's education and evangelism evolved beyond traditional church outreach to become a distinct movement that intertwined faith, liberation, and national identity. Sustained by both Methodist and Presbyterian efforts, this movement offered Korean women in exile and diaspora a new theological and political language to articulate their existence, organize communities, and participate in the larger story of resistance against colonial oppression. The Christian women's education and evangelical movement in Manchuria was not a monolithic institution but rather a diffuse network of schools, training classes, Sunday groups, and grassroots societies that reframed women's roles from passive receivers to active agents of mission, reform, and nationhood.

For Methodist women, the work of evangelism and education in Manchuria and Siberia was inseparable from the recovery of voice and dignity. In a society where women had long been denied names and public presence, the Methodist women's mission emphasized naming—not only in the literal sense of receiving Christian names but in cultivating self-awareness as full human beings with God-given worth. As one missionary later wrote, "Having a name meant having existence. Without a name, there was no agency, no will, no independence."11 Women like Choi Naomi and Rhoda Yang were at the forefront of this awakening, founding women's missionary societies (여선교회), Protection Associations for Women (보호여회), and Sunday Schools that created spaces for women to learn to read, interpret the Bible, speak publicly, and engage in mutual aid. In doing so, they actively challenged the Confucian patriarchal order that had long confined women to silence.

Education formed a cornerstone of this transformative work. Female missionaries organized Banil School (short-term literacy and anti-illiteracy classes for adult women), trained Bible women, and initiated public prayer and scripture memorization competitions. These educational activities were not isolated from the broader political context but empowered women to see the Japanese colonial condition as both a national loss and a spiritual crisis that demanded inner transformation and outward witness.

While less institutionalized than its Methodist counterpart, the Presbyterian women's movement in Manchuria developed a robust infrastructure through Bu-nyeo Dori-ban (婦女道理班, Women's Moral Doctrine Classes), informal home meetings, and theological literacy programs. Initiated around 1923 in areas such as Laeyang (萊陽) and Jimo (即墨), these classes provided rural and displaced women with basic education in vernacular Korean and scriptural knowledge. The dual aim was to build women's spiritual resilience and to raise a generation of indigenous Christian women leaders who could disciple others within their own communities.12

The wives of missionaries—including Bang Seung-hwa, Gye Eun-seung, and Kim Jong-sun—played pivotal roles in these efforts. They served not merely as translators and cultural bridges but often led classes themselves, developed women's curricula, and provided counseling and community care. These missionary wives rejected the notion of being mere "helpers" to their husbands and instead embodied a form of "relational evangelism" that reached women whom male missionaries could not access. The evangelical character of the Presbyterian movement extended beyond spiritual conversion to encompass nation-building theology. As one missionary wife observed, "To teach a woman to read the Gospel is to restore her homeland's spirit." The movement's teachings encouraged Korean women in exile to embrace not only personal salvation but also communal hope, linking the promise of Christ's liberation to the longed-for liberation of the Korean nation.

Across denominational lines, this Christian women's movement absorbed and reinterpreted the spirit of the March First Movement of 1919. Many women involved in Methodist and Presbyterian educational missions had either participated in or were deeply inspired by the patriotic fervor of that period, often blurring the line between church gathering and nationalist meeting. Missionary societies became spaces for clandestine reading of Korean independence tracts, while women teachers embedded anti-Japanese critique in lessons on biblical figures like Esther and Deborah. The movement extended into broader reform campaigns including the temperance movement, abolition of concubinage, child education, and women's public hygiene—causes viewed not merely as moral imperatives but as national responsibilities.

The theological framework that distinguished this movement was its integration of spiritual and political liberation. These women did not frame their activism simply as social reform but as a manifestation of divine calling. Their belief that Christ had set women free from sin extended to freedom from feudalism, ignorance, and imperial domination. Women's missionary education thus became a covenantal act—a way of stewarding both spiritual gifts and national destiny.

By the end of the 1920s, the Christian women's education and evangelism movement in Manchuria had accomplished what few contemporary movements could: it offered Korean women a framework of empowerment that was simultaneously religious, social, and national. Through women's societies, Bible classes, and localized schools, the movement laid the foundation for postwar Korean Christian women's leadership in both North and South Korea. It also demonstrated a rare case of mission-turned-indigenous-resistance. Rather than functioning merely as colonial religious appendages, these women's groups became networks of local agency, theological innovation, and sociopolitical critique. Their faith was not an escape from history but a way of entering history redemptively.

Key Theological Issues

I could not find anything resembling a doctrinal issue specific to this period.

The two previously discusses questions: whether to increase the pace of indigenization (the call for greater indigenization was particularly pronounced amongst Catholic clergy) and how to square the “imperialist” nature of mission Christianity with growing Chinese statism / nationalism remained in evidence. The Protestant Union mission was at the forefront of this, with a 1926 statement that mission workers was simply “on loan” to Chinese churches, which retained autonomy. It was subsequently decided that the location and work of foreign missionaries by assigned by the Chinese Church itself.

The question of how to respond to Japan’s increasing presence in the region was not yet significant. The Protestants did tend to view Japan with greater suspicion than did the Catholics, in part because the conflict with native Protestants had already (1919) emerged in colonial Korea, although there was as yet no significant criticism of Japan in the Protestant missionary sources. Catholic express a clearer view that the Japanese presence was a force for progress, and a necessary counterbalance to the rise of Bolshevist infiltration from the South or from the Soviet Union.

Although not doctrinal issues, this difference does reveal differing attitudes towards political pastoralism, i.e., the long-term presence of missionaries and the depth of long-term political and cultural influence that the two missions envisioned leaving in the region.

Institutional Division and Strategic Realignment of Korean Presbyterian Missions in Manchuria

The 1920s marked a period of significant institutional restructuring within Korean Presbyterian missions in Manchuria. As Korean congregations multiplied following the 1910s, the Pyongbuk Presbytery (평북노회), which had originally overseen the region, became inadequate for managing the expanding scope and complexity of church operations in Manchuria. The Chosun Presbyterian Church responded by implementing a systematic territorial division, establishing the Southern Manchurian Presbytery in 1920 and the Kando (Jiandao) Presbytery in 192113.

The Southern Manchurian Presbytery operated from urban centers including Mukden (Shenyang), Tonghua, Tieling, and Liaoyang—regions experiencing rapid industrialization and urban development. Kim Jin-geun and Choi Sung-joo had previously initiated missionary work in these areas, which proved conducive to advanced church administration, presbytery-level ordination, and financial self-sufficiency14.

The Kando Presbytery, by contrast, administered predominantly rural, ethnically Korean settlements in Yeongil, Hualong, and Yongjeong. Pastors such as Kim Young-je and Kim Nae-beom emphasized evangelism alongside education-oriented ministries in these areas. Churches in this region frequently functioned as schools, literacy centers, and political meeting spaces, reflecting the area's strong connections to Korean nationalist activities.

A 1920s comity agreement among Protestant denominations in Manchuria established territorial divisions designed to minimize inter-denominational competition and enhance outreach efficiency. The arrangement allocated:

  • South Manchuria to the Chosun Presbyterian Church (Bongcheon Presbytery)

  • North Jiandao (400,000 Koreans, approximately 10,000 Christians) to the Canadian Presbyterian Church

  • West Jiandao (350,000 Koreans, approximately 4,600 Christians) to the American Northern Presbyterian Church

  • North Manchuria and Siberia to the Korean-led North Manchuria Presbytery

  • Adjacent regions to Methodist jurisdictions, divided between the Northern Methodist Episcopal Church and Southern Methodist Episcopal Church15

This strategic territorial organization reflected more than administrative efficiency; it demonstrated the emergence of Korean churches as equal mission partners capable of developing contextually appropriate ministries responsive to local political and cultural conditions. Through these regional presbyteries, Korean Christians in Manchuria established the institutional framework necessary for sustained autonomy and missionary expansion throughout Northeast Asia.

Anti-Christian Leaders

None to speak of.

Considering that we are only a couple of decades away from the Boxers, it is safe to assume that missionary writers would have been very sensitive to any potential threat.

Yet even sources that occasionally mention that a student or local official had some harsh words to say about the Christians, no names are given, and there is no follow up that would indicate a particularly irksome individual or organized anti-Christian movement. For the latter, please see below.

Anti-Christian Organizations

As indicated, there was no mention in the sources of an organized anti-Christian movement. There were, however concerns that such a movement could develop over time. In the eyes of mission writers, this future threat would come from one of three sources:

1. Energized Guomindang governance:

Catholic sources from this decade contain passing mentions about the “spirit of independence” growing among catechists, as well wariness of anti-foreign / anti-Christian sentiments from local officials (anecdotally, these officials often seem to come from the South, including in previous periods)

Although less frequent or violent than those further south, outbreaks of anti-foreign sentiment were often precipitated by events elsewhere, such as the 1925 shooting of protesters in Shanghai. The Protestant Synod in Shanghai offered to relinquish extraterritorial rights for its missionaries, and the Manchuria mission issued a statement in sympathy with the protesters and Chinese national aspirations. In 1927, local authorities banned any new construction of a school, church or dispensary. In 1931, they forced the closure of the Catholic Franco-Chinese College, which had around one hundred students in Shenyang. This school had been the site of significant nationalist agitation.

2. Communism:

There is some concern of Communism, either in the form of hostility from the Soviet Union or infiltration from the South. Notably, the latter is associated (in the sources) with radical elements in the Guomindang, and secondarily with Sun Yat-sen’s sanmin zhuyi ideology.

3. Public disorder:

The most consistently-mentioned threat was the lack of order. Fed by demobilized or deserter troops, gangs of bandits controlled large swathes of countryside, attacking travelers and occasionally villages. Foreigners and Christians were singled out the extent that they were perceived as a high-value target, rather than being driven by anti-foreign sentiment.
