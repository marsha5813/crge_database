Manchuria: Agency (Pre-1900)

Christian or Pro-Christian Leaders

Catholic missions:
Catholic missions in Manchuria were led by the Missions Etrangères de Paris. Because the ecclesiastical jurisdiction of Manchuria was not yet recognized as a diocese, but rather as an Apostolic Vicariate (essentially a diocese in training), the head of each jurisdiction was first named a de jure bishop of a defunct diocese elsewhere in the world. This titular appointment was purely a formality in order to give the appointment the rank of bishop. I do not add them here, but they do appear in official biographies. The official title of a de facto bishop of a Apostolic Vicariate is an Vicar Apostolic. In 1898, the Catholic jurisdiction was split into Manchuria and South Manchuria.

Bishop Emmanuel-Jean-François Verrolles: 方濟各 / 吳樂爾 (1805-1878)

Born in Bayonne, France, in 1805. Ordained in 1828 and joined the Société des Missions Etrangères in 1830. Verrolles’ entire missionary career was spent in China. From 1831-1837, he was a mission priest in Muping 穆坪, located in the far west of Sichuan. In 1838, he was placed in charge of the new Apostolic Vicarage of Manchuria and Mongolia, later changed to the Apostolic Vicariate of Manchuria after Mongolia was returned to the jurisdiction of the vicariate of Beijing. Seeking a physical home for the new mission, Verrolles purchased a piece of land in the village of Bajiazi 八家子 near Tieling 铁岭 for the construction of a church and presbytery. Bajiazi already had a few hundred Catholics and would remain one of the key concentrations of Catholics in Manchuria. A second church at Yangguan 阳关 would serve as his residence.

Verrolles traveled twice to Europe to seek political and papal support for the new mission. Beyond resources, he sought to ensure against the further erosion of territory of the sort that had placed Mongolia under the jurisdiction of Beijing. Verrolles was well received by Pope Pius IX as well as in the French court, having been offered an arch-bishopric by King Louis-Philippe and receiving an annuity of three thousand francs from Prince Louis-Napoleon.

Verrolles oversaw the transition to the new era following the treaties of Tianjin and Beijing (1858-1860), which gave missionaries the freedom to travel and preach openly in China. He sent priests out across Manchuria, particularly to non-Han areas of the north as well as into Siberia. His successes invited conflict with the more recently-arrived Protestants, who made him the subject of satirical articles in the local press. Verrolles died in 1878 and was interred in Yingzi (Yingkou). Following his death, his residence at Yangguan was repurposed as a seminary.

Bishop Laurent Guillon 紀 M.E.P. (1854-1900)

Born 1854 in Aix-en-Provence, France. Ordained in 1877, having already joined the Société des Missions Etrangères while still in seminary. Guillon departed for Manchuria in 1878 and studied Chinese language at the seminary in Yangguan. He was struck ill twice, once by smallpox in 1880, and again by typhoid in 1882.

In 1889, Guillon was appointed Vicar Apostolic of Manchuria. He succeeded the brief tenure of two predecessors, Constant Dubail, M.E.P., and Louis-Hippolyte-Aristide Raguit, M.E.P., each of whom had died soon after taking office. An energetic administrator, Guillon quickly grew the number of Catholics and Catholic mission in the territory. Besides sending priests on rounds to deliver sacraments to far-flung Catholic villages, Guillon placed particular emphasis on developing Catholic social institutions such as orphanages, schools and dispensaries. This strategy aimed to counter the successes of the Protestant missions, with whom Catholic relations were strained. Guillon oversaw the Catholic mission response to the 1894-1895 Sino-Japanese War, the first phase of the ongoing military crisis that would engulf the region.

In 1898, Guillon petitioned successfully for the region to be divided into two Apostolic Vicariates, Southern Manchuria and Manchuria, corresponding roughly to the provinces of Liaoning and Jilin, respectively (Heilongjiang was still aspirational, it would in 1931 become the Apostolic Prefecture of Qiqihar, essentially an aspirational Apostolic Vicariate designated specifically for evangelization). Guillon remained Vicar Apostolic of Liaoning, which at the time of separation had 23 missionaries, 8 indigenous priests, 115 catechists, and 20,050 Catholics.

Guillon held the position of Vicar Apostolic at the time of the Boxer Uprising. He remained at his post in Mukden and was killed during an assault on the cathedral in 1900.

Bishop Pierre-Marie-François Lalouyer M.E.P. 藍祿葉 (1850-1923)

Born in 1850 in Rennes, France. Joined Société des Missions Etrangères in 1871 and was ordained priest of the society in 1873. Later that same year, he arrived in the port city of Niuzhuang and was dispatched by Emmanuel Verrolles to begin study of Chinese language at Yangguan. In 1874, Lalouyer was given a position in the Mukden seminary, and in 1889 was placed in charge of the seminary at Chaling. In between these two positions, he was head of the parish of Mukden and sent to a district on the western coast of Liaodong.

In 1897, Lalouyer was named coadjutor along with Guillon of the Apostolic Vicariate of Manchuria and sent to the northern city of Jilin, where he purchased a piece of land for a house. This was in preparation for the subsequent (1898) division of Manchuria into two Apostolic Vicariates, Southern Manchuria and Manchuria, with Lalouyer being placed in charge of the former.

Protestant missions:
Protestant leadership came from the Scottish and Irish missions, later joined by British, American, and Swedish missions. The Scottish and Irish joined in 1885 into the Presbytery of Manchuria, often simply referred to as the Union, which would remain the umbrella organization for all Protestant efforts moving forward.

William C. Burns (1815-1868)

Burns was the pioneer Protestant missionary to Manchuria.

An energetic evangelist, Burns was deeply connected to the revivalist movement, having experienced a moment of personal inspiration at a revival meeting during his early twenties that convinced him of a personal calling from God. Burns’ career had already taken him through his native Scotland, Britain, Ireland and Canada before he was sent by the English Presbyterian Church on mission in China in 1847. Burns first encountered the Chinese language on his shipboard journey to Hong Kong, eventually becoming sufficiently proficient to pen a Chinese translation of the 17th–century Christian tract, the Pilgrim’s Progress. Like his evangelical wanderings, Burns travelled frequently rather than remaining within one mission station, gradually moving north from Hong Kong, Shantou, Xiamen, Fuzhou and Beijing before settling in the Manchurian port town of Niuzhuang in 1867.

In that year, Burns appealed to the English Presbyterian Mission board to add the region to the work of the mission. That successful appeal took on the additional tenor of a dying wish, as Burns died after a brief illness the subsequent year. Subsequent missionary writings portray Burns and his call to mission in highly reverential terms. (Boyd, 53-54)

Dugald Christie (1855-1936)

The first and most important medical missionary to Manchuria.

Born in Scotland, Christie trained at the Royal College of Physicians and Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh, receiving both qualifications in 1881. The subsequent year, he was sent to Manchuria, volunteering to leave the more comfortable foreign enclave in the port city of Niuzhuang and instead move upcountry to the city of Mukden (Shenyang) where he opened a small dispensary that by 1884 had grown into a twelve-bed hospital. In his memoir, Christie describes significant resistance to Western medicine, including patients who assumed nefarious intent from the drugs stocked in the clinic, fears that he attributed to rumors started by Chinese doctors. Christie describes an incident during the Sino-French Tonkin war of 1884 in which a French Catholic priest visited the clinic, igniting rumors that visitor’s long black robe concealed a child which was to be sacrificed for its eyes and heart. Despite these setbacks, the clinic continued to train local Chinese assistants and in 1892 enrolled its first class of medical students as the Shengching (Shengjing, another name for Shenyang) Medical School. (Thirty Years in Moukden, pp. 5-6)

Having established this foundation, both the hospital and Christie’s career as a medical missionary would later take off in more dramatic form in response to later public health crises, particularly the outbreak of pneumonic plague in 1910.

Edited by Sun

In the study of Manchurian missionaries, it's difficult to find a figure as fascinating as Dugald Christie (1855–1936). He was not merely a medical missionary but a true pioneer who indigenized Christianity and Western medicine within the complex religious and cultural landscape of Manchuria. After obtaining his medical qualifications in Edinburgh in 1881, Christie arrived in Manchuria in 1882 as a missionary for the Scottish United Presbyterian Church, making a distinctive choice from the start. While other missionaries remained in the safe port city of Newchwang (牛莊), he boldly ventured inland to Mukden (奉天, present-day Shenyang). This was a place where traditional Manchu religious concepts intermingled with Chinese folk beliefs, harboring deep-seated distrust of Western religion.

The reality Christie faced in Mukden was harsh. The small clinic he established (later expanded to a 12-bed hospital in 1884) was viewed with suspicion by local residents. Particularly intriguing is how the rumors circulating at the time reflected the religious imagination of the Manchurian region. The story that "French priests hid children under black robes to extract their eyes and hearts" went beyond simple xenophobia, bearing striking resemblance to the evil spirits described in Manchurian shamanism. Christie analyzed how these rumors spread in connection with specific events during the Sino-French War of 1884, demonstrating his considerable understanding of local religious culture1. However, Christie's true innovation emerged with the establishment of the Shengjing Medical School (盛京醫學堂) in 1892. This institution was not simply a place to teach Western medicine. Christie sought to reinterpret traditional Manchurian concepts of healing—the integrated treatment of body and soul—within a Christian context. He taught students not only medical skills but also the Christian spirit of service, which strangely resonated with the tradition of shamanic healers serving their communities in Manchurian shamanism. In some ways, Christie was transplanting a Christian "healer-servant" model onto Manchurian soil2.

Christie's success was also evident in his political wisdom. He built trust with the local power structure by treating the Governor of Mukden and hundreds of officials. This was not merely medical service but a Christian transformation of the special relationship that healers traditionally maintained with those in power in Manchurian tradition. A fellow missionary recorded that he "treated hundreds of officials and their families," creating a favorable atmosphere for the entire missionary enterprise3. Even more impressive was Christie's maintenance of political neutrality. When Chinese officials in Manchuria suspected Western missionaries of being agents of imperialism, he remained strictly within the religious sphere. When missionary facilities were damaged in 1883 and 1885, he chose direct communication with Qing officials rather than relying on consular protection through treaty rights. This respected the cultural custom in Manchurian tradition where conflict resolution occurred through direct dialogue between authorities4.

By the 1890s, Christie had firmly established himself as a pioneer who indigenized Christianity and Western medicine on Manchurian soil. His approach was not a simple transplantation of Western culture but was achieved through creative dialogue with local religious culture, trust-building with the power elite, and the pursuit of long-term change through education. This became a model for subsequent Manchurian missionaries to follow, making its significance profound5.

John Ross (1842–1915) 羅約翰

Raised in a Gaelic-speaking area of northern Scotland, Ross served several local churches before leaving for China under the United Presbyterian Church in 1872.

Ross was based initially in Yingkou and later moved to Shenyang, where he became active evangelizing to the local Korean community. He would complete the first Korean-language translation of the New Testament that was widely used in Korea, as well as Korean language primers and an English-language history of Korea for use by later missionaries.

Ross was known for being an institution-builder, raising funds for the Dongguan church outside of the then city walls of Shenyang. That church was a center of the Korean Christian community in Shenyang. It was damaged in the Boxer violence, but subsequently rebuilt. In his writing, he encourages a mission strategy based on the acceptance of Chinese religions and practices. He returned to Scotland in 1910 and died in 1915.

Rev. John Ross (1842–1915) represents a critical figure in understanding the complex dynamics of Christian evangelization within the indigenous religious landscape of Manchuria during the late Qing period. Operating primarily from Newchwang (牛莊) between the 1870s and 1890s under the United Presbyterian Mission (UPM), Ross's methodology reveals significant insights into cross-cultural religious transmission and the negotiation of foreign spiritual authority within established Manchu-Chinese religious frameworks.

Ross's translation enterprise, initiated in 1874, demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of linguistic religiosity that extends beyond mere textual conversion. His collaboration with converts like Ma Tai Wu from Pyongyang produced a northeastern dialectal rendering of the New Testament that deliberately circumvented Beijing's linguistic hegemony—a decision reflecting both practical evangelistic concerns and implicit recognition of regional religious identity. The 1880 publication represents the first localized Christian scripture produced within Manchuria's cultural sphere, marking a pivotal moment in the indigenization of foreign religious texts within the broader context of Manchu spiritual traditions.6

The 1881 evangelistic expedition through Shenyang and the Liaodong Peninsula illuminates the material conditions under which religious conversion occurred in rural Manchuria. Ross's documentation of transforming "fear into respect" through sustained presence in repurposed sacred and communal spaces reveals the gradual process by which foreign religious practices gained legitimacy within existing community structures.7 This approach contrasts sharply with more aggressive missionary strategies that directly challenged indigenous religious authorities.

Ross's diplomatic handling of the 1884 vandalism incident at Newchwang demonstrates his acute awareness of the political dimensions inherent in religious activity under Qing administration. By bypassing British consular intervention and engaging directly with local magistrates, Ross positioned the mission within existing administrative frameworks rather than as an extension of foreign political power—a strategy that acknowledged the complex relationship between religious authority and imperial governance in Manchurian society.8

The 1886 medical rumors crisis provides particularly valuable insight into the intersection of traditional healing practices and Christian medical missions within Manchu religious culture. The accusation regarding ritual extraction of children's organs reflects deeper anxieties about foreign spiritual power and its potential threat to indigenous therapeutic traditions. Ross's response—public transparency and direct engagement with traditional medical practitioners—reveals his understanding that successful evangelization required accommodation with, rather than wholesale rejection of, existing healing cosmologies.9

Ross's theological methodology, particularly his integration of Confucian ethical frameworks with Christian doctrine, demonstrates a nuanced approach to religious syncretism that recognized the sophisticated intellectual traditions within Manchu scholarly culture. His published analyses of Chinese religious systems reflect an ethnographic sensibility that, while maintaining Christian theological supremacy, acknowledged the moral and spiritual complexity of indigenous religious traditions—an approach that facilitated dialogue rather than mere replacement.10

The institutional legacy Ross established—vernacular translation, indigenous leadership development, local printing capacity, and diplomatic religious engagement—created sustainable frameworks for Christian presence within Manchurian religious ecology. These methodological innovations became foundational for subsequent Protestant missions throughout northeast Asia, demonstrating the lasting impact of religiously sensitive evangelistic approaches within complex multi-ethnic spiritual landscapes.

Wang Jingming, aka “Old Wang” (c. 1840–1885)

Described in contemporary publications as the first Chinese evangelist in Manchuria, “old Wang” was both an actual figure and an embodiment of the Protestant ideal of successful conversion. According to John Ross, Wang was “the first baptized, the first preacher set apart, and the first elder ordained by the writer.” (5)

Accounts of Old Wang’s life are fairly stylized, the longest being in a pamphlet by John Ross. This account presents Wang as a sincere convert, not always sophisticated in his understanding of the Christian teaching, but with a burning desire to preach to any who would listen (and some who would not). Matching well the Pauline model of heartfelt spiritual transformation, Old Wang embodied the ideals of Protestant mission in Manchuria. Wang was born in a village near Beijing, given a basic education but insufficient land to make a living as a farmer. He worked in various trades in Jilin and Niuzhuang, where he took up smoking opium. In 1873, Wang happened upon a copy of a gospel which intrigued him to regularly come and hear the preaching and catechisms of the Protestant mission. He soon converted and remained insatiable in his desire to understand the scriptures, a “diligent student of the truth, though not a remarkable bright one.” For the remainder of his life, Wang was an unquenchable evangelist, distilling the essence of the gospel in his heart and relating it in a manner that the poor in his own country could understand and appreciate, a process that marked the transition from mission to native church. In 1884, Wang became ill and after a period under the care of the mission. He died at roughly the age of 40.

A similar life trajectory of a figure nicknamed “Blind Chang” also appears in tracts of the time.

John McIntyre

07/18/1837 ~ 1905

Rev. John McIntyre's ministry in Liaoyang represents a fascinating case study in cross-cultural religious diplomacy during the late Qing period. His approach to establishing Protestant Christianity in inland Manchuria reveals both the pragmatic necessities and theological complexities facing foreign missionaries in this turbulent era.

When McIntyre arrived in Liaoyang in 1885, he encountered the fundamental challenge that would define his entire ministry: how to plant a foreign religion in soil already deeply cultivated by centuries of Confucian orthodoxy and popular religious practice. Rather than wielding the extraterritorial privileges available to him through unequal treaties, McIntyre chose a path of cultural accommodation that would prove both strategically wise and theologically significant. His negotiations with Qing officials over the church construction reveal a missionary who understood that lasting religious change required genuine local acceptance, not merely legal compliance. By establishing the church just outside Liaoyang's urban core, McIntyre demonstrated an intuitive grasp of Chinese spatial concepts—recognizing that religious buildings carried symbolic weight that extended far beyond their immediate function. This decision to work within, rather than against, local sensibilities would become characteristic of his entire approach.11

The 1886 conflict with the Confucian gentry provides perhaps the most illuminating example of McIntyre's methodology. The gentry's complaint about desecration of ancestral tablets touched the very heart of Chinese religious consciousness—the sacred obligation to honor one's ancestors. A less culturally attuned missionary might have dismissed such concerns as superstition. Instead, McIntyre recognized that he was encountering a genuine theological challenge that required serious engagement rather than dismissal. His willingness to modify the church structure and relocate certain activities demonstrated remarkable religious flexibility. More significantly, his decision to hold public discussions with the gentry represented an early experiment in what we might now call interfaith dialogue. This approach acknowledged that Christianity's success in China would depend not on overwhelming local traditions but on finding common ground with them.12

The crisis of 1894 during the First Sino-Japanese War tested McIntyre's carefully cultivated relationships under extreme pressure. His fluency in local dialect—a skill many missionaries never bothered to develop properly—proved crucial in navigating the dangerous waters of wartime suspicion. By positioning himself as a mediator between Chinese and Japanese authorities, McIntyre demonstrated how religious authority could transcend political boundaries when grounded in genuine local trust. His emphasis on Christianity's charitable and healing mission during this crisis reveals sophisticated theological understanding. Rather than defending abstract doctrinal positions, he presented Christianity through its practical fruits—an approach that resonated deeply with Confucian emphasis on moral action over theoretical knowledge.13

McIntyre's theological dialogues with Confucian scholars represent some of the earliest systematic attempts at finding common ground between Christian and Chinese philosophical traditions. His citations from the Analects and Mencius were not mere rhetorical flourishes but reflected genuine engagement with Chinese intellectual heritage. The scholar's comment that McIntyre's teachings aligned with "benevolence and righteousness" suggests that these discussions achieved something rare in missionary history—genuine intellectual respect across cultural boundaries.14

From a broader perspective on Manchu religious history, McIntyre's work occurred during a period when traditional religious boundaries were already shifting. The late Qing witnessed increasing syncretism between Han Chinese religious traditions and Manchu administrative structures. McIntyre's success in Liaoyang can be understood partly as his ability to recognize and work within these existing patterns of religious adaptation rather than fighting against them. His legacy extended far beyond the immediate expansion of Presbyterian infrastructure. By demonstrating that foreign religious traditions could establish themselves through cultural accommodation rather than cultural confrontation, McIntyre provided a template that would influence missionary strategy throughout Manchuria. His approach suggested that successful religious transformation required not the replacement of existing traditions but their gradual reinterpretation within new frameworks.15

The significance of McIntyre's decade in Liaoyang lies not merely in its immediate results but in its demonstration that cross-cultural religious encounter could be conducted with mutual respect and genuine intellectual engagement. His ministry stands as an early example of what contemporary missiologists might call "contextual theology"—the adaptation of Christian message to local cultural forms without abandoning essential theological commitments.

Robert W. Westwater

Dr. Robert W. Westwater of the Irish Presbyterian Mission represents a paradigmatic case study in the negotiation of biomedical authority within Manchuria's complex therapeutic landscape during the late Qing period. Operating within the contested epistemological terrain where Western scientific rationalism encountered indigenous healing cosmologies, Westwater's methodology illuminates critical dynamics of medical syncretism and the strategic accommodation of foreign therapeutic practices within established Manchu-Chinese wellness traditions.

Westwater's deployment to Liaoyang marked a significant departure from conventional missionary medical approaches that typically privileged technological superiority over cultural integration. His systematic engagement with local herbalists and traditional healers reveals an ethnographic sensibility that recognized indigenous therapeutic knowledge as legitimate epistemic systems rather than mere superstition to be displaced. The incorporation of vernacular medical terminology in clinical interactions demonstrates his understanding that successful biomedical intervention required linguistic and conceptual translation that respected existing frameworks of bodily understanding and pathological causation.16

The typhoid outbreak crisis of the mid-1890s provides exceptional insight into the mechanisms by which foreign medical authority gained legitimacy within Manchurian religious and social structures. The initial attribution of epidemic causation to the mission compound reflects deeper cosmological concerns about foreign spiritual contamination and the disruption of local therapeutic equilibrium. Westwater's response—public transparency of purification protocols and collaborative engagement with Chinese physicians—represents a sophisticated strategy of legitimation that positioned biomedicine as complementary to, rather than competitive with, indigenous healing traditions.

The Buddhist temple forum constitutes a particularly significant moment in the history of medical-religious syncretism in Manchuria. Westwater's deployment of Daoist cosmological metaphors to explain germ theory demonstrates his recognition that successful therapeutic communication required translation not merely of language but of fundamental ontological categories. The integration of traditional Chinese medical terminology such as "heat-clearing" (清热) and "qi flow" (气流) reveals his understanding that biomedical concepts could be rendered comprehensible and acceptable through existing therapeutic vocabularies.17

The 1897 mortuary examination controversy illuminates the complex intersection of medical practice with indigenous death ritual and spiritual protocol within Manchu religious culture. The accusation regarding improper handling of deceased bodies reflects profound anxieties about foreign intervention in sacred transitions between life and death. Westwater's strategic invocation of Confucian ethical justifications for post-mortem investigation demonstrates his sophisticated understanding of how biomedical procedures could be legitimated through appeal to established moral and philosophical authorities within Chinese intellectual tradition.18

Westwater's mentorship philosophy—treating indigenous healers as "neighbors with different skills" rather than competitive adversaries—reveals a methodological approach that recognized therapeutic pluralism as a sustainable framework for cross-cultural medical engagement. This perspective contrasts sharply with the more aggressive displacement strategies employed by other foreign medical practitioners and suggests an early recognition of what contemporary medical anthropology identifies as "therapeutic integration."19

The institutional legacy Westwater established in Liaoyang represents more than mere hospital construction; it constituted the creation of new epistemological spaces where biomedical and indigenous therapeutic knowledge systems could coexist and mutually inform practice. His approach to medical evangelization—emphasizing ethical transparency, cultural accommodation, and collaborative engagement—created sustainable frameworks for foreign medical presence that acknowledged rather than challenged the fundamental cosmological foundations of Manchurian healing traditions.20

This methodological sophistication had profound implications for the broader trajectory of medical missions throughout northeast Asia, establishing precedents for culturally sensitive biomedical intervention that recognized the complex relationship between therapeutic efficacy, religious legitimacy, and social acceptance within indigenous communities. Westwater's work thus provides essential insights into the processes by which foreign religious and medical systems gained acceptance within established cultural matrices without requiring wholesale abandonment of indigenous spiritual and therapeutic frameworks.

Christian or Pro-Christian Organizations

Christianity in Manchuria was both young and highly centralized, meaning that for most of its early history, the church was synonymous with Catholic and Protestant missions. Of these, the former was more tightly organized under a foreign mission presence, while the later emphasized initiative by native converts, aka indigenization.

Catholic mission:

Catholic mission during the period of initial Jesuit encounter during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries had made some impact on a handful of scattered villages, but apart from extremely infrequent contact with missionaries in Mongolia, these early communities spent the next few centuries largely cut off from the Church. Later missionaries would occasionally mention visiting a village with a long since dissipated Christian presence, with only the physical remains of a cross as testament.

The resurgence of formal Catholic mission presence in Manchuria only occurred in 1838, when the Apostolic Vicariate of Liaotong (Liaodong) was established out of the larger territory of the Diocese of Beijing, which at the time included most of China. This large territory was placed in the charge of the Société des Missions Etrangères, commencing with its inaugural head, Bishop Emmanuel Verrolles. (The MEP is/was founded in 1658 as a specialist in Asian mission. It was active throughout East Asia, particularly in areas of French interest.) Two years later, the Apostolic Vicariate of Liaotong was changed to the Apostolic Vicariate of Liaotong and Manchuria, representing the loss of the northwestern areas of Gansu (later renamed the Apostolic Vicariate of Shanxi) and the formation of a new mission under the Belgian Congregation of the Immaculate Heart of Mary (Scheutist) in Mongolia.

Manchuria would remain under this Vicariate Apostolic until 1898, when in response to the growth of the Church and the unwieldy size of the territory it covered, the Apostolic Vicariate of Liaotong and Manchuria was divided into the Apostolic Vicariates of southern and northern Manchuria, centered in Mukden and Jilin, respectively. This division came at the recommendation of Bishop Laurent Guillon, who remained the head of the Mukden Apostolic Vicariate until his death in 1900. In 1898, there were an estimated 25,000 Catholics in the entire territory of Manchuria, of which 17,433 were in Southern Manchuria.

Protestant Union

The earliest Protestant missionary in Manchuria was William C. Burns, who arrived in Niuzhuang in 1867. Although Burns died one year later, his arrival still had the effect of placing Manchuria on the Protestant map, his death serving as a rallying call for later generations of missionaries. Through the 1870s, the Protestant presence in Niuzhuang remained small, consisting of a chapel and dispensary, and one or two missionaries of the Scottish United Presbyterian Mission. Baptisms were relatively few and hostility towards foreigners occasionally broke into violence. In 1885, the Scottish mission and the Irish Presbyterian Mission joined in the Presbytery of Manchuria, often simply referred to as the Union, initiating a period of takeoff. (These missions had already been active in the British Isles. The Irish mission had a foundation in Gujarat. The Scottish mission only worked in China, with their efforts focused on Manchuria.) By 1893, the mission reported a total of 896 baptized Christians. With the 1894 outbreak of war between China and Japan, all missionaries were ordered to leave the interior and seek shelter in the port, a decision which Protestant sources portray as allowing the native church to stand on its own, but by Catholic sources as the Protestant missionaries abandoning their flock. Regardless, the Protestant mission grew rapidly in the subsequent years aided by the influx of new missionaries and the reputational boost provided by the mission’s medical and charitable work, particularly during the period of Sino-Japanese War. Over 2,500 were baptized in 1899 alone, and the total membership of both missions of the Manchurian (Protestant) Church included 19,646 members, as well as 251 churches, chapels and houses of prayer (Boyd 63)

Scottish United Presbyterian Mission (UPM)

From 1872 to 1900, the Scottish United Presbyterian Mission (UPM) in Fengtian—what we now call Liaoning Province in South Manchuria—launched one of the most fascinating experiments in cross-cultural Christianity that Northeast Asia had ever seen. This wasn't your typical Western missionary venture where foreigners simply transplanted their church structures onto foreign soil. What happened here was far more complex: a genuine wrestling match between Scottish Presbyterian theology and the harsh realities of a borderland in flux.

The timing couldn't have been more dramatic. When UPM missionaries like Dr. John Ross and Rev. Alexander Williamson arrived, Manchuria was undergoing massive upheaval. The Treaty of Tianjin in 1858 had cracked open the region, and the Qing government—stretched thin by internal rebellions and foreign pressure—finally lifted restrictions on Han Chinese migration into their ancestral homeland. The result was the great Chuang Guandong movement: over 25 million Chinese, mostly desperate farmers from Shandong and Hebei, flooded into Manchuria between 1891 and 194221. Picture this human tide of displaced people, Korean refugees, indigenous groups, and opportunistic merchants all converging in a region where empires competed and survival was never guaranteed. This was the mission field the UPM inherited The Presbyterians brought their trademark theological rigor—catechetical instruction, systematic training of native preachers, and church governance modeled directly on Scottish polity. But here's where things get interesting: John Ross's 1887 translation of the New Testament into Korean, working out of Fengtian with Korean merchants and scholars who had fled to Manchuria, became a pivotal moment that extended far beyond linguistics. This wasn't just about making scripture accessible; Ross had essentially created the textual foundation for Korean Protestantism and opened a corridor of influence from Manchuria straight into the Korean Peninsula22.

What fascinates me about this translation project is how it fundamentally altered the power dynamics of the mission. Once Korean and Chinese converts could read scripture in their own languages, they stopped being passive recipients of foreign religious instruction. They became interpreters, catechists, preachers—active agents in their own spiritual communities. The center of authority gradually shifted from missionary control toward indigenous leadership, creating what we might call a vernacular Christianity that was both authentically Presbyterian and genuinely local. But the UPM quickly discovered that theological purity meant nothing if people were starving or dying of disease. Southern Manchuria was a refugee zone, flooded with displaced Chinese and Koreans fleeing poverty, land shortages, and imperial warfare. The missionaries had to make a hard choice: stick to preaching salvation or address the immediate suffering around them. They chose engagement. By 1908, Robert Boyd could document a network of UPM schools, medical clinics, and relief centers stretching across key cities like Moukden (Shenyang), Newchwang (Yingkou), and Liaoyang23.

The medical and educational initiatives opened doors that pure evangelism never could, especially to women. In a Confucian society where women were systematically excluded from public life, UPM's female missionaries—often invisible in official mission records—ran schools and clinics that brought literacy, basic healthcare, and theological education directly to women and girls24. This was revolutionary stuff. These women began participating in house churches, leading prayer meetings, even teaching—completely upending both Christian gender roles and traditional Manchurian patriarchy.

Meanwhile, the political situation was becoming increasingly volatile. Southern Manchuria sat at the crossroads of Russian, Japanese, Korean, and Chinese imperial ambitions—a powder keg that would eventually explode into the creation of Manchukuo. The UPM tried to stay out of imperial politics, but their institutions inevitably became entangled with emerging nationalist movements. Korean refugees, many fleeing Japanese colonial rule, began appropriating Christian rhetoric for anti-imperial resistance, especially in the borderlands between Liaodong and northern Korea25. This created something remarkable: a borderland church that was simultaneously transcendent and intensely practical. On one hand, it mediated Western Presbyterian theology; on the other, it embodied a survival ethic for communities under extreme stress. By the late 1890s, the mission was transitioning from foreign control to indigenous leadership, with Korean and Chinese elders trained in UPM educational programs taking increasing responsibility for church governance. This represented a profound theological decentralization—what scholars call the shift from "mission-centered" to "church-centered" Christianity26.

The UPM's impact in Manchuria between 1872 and 1900 operated on two levels simultaneously. Internally, it fostered an indigenous Presbyterian church capable of theological, administrative, and liturgical self-governance. Externally, it created a social infrastructure—schools, clinics, literacy programs, famine relief—that made Christianity not just relevant but essential in a region torn apart by imperial competition and social dislocation. The emphasis on vernacular literacy, local leadership, and practical engagement with suffering established the foundation not only for Presbyterian expansion in Manchuria but for the transnational Christian networks that would flourish throughout Korea and the Korean diaspora in the decades ahead.

Presbyterian Church in Ireland (PCI)

The PCI mission began in 1869 when Irish missionaries, responding to William C. Burns' appeal, established what would become one of the first sustained Protestant missions in northeast Asia. The early years were marked by both ambitious evangelistic efforts and sobering realities. Missionaries like Rev. John Ross and Rev. John McIntyre employed conventional methods—open-air preaching, tract distribution, and engagement with merchants at commercial centers like Newchwang and Moukden—yet quickly encountered the harsh realities of life in Manchuria's challenging climate.

The deaths of Elizabeth Hunter and Mary Ross in the 1870s forced the Foreign Mission Board to confront fundamental questions about missionary sustainability. Rather than simply mourning these losses, the Board engaged in serious deliberation about whether improved medical preparation or relocation might better serve their mission goals. Their decision to maintain the Manchurian presence despite these risks reflected both strategic calculation and theological conviction about the region's importance27.

Ross's linguistic work represents perhaps the most significant long-term contribution of the Irish mission. His Korean New Testament translation, completed in 1887, demonstrated remarkable foresight about Manchuria's role as a cultural crossroads. However, this achievement generated considerable internal debate within the PCI. Critics questioned whether resources devoted to Korean translation diverted attention from the primary Chinese mission field. Ross's defense—that Manchuria's borderland character and substantial Korean migrant population required a transnational approach—proved prescient, as his translation became foundational for Korean Protestant Christianity28.

The expansion into educational and medical work during the 1880s and 1890s reflected broader trends in Protestant missions while generating specific tensions within the Irish context. The establishment of schools for both boys and girls challenged local cultural norms, particularly regarding female education. The emergence of Bible women as key figures in reaching secluded women demonstrated adaptive strategies for navigating Confucian social structures. Dr. Robert Greig's medical work illustrated how Western healing practices could create opportunities for religious dialogue, though it also raised questions about the relationship between social service and evangelism that would persist throughout the mission's history29.

Political upheavals, particularly during the Sino-French War (1884-85), exposed the vulnerabilities of foreign missionary presence. Anti-foreign sentiment in rural areas and occasional hostility from local elites forced missionaries to reconsider their dependence on British consular protection. The resulting debates revealed competing philosophies of mission: some advocated a "suffering servant" model emphasizing solidarity with persecuted local Christians, while others maintained that diplomatic protection was essential for the Gospel's survival. The compromise approach—maintaining formal protection while increasingly empowering local evangelists—reflected pragmatic adaptation to changing circumstances30.

Presbyterian Church in the United States of America (PCUSA)

The PCUSA entered Manchuria in the 1880s with different advantages and challenges than their Irish counterparts. Drawing on established experience in China's interior and Korea, and benefiting from growing American diplomatic influence in East Asia, PCUSA missionaries like Rev. A. E. Fulton and Dr. J. W. Lowe pursued an explicitly institutional approach. Their mission compounds—featuring chapels, schools, dispensaries, printing presses, and residences—embodied a comprehensive vision of Christian transformation that went well beyond simple evangelism31.

This institutional emphasis, however, generated significant internal debates about power, authority, and cultural adaptation. The question of Chinese leadership integration proved particularly contentious. While Presbyterian polity theoretically emphasized self-governing churches, American missionaries often struggled to translate this principle into practice. Concerns about education levels, doctrinal understanding, and political reliability—especially heightened after the Sino-Japanese War of 1895 intensified Chinese nationalism—created barriers to genuine indigenous leadership development. These hesitations reflected broader tensions between missionary ideals and colonial-era assumptions about cultural hierarchy.

Interdenominational cooperation presented another area of debate that revealed competing visions of missionary purpose. As ecumenical impulses gained strength in Protestant circles, the PCUSA faced pressure to coordinate with British and Canadian missions in educational and medical work. Supporters saw this as a path toward creating a unified Chinese Church that transcended denominational boundaries. Critics worried about doctrinal compromise and preferred maintaining distinct American Presbyterian identity32. The resulting patchwork of selective cooperation—joint facilities in some locations, denominational independence in others—reflected the difficulty of balancing theological conviction with practical collaboration.

The 1890s famine in northeast China provided both opportunity and challenge for American Presbyterian missions. Their substantial humanitarian response, including distribution of grain, clothing, and medicine, demonstrated the potential for missions to address immediate physical needs while pursuing spiritual goals. However, this dual approach sparked debates about the ethics of combining aid with evangelism. Critics both within and outside the mission accused missionaries of practicing "rice Christianity"—exploiting poverty for conversion purposes. Defenders argued that genuine compassion required attention to both material and spiritual needs. The PCUSA's eventual endorsement of this integrated approach reflected broader Protestant trends while acknowledging the moral complexities involved.

Russian Orthodox Church (ROC)

Between the 1870s and 1900, the Russian Orthodox Church (ROC) established itself as a significant institution within Russian imperial expansion into Manchuria, particularly alongside the development of the Chinese Eastern Railway (CER). While formal missionary activities targeting non-Russian populations remained limited during this period, the Church became integral to Russian imperial strategy, serving primarily as an institutional vehicle for Russian nationalism and colonial settlement rather than as an evangelical organization.

The Orthodox presence in the region originated with the Russian Ecclesiastical Mission (REM) in Beijing, which had been established in the 18th century but gained renewed importance in the late 19th century as Russia's strategic interests in the Far East intensified. This mission provided logistical and spiritual support for clergy deployed to the Russian Far East and subsequently to Manchuria. By the 1880s, Orthodox priests were regularly assigned to serve Russian consular personnel, military units, and railway workers in towns along the Russian frontier33. Although their evangelical efforts among Han Chinese and Manchu populations were minimal and generally discouraged by Qing authorities, the Orthodox Church's institutional presence became a fundamental component of Russian frontier identity.

The construction of the Chinese Eastern Railway in the 1890s significantly expanded the ROC's institutional footprint. As thousands of Russian engineers, soldiers, and settlers relocated to the region under CER administration, the ROC established chapels, schools, and clerical residences in key locations including Harbin, Hailar, and Pogranichny34. The Church's primary function remained pastoral, addressing the spiritual and moral needs of Russian expatriates. However, this pastoral role carried broader symbolic significance: the establishment of Orthodox structures—particularly the planning and subsequent construction of St. Nicholas Cathedral in Harbin—served as formal assertions of Russian presence and authority.

In Harbin, prior to the cathedral's completion in the early 20th century, the ROC participated in shaping the emerging city as a distinctly Russian cultural space. Urban planning positioned Orthodox structures in prominent, elevated locations that dominated the visual landscape. Religious festivals and public processions functioned as expressions of both faith and political loyalty to the Russian Empire. The Church also operated religious schools that provided instruction in Russian language and Orthodox doctrine, contributing to the development of a settler population aligned with imperial objectives.

This integration of religious and national identity was reinforced through the ROC's involvement in imperial ceremony and diplomatic functions. Orthodox clergy frequently served in quasi-consular capacities, mediating disputes, conducting public ceremonies, and reinforcing concepts of divinely sanctioned Russian authority. Contemporary sources describe the ROC's presence in Manchuria as symbolizing "the fulfillment of Russia's sacred calling to bring order and morality to the East"35. This perspective reflected broader imperial discourse that positioned Russia as a spiritual authority in Asia, distinct from both Western modernity and what Russian observers characterized as Asian religious traditions.

Despite its central role in Russian settler culture and political identity in Manchuria, the ROC achieved limited engagement with local Chinese populations during this period. This restricted evangelical outreach resulted from both theological practices and political considerations. Unlike many Western Protestant missions, the ROC emphasized sacramental participation and clerical hierarchy over text-based evangelism. Additionally, Qing authorities maintained suspicion toward Russian political and religious institutions, further limiting outreach beyond Russian communities36. Nevertheless, Orthodox symbolism—expressed through architecture, ritual, and language—established the ROC as a significant cultural presence and essential element of Russian imperial infrastructure.

The Russian Orthodox Church in Manchuria during the final decades of the 19th century functioned primarily as a religious-political institution that established Russian national identity within colonial territory. Through the sanctification of Russian settlements, performance of imperial rituals, and mediation of cultural boundaries, the ROC became a visible and lasting manifestation of Russian imperial presence in northeast Asia.

Christian or Pro-Christian Events/Movements

Apart from the founding of institutions, there were few major movements or events during this period. For both missions, it was a period of slow growth and foundation building.

Key Theological Issues

Behind the overriding question of how to missionize this vast, newly-opened territory most quickly and effectively lay a deeper problem of theological and practical correctness. By most accounts, the most pressing issue was not Chinese beliefs, which by all accounts were expected to fall away sooner or later, but rather the pernicious influence of other Christian missions who could potentially lead Chinese converts to error. Expressed in the generally cool relations between the Catholic and Protestant missions, this division was considered by both missions to be of highest long-term importance.

The rivalry between the French Catholics and Scottish and Irish Presbyterians included issues of geopolitical and national pride, but was also the difference between two distinct philosophies of mission. Concerning the former, both French and British political legations in Beijing did at times attempt to claim the mantle of speaking for the interests of all Christian missions, regardless of nationality. The latter was a thornier problem. Missionaries often spoke of their rivals in terms as “heretics” and took particular pride in claiming converts from each other. Relations were not always hostile: Catholic and Protestant clergy would occasionally visit each other on festive occasions such as the opening of a new school or departure of an old missionary returning to Europe, but hostility was sometimes expressed openly, as when a Protestant missionary penned a newspaper article attacking the moral character of the French bishop, Verrolles.

The most significant differences were theological. Specific to mission field, the two had differing perspectives on the nature of (true) conversion, and the role of the missionary clergy. Simply put, Catholic practice aimed to create coherent and distinct villages of coreligionists, led by clergy, with institutions that would raise children in the faith and crucially provide the sacraments. (Of note: Catholic baptism of the dying, notably children, was a high sacramental priority that I doubt was viewed favorably by the Protestants.) Although the indigenous clergy had grown in number and more than proven themselves in the face of persecution and the hardship of ministering to villages separated by extremely long distances, there was as yet no serious discussion of creating a Chinese Church that was free of European leadership. The model of the Catholic mission was very much the Church Universal, highly structured, hierarchical, and centrally-led.

In contrast, the Protestant missions idealized the model of Pauline conversion, the individual, heartfelt calling by the Holy Spirit epitomized in model converts like Wang Jingming (Old Wang). Mission itself consisted of preaching on street corners and in humble churches, while the provision of services such as dispensaries and hospitals was a social gospel philosophy of winning converts through moral example. While the Catholics kept tight control over who amongst their ranks could minister and where, the Protestants prized the independence of the converts to preach in whatever way they saw fit, taking the lack of sophistication not as error but as evidence of heartfelt sincerity. This ideal is seen in the life stories of Protestant missionaries such as William Burns, who are portrayed (in positive terms) as so burning with the Holy Spirit that they started preaching right off the boat, regardless of their ability with the Chinese language. The elevation of sincerity (over doctrinal sophistication) as a primary virtue is replicated in the effusive praise given to converts like Old Wang.

The difference between the two mission strategies became clear as the Boxer violence loomed. The Protestant missionaries largely sought refuge in the port city of Niuzhuang, leaving the Chinese Christians to face what they understood to be a test of faith (often portrayed as divinely sent.). In contrast, the Catholic priests made a point of remaining with their churches, both congregation and building, which in some cases also provided physical defense to the community. In subsequent accounts, each made a point of emphasizing the lack of faith and willingness to apostatize by converts on the other side.

This rift would remain throughout the next few decades and would signify the very different vision that each mission had for Christianity in China.

Anti-Christian Leaders

For reasons discussed in 6. below, I can find no named leaders that emerge as enemies of Christianity.

Rather, the groups that appear as most likely enemies are social types. These include conservative townspeople and villagers, officials who are either personally set against Christianity/foreigners or simply want to remove those who would create the conditions for violence. Protestant sources note that Manchu elites in northern cities like Qiqihar and Jilin tended to be more reflexively anti-Christian. Those with a direct conflict include doctors who were in competition from the mission dispensaries and local educated elites (such as sishu teachers), who proclaimed that the Christians were here to replace Confucius.

Zailijiao, a religious teaching that erupted into violence in 1892, is credited with an inchoate hatred of Christians and foreigners, but it is not at all clear how this was related to their teaching, whether it represented a variation of apocalyptic violence, or if it was simply a precursor of Boxer attitudes.

Each of these types of anti-Christian groups had a common element: they were disorganized and leaderless.

Anti-Christian Organizations

There was little organized resistance to Christianity at this time, the key word here being organized. Rather, resistance to Christianity came from a variety of sources, nearly ubiquitous but generally small in scale. Missionary sources mention a handful of political officials as being particularly and often violently anti-Christian.

The most notable case of official persecution was in the northern town of Hulan, where missionary Louis Conraux was captured and tortured by local officials during the 1880s. (I believe this was in connection to the Zaili revolt). This area would later (post-Boxer) become a Christian stronghold. In another earlier case from 1888 the Catholic orphanage near Haicheng was attacked, the two missionaries driven out “with rifles and lances” and 25 of the 53 children killed.

However, while some such officials were indeed motivated by dislike of foreigners of Christians, others had no particular thought beyond preventing the outbreak of violence against the foreigners who after 1871 were increasingly willing to take up matters with the foreign legations. Facing fears of a Russian invasion, one such official in “San-sing” (in “Amur” province, assume they mean Heilongjiang) imprisoned local Christians to convince them to recant their faith. When that was unsuccessful, he ordered them to leave, even offering to recompense them for lost lands. When the Christians replied that they would leave only in response to an official decree, the official wrote one and ordered soldiers to banish any Christian from the territory, and to punish anyone caught harboring or selling land to a Christian. (Guillon 1891).

Of particular note is that clerical (Buddhist and Daoist) religions did not seem to be particularly anti-Christian, at least not consistently or as a matter of principle. Protestant sources report that Muslims tended to be more friendly to the missionaries, for example being willing to engage in commerce when Han Chinese would not.

Anti-Christian Events/Movements

The main event that could be characterized as anti-Christian was the Boxer Uprising, which will be discussed in the subsequent decade.

Before that event there were tremors of lesser size and magnitude that in retrospect displayed elements of the violence that was yet to come.

Most anti-Christian sentiment was expressed at the small scale, either small acts of non-violent hostility to the missionaries, or individuated acts of violence against converts within families or communities. Missionary accounts treat the former as a daily occurrence attributed to hatred of foreigners and/or their religion. They included such acts as refusing to sell or rent land to foreigners, harassing foreigners while preaching or while going about more mundane affairs, or spreading rumors about evil intentions/magic. Often those with the most vocal opinions also had a direct conflict of interest, e.g., Confucian students or doctors of Chinese medicine. On a few occasions threats did erupt into violence against missionaries, such as in 1885 when a missionary doctor was surrounded and captured by soldiers who suspected him of murdering children for their eyes and hearts (for use in making medicine). (Boyd 59)

Individuated violence towards converts was frequent. The common pattern was that a Christian individual or family was targeted by neighbors or family members with a demand to recant. Often the hostility was due to a perceived threat to collective security or practice, e.g., refusal to participate in family sacrifices or contribute to the maintenance of a village temple, or else the fear that harboring a Christian in their midst would bring misfortune or divine punishment. In missionary accounts, attention is naturally given to those who refused to apostatize and were tortured, exiled or killed. In their depictions of violence against converts, both Catholic and Protestant accounts clearly mimic the images of early Roman martyrdom.

The following account is typical:

"a Christian, head of household, and a man of wealth and influence, had apostatized publicly five years ago, to the great sorrow of his parents and all of the Christians. To worsen his scandal, this unhappy man hosted a great meal to which all the surrounding pagans were invited. He exposed, in front of them, the plaques of the ancestors all spread out, along with the images of the false gods and all sort of Chinese superstitions; he commanded the family to honor these plaques and images, and they entreated him adore only the true God from now on. Prayers and supplications were useless and could not bring back the man back to his senses. His son, seven years old, taught secretly by his Christian mother and other relatives, refused to burn incense to the ancestors on New Year's Day, and nearly wore the crown of martyrdom, with his own father as the torturer. In sorrow, the neighbors had to tear the man off of his son, his hands bloodied, saying that he wanted to offer his son in sacrifice.” (Annual bishop report, Guillon, 1892)

Spikes in violence were prompted by a few sources. Rumors about the nefarious intentions of foreign medicine (both Catholic and Protestant missions maintained dispensaries) were common in Shenyang and on a few occasions nearly broke into violence. External events such as the Tianjin massacre of 1871 and rumors about an immanent invasion of China by Russia during the 1880s prompted a general air of suspicion and rumors of retaliation in which all of the foreigners would be slaughtered. Such claims (often expressed as a threat directly to the missionaries) rose and fell in conjunction with other rumors about political intrigue.

The first bout of organized violence was the Zaili revolt of 1892. This revolt was a precursor to the Boxers in that it was not a focused and clearly-led rebellion but rather a loosely coordinated snowball of localized violence that turned partly against the missions and Chinese Christians with same sorts of claims of ridding China of the foreigners and their malevolent magic that would later characterize the Boxers.

The letter of Guillon (1892), then VA of Manchuria, describes the Zaili massacre of Christians in Jilin:

“an indigenous priest, P. Lin, and hundreds of neophytes had their throats cut with distinction neither of age nor of sex. … The plot hatched by Tsai-Li-Ti, a sect dominating in Mongolia and Manchuria, had been directed against the Christians in the area of the massacres, but it had burst earlier than its authors would have liked. It was the suddenness of the blow which saved us.” (Annual bishop report, Guillon 1892)

Anti-Missionary and Local Opposition

Following the Treaties of Tientsin (1858) and Beijing (1860), Christian missionary activity expanded into Manchuria, establishing a presence that would generate sustained local opposition. While the region did not become a primary site of organized anti-Christian violence during the 1870s and 1880s, it nonetheless experienced mounting tensions characterized by land disputes, contestation over foreign privileges, and growing anti-foreign sentiment. Rather than manifesting as coordinated uprisings, anti-missionary resistance in Manchuria took more diffuse forms—rumors, social ostracism, and localized opposition—that nevertheless significantly altered the region's religious and political dynamics.

The legal protections afforded to missionaries under the new treaty framework enabled foreign missions to acquire property and establish institutions throughout Liaodong and southern Jilin. Mission stations constructed schools, churches, and orphanages, creating visible foreign presences within communities organized around Confucian social hierarchies and ancestral practices. These developments generated friction with established religious customs, particularly the ancestral rites and lineage-based observances that structured rural Manchurian society. Local populations often viewed missionaries as disruptive forces within existing spiritual and kinship networks, while converts—frequently drawn from economically marginal households—faced accusations of abandoning ancestral obligations and community solidarity37.

Anti-Christian discourse proliferated throughout Manchuria during the 1880s, drawing from publications such as the Bixie Jishi (辟邪纪实, A Record of Facts to Ward off Heterodoxy), first published in 186138. Although originating in central China, condensed versions of this and similar texts reached Manchuria through commercial printing networks and itinerant performers. These materials characterized missionaries as sexual predators and child murderers, employing the term "pig-goat devils" (猪羊鬼子)—a linguistic construction that played on the phonetic similarity between "foreign" (洋) and "goat" (羊). Catholic doctrine was similarly ridiculed through the transformation of "Lord of Heaven" (天主) into "heavenly pig" (天猪)39.

Such characterizations found receptive audiences in Manchuria due to existing cosmological frameworks regarding ghosts (鬼) and outsiders. As Thoralf Klein has observed, the designation yang guizi (洋鬼子, foreign devils) did not invoke Western conceptions of Satanic evil, but rather referred to spirits or entities disconnected from ancestral care—outsiders rendered dangerous through their lack of ritual integration within family and community structures40. Missionaries, as representatives of foreign religious traditions, could readily be positioned within this conceptual framework. Local narratives attributed various malevolent activities to missionary presence, including grave desecration, child abduction, and well poisoning—accusations that merged Chinese demonological traditions with anxieties about foreign encroachment.

These tensions extended beyond rhetorical opposition to encompass organized resistance activities. Village communities frequently submitted petitions to local magistrates protesting church construction or alleging Christian violations of social protocols. Confucian scholars and local gentry often spearheaded such efforts, positioning themselves as guardians of orthodox practice and community autonomy. In various locations, Christian converts faced public humiliation, while mission schools encountered community hostility sufficient to force relocations41.

Manchuria's regional significance intensified these concerns. As the ancestral homeland of the Qing dynasty and a strategic buffer against Russian expansion, the region carried particular military and political importance. The presence of Russian Orthodox missions in the Amur region and Vladivostok contributed to suspicions that all missionary activities—whether Catholic, Protestant, or Orthodox—served imperial intelligence functions42. As railroad surveys and mining concessions extended into northeast China, missionary expansion appeared to presage broader foreign territorial ambitions.

By the late 1880s, Manchuria had developed into a zone of sustained, if largely non-violent, anti-missionary opposition, where circulating narratives about demonic foreigners, gentry-led mobilization, and official ambivalence toward treaty enforcement intersected. Although the region avoided the large-scale violence that occurred elsewhere during the 1890s, its anti-Christian agitation contributed to the broader atmosphere of suspicion and resentment that would later culminate in the Boxer Uprising. In this context, Manchuria had already been conditioned for conflict through ideological preparation via texts like the Bixie Jishi, spiritual framing through ghost-devil discourse, and political positioning as an imperial frontier zone.
